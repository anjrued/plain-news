Federal government blocks cruise passengers from leaving hantavirus quarantine

5:00 PM ET

Two passengers from a cruise ship that experienced a hantavirus outbreak are being prevented from leaving a federal quarantine facility, with at least one passenger saying she is being detained against her will.

The federal order marks an unusual use of quarantine powers. Hantavirus, a rare but serious illness typically transmitted through contact with rodent droppings or urine, has an incubation period of up to six weeks. The virus can cause hantavirus pulmonary syndrome, which has a fatality rate of roughly 36 percent according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

One detained passenger told reporters she feels betrayed by the government's decision to block her departure from the quarantine unit. The legal basis for the detention and how many total passengers remain in quarantine have not been publicly disclosed.

Unlike highly contagious airborne diseases, hantavirus does not spread from person to person under normal circumstances. This has raised questions about the proportionality of the quarantine measures. Public health experts note that federal quarantine authority is rarely invoked and typically reserved for diseases that pose immediate transmission risks to the broader population.

The cruise ship outbreak itself represents an uncommon occurrence. Hantavirus cases in the United States typically occur in rural areas where people encounter rodent infestations in cabins, sheds, or other structures. How the virus came to be present on a cruise vessel remains unclear.

Federal quarantine powers are authorized under the Public Health Service Act and enforced by the CDC. The agency can detain, medically examine, or conditionally release individuals reasonably believed to be carrying certain communicable diseases. However, the authority is constrained by due process requirements and medical necessity standards.

The current situation has sparked debate about where public health authority ends and individual liberty begins. Civil liberties advocates argue that quarantine measures must be narrowly tailored to actual transmission risks. Public health officials counter that the severity of hantavirus, combined with uncertainty about exposure levels on the ship, justifies a cautious approach.

Passengers who feel they are being unlawfully detained have the right to petition for habeas corpus relief in federal court. Whether the detained passengers will pursue legal challenges remains unknown.

The quarantine period could extend for several more weeks based on the virus's incubation timeline. Federal officials have not indicated whether passengers will be released early with monitoring provisions or required to remain in the facility for the full observation period.

The incident highlights ongoing tensions over government emergency health powers, particularly following broader debates about public health authority during the COVID-19 pandemic.
