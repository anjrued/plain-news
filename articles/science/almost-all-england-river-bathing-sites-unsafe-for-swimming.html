Almost all England river bathing sites unsafe for swimming

5:00 PM ET

Warning signs are posted at 12 of England's 14 officially designated river bathing sites, telling people not to swim due to unsafe water conditions. Tests have found excessive levels of bacteria linked to fecal contamination at nearly every location.

The Environmental Agency monitors these sites regularly during bathing season, testing for bacteria that indicate sewage or agricultural runoff. When bacteria levels exceed safety thresholds, signs go up advising against swimming. The current situation represents a near-total failure of England's designated river bathing program.

Only two of the 14 sites currently meet safety standards for public swimming. The affected locations span across England, suggesting this is a systemic problem rather than isolated incidents.

The contamination comes from multiple sources. Sewage overflows from water company infrastructure dump untreated wastewater directly into rivers during heavy rainfall. Agricultural runoff carries animal waste from fields into waterways. Both create conditions where bacteria harmful to human health can thrive.

The timing is significant because these sites were specifically designated as official bathing locations, meaning they should meet higher standards than other waterways. The designation was meant to improve public access to wild swimming spots while ensuring safety through regular monitoring and water quality improvements.

Swimming in contaminated water can cause stomach illnesses, skin infections, and respiratory problems. Particularly vulnerable are children, elderly people, and anyone with weakened immune systems. The bacteria detected in testing indicates the presence of human or animal waste, which can carry pathogens including E. coli and other harmful microorganisms.

The announcement comes as more locations are being added to England's bathing site program. Advocates for wild swimming have pushed for years to expand official bathing designations, arguing that monitoring and regulation would drive improvements in water quality. The current state of existing sites raises questions about whether expansion makes sense before addressing fundamental contamination problems.

Water companies face mounting criticism over their failure to invest adequately in infrastructure while paying substantial dividends to shareholders. Agricultural practices that allow runoff into waterways also face scrutiny, though regulation of farm pollution has been limited.

The government's Environment Agency is responsible for monitoring and enforcement, but environmental groups argue that years of budget cuts have weakened its ability to hold polluters accountable. Fines for pollution incidents often amount to small fractions of company revenues.

For now, anyone hoping to swim in England's rivers at designated sites faces a stark reality: the water at almost every official location is too contaminated to be safe.
