Almost all England river bathing sites unsafe for swimming

6:00 PM ET

Signs warning people not to swim are now posted at 12 of England's 14 designated river bathing sites after water quality tests found unsafe bacteria levels linked to fecal contamination.

The warnings affect nearly all official inland river swimming locations across the country, raising concerns about water safety as summer weather drives more people to seek relief in natural water bodies. Only two sites currently meet safety standards for swimming.

The Environment Agency tests water at designated bathing sites throughout the swimming season, measuring bacteria levels that indicate fecal contamination from sewage, agricultural runoff, or other sources. When bacterial counts exceed safety thresholds, authorities post warnings advising against swimming due to health risks including stomach illness and infections.

The widespread contamination comes as England has been expanding its official bathing water designation program. The government recently announced additional river locations will receive formal bathing site status, which requires regular monitoring and public reporting of water quality. However, the current results show that designation alone does not guarantee safe swimming conditions.

River water quality has become a significant public concern in England, with sewage overflows from water companies contributing to pollution alongside agricultural runoff and urban drainage. Water companies have faced mounting criticism for discharging untreated sewage into rivers and coastal waters, particularly during heavy rainfall when combined sewer systems become overwhelmed.

The bacteria measured in these tests typically originate from human or animal waste. High levels can cause a range of illnesses in swimmers, from minor stomach upset to more serious infections affecting the ears, eyes, respiratory system, or skin. Children, elderly people, and those with compromised immune systems face elevated risks.

Designated bathing sites receive more intensive monitoring than other swimming locations, meaning many popular informal swimming spots across England likely have unknown water quality. The testing regime at official sites provides at least some data for swimmers to make informed decisions, though the current results offer little reassurance.

Environmental groups have long advocated for stronger regulation of pollution sources and increased investment in water infrastructure. The visible failure of most designated sites to meet safety standards adds urgency to calls for action on river water quality.

For now, swimmers face a stark choice at most of England's monitored river bathing locations: ignore the warnings and risk illness, or skip the swim entirely. The situation highlights a broader challenge facing the country's waterways, where decades of underinvestment and inadequate pollution controls have degraded what should be public resources available for recreation and wildlife alike.
