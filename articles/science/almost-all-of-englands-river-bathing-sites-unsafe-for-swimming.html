Almost all of England's river bathing sites unsafe for swimming

6:00 PM ET

Signs warning people not to swim are posted at 12 of England's 14 designated river bathing sites due to elevated bacteria levels linked to fecal contamination. The widespread pollution affects newly designated locations that were meant to provide safe inland swimming alternatives for the public.

The Environment Agency tests water quality at official bathing sites and posts warning signs when bacteria counts exceed safety thresholds. High levels of bacteria associated with human and animal waste can cause illness in swimmers, including stomach bugs, skin infections, and respiratory problems.

The contamination comes as England expands its network of designated bathing sites beyond traditional coastal locations. River bathing has grown in popularity, particularly since the pandemic, but water quality monitoring has revealed serious pollution problems at inland sites.

Only two of the 14 designated river bathing locations currently meet standards safe enough to swim without warning signs. The high failure rate raises questions about whether rivers should have been designated as bathing sites before pollution problems were addressed.

Sewage overflow from treatment plants and agricultural runoff are the primary sources of fecal bacteria in English rivers. Water companies have faced mounting criticism for dumping untreated sewage into waterways, particularly during heavy rainfall when systems become overwhelmed.

The government announced additional river bathing site designations even as existing locations remain unsafe. Designation requires the Environment Agency to monitor water quality regularly during bathing season, from May to September, but does not guarantee the water is clean enough to swim in.

Environmental groups argue the widespread warnings demonstrate systemic failure to protect river water quality. They point to decades of underinvestment in sewage infrastructure and weak enforcement of agricultural pollution regulations.

The bathing site warnings create a public health dilemma. People swim in rivers regardless of official designation, but may be more likely to swim at designated sites assuming the water is safe. Warning signs provide crucial information, but their near-universal presence suggests the designation system is identifying problems faster than solutions are being implemented.

Local communities have campaigned for years to win bathing site status for popular swimming spots, believing official recognition would force improvements in water quality. The current situation suggests designation alone is insufficient without enforcement action against polluters and infrastructure investment.

The Environment Agency continues to test water weekly at designated sites throughout the swimming season. Bacteria levels can fluctuate based on rainfall, temperature, and pollution events, so some sites may become safe to swim at certain times even if currently posted with warnings.
