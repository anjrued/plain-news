Iran claims control over larger section of Strait of Hormuz

6:00 PM ET

Iran has published a map asserting its armed forces have oversight across more than 22,000 square kilometers of the Strait of Hormuz, escalating its territorial claims over the vital shipping channel.

The strait is one of the world's most strategically important waterways. Roughly one fifth of global oil supplies pass through the narrow passage between Iran and Oman, connecting the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea beyond.

The new Iranian map shows an expanded area of claimed military jurisdiction within the strait. Iran has not specified what operational changes this claim might bring, but the announcement signals increased assertiveness over waters that serve as a critical chokepoint for international energy markets.

The strait is only 21 miles wide at its narrowest point. International shipping lanes pass through Omani territorial waters on one side and Iranian waters on the other. Iran's Revolutionary Guard Navy has previously detained or harassed vessels transiting the area, and Tehran has periodically threatened to close the strait during periods of tension with Western powers.

The timing of Iran's announcement comes amid broader regional uncertainty. Relations between Iran and the United States remain strained, with disagreements over Iran's nuclear program and its support for armed groups across the Middle East. Any disruption to shipping through the strait would have immediate effects on global oil prices and supply chains.

Oman, which controls the southern shore of the strait, has not publicly responded to Iran's map. The country has traditionally maintained neutral relations with both Iran and Western powers, positioning itself as a potential mediator in regional disputes.

The United States Navy's Fifth Fleet, based in Bahrain, regularly patrols Gulf waters and has previously stated its commitment to ensuring freedom of navigation through international waterways. The US and allied nations have conducted joint naval exercises in the region, citing the need to keep shipping lanes open.

International maritime law allows nations to claim territorial waters extending 12 nautical miles from their coastlines, with additional economic zones beyond that. The strait's geography means that no international waters exist within it, but the principle of innocent passage allows ships to transit through territorial seas for continuous and expeditious travel.

Iran has not announced new restrictions on shipping or military activities to accompany its map. However, the public assertion of expanded oversight adds another element of uncertainty to a waterway where tensions have periodically flared into confrontation.
