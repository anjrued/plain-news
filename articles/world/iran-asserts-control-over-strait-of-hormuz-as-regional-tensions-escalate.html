Iran asserts control over Strait of Hormuz as regional tensions escalate

5:00 PM ET

Iran has published a map claiming its armed forces maintain oversight across more than 22,000 square kilometers of the Strait of Hormuz, a narrow waterway through which roughly one-fifth of global oil supplies pass daily.

The strait, at its narrowest point just 21 miles wide, separates Iran from the Arabian Peninsula and connects the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea. It serves as the sole sea route from the Persian Gulf to the open ocean.

Iran's formal assertion of control over such a large area of the strait represents a significant statement about its willingness to regulate or potentially restrict traffic through the waterway. The country has previously threatened to close the strait during periods of heightened tension with Western nations, particularly over its nuclear program and economic sanctions.

Any sustained disruption to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz would have immediate global consequences. The waterway handles approximately 21 million barrels of oil per day, along with substantial volumes of liquefied natural gas. Major energy importers including China, Japan, India, South Korea, and European nations rely on this route.

The Iranian government has not specified what practical changes its claimed oversight might entail or whether it plans new restrictions on commercial or military vessels transiting the strait. International maritime law generally recognizes the right of innocent passage through straits used for international navigation, even when they pass through territorial waters.

The timing of Iran's announcement comes amid ongoing regional instability. Relations between Iran and Western nations remain strained over its nuclear enrichment activities, its support for armed groups across the Middle East, and its deepening military cooperation with Russia.

The United States maintains a naval presence in the region through its Fifth Fleet, headquartered in Bahrain. American and allied warships regularly transit the strait, sometimes leading to tense encounters with Iranian vessels.

Several Gulf Arab states, including Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates, have invested in pipeline infrastructure designed to bypass the Strait of Hormuz entirely, allowing some oil exports to reach ports on the Gulf of Oman or the Red Sea. However, the volume these pipelines can handle represents only a fraction of total regional exports.

Previous Iranian threats to close the strait have typically prompted sharp responses from Washington and its allies, who have maintained that they would not tolerate such action. The international community has long considered free navigation through the strait essential to global energy security and economic stability.

Neither the United States nor other major powers have yet issued formal responses to Iran's latest territorial claim over the waterway.
