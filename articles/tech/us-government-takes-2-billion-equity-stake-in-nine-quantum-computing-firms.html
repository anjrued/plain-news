US government takes $2 billion equity stake in nine quantum computing firms

6:00 PM ET

The United States government has invested $2 billion in exchange for equity positions in nine quantum computing companies, marking a significant federal intervention in the emerging technology sector. The investment represents a direct ownership stake rather than traditional grant funding, giving taxpayers a potential financial return if these companies succeed.

Quantum computers use the principles of quantum mechanics to perform certain calculations exponentially faster than conventional computers. The technology remains largely experimental but promises breakthroughs in drug discovery, cryptography, materials science, and artificial intelligence. China and other nations have poured billions into quantum research, making the field a focal point of technological competition.

The beneficiaries include at least one startup backed by an investment firm with connections to the Trump family, raising questions about the selection process. The administration has not yet disclosed the complete list of recipients or the criteria used to determine which companies received funding. Government officials have described quantum computing as critical to national security and economic competitiveness.

Equity investments differ from the typical government approach of issuing research grants or contracts. By taking ownership stakes, the federal government shares both the risk of failure and potential rewards of success. This model has precedents in other sectors but remains relatively uncommon in technology policy.

The $2 billion figure places this initiative among the larger technology investments in recent federal budgets. For comparison, the CHIPS Act allocated $52 billion for semiconductor manufacturing, though much of that funding takes the form of subsidies rather than equity positions.

Quantum computing companies have struggled to attract sufficient private investment despite the technology's promise. Building quantum computers requires expensive specialized equipment, and commercial applications remain years away. The technical challenges are formidable. Quantum systems are extremely fragile and require near absolute zero temperatures to function. Error rates remain high, and scaling from today's experimental processors to commercially viable machines has proven difficult.

Several of the funded companies are racing to achieve quantum advantage, the point at which quantum computers can solve practical problems faster than classical supercomputers. Google claimed to reach this milestone in 2019, though competitors disputed the significance of the calculation performed.

The equity arrangement means American taxpayers now own portions of these private companies. If any achieve commercial success, the government could sell its shares at a profit. If the companies fail, the investment will be lost. The administration has not indicated whether it will seek board representation or attempt to influence company decisions.

Critics may question whether direct government ownership in specific companies creates conflicts of interest or picks winners and losers inappropriately. Supporters argue the strategic importance of quantum technology justifies federal involvement to prevent other nations from dominating the field.
