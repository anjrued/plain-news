Trump administration blocked Americans exposed to Ebola from returning home, sent them to Europe instead

6:00 PM ET

The Trump administration prevented Americans exposed to Ebola from returning to the United States, instead redirecting them to Berlin and Prague, according to new reports. The decision affects individuals who had contact with an Ebola patient but were not themselves infected.

Administration officials denied refusing entry to the Americans when questioned, but would not explain why the individuals were sent to European cities rather than allowed to return home. The move marks an unusual departure from standard protocols for managing potential disease exposure among US citizens abroad.

Ebola is a severe viral disease that spreads through direct contact with bodily fluids of infected individuals. The current case involves exposure rather than active infection, meaning the individuals were in contact with someone who had Ebola but have not tested positive themselves. Standard medical practice involves monitoring exposed individuals for symptoms during the virus's incubation period, which can last up to 21 days.

The decision to divert Americans to third countries raises questions about both the legal authority to prevent citizens from returning home and the diplomatic arrangements made with Germany and the Czech Republic to receive them. Neither the State Department nor health agencies have clarified whether Berlin and Prague were consulted before the Americans arrived, or what medical facilities are providing monitoring and care.

Public health experts typically recommend that exposed individuals be monitored in isolation while allowing them to remain in familiar surroundings when possible, as this reduces stress and improves compliance with monitoring protocols. Sending potentially exposed people across multiple international borders also creates additional contact points and complicates tracking if symptoms do develop.

The administration's reluctance to provide clear answers about the decision-making process has drawn criticism from former health officials and diplomats. Questions remain about what specific authority was invoked to prevent the Americans from returning, whether this represents a new policy approach to disease exposure, and how long the individuals will remain in Europe.

This incident occurs amid broader debates about executive authority over travel restrictions and public health measures. The lack of transparency around the decision contrasts with typical protocols for managing infectious disease exposure, which generally involve clear communication about risk assessment and protective measures.

The affected Americans' current medical status and expected return timeline have not been disclosed. Neither the German nor Czech governments have publicly commented on hosting Americans for Ebola exposure monitoring.
