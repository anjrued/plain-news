Today, 7:00 PM ET

Iran has published a map claiming its armed forces oversee more than 22,000 square kilometers of the Strait of Hormuz, escalating territorial assertions over one of the world's most critical oil shipping routes.

The strait is a narrow waterway between Iran and Oman through which roughly one-fifth of global oil supply passes daily. Any disruption to shipping through the passage would have immediate effects on energy markets and global trade.

The Iranian government's claim of expanded oversight comes amid ongoing tensions in the Middle East. The strait is just 21 miles wide at its narrowest point, with shipping lanes that pass through Iranian and Omani territorial waters.

Iran has periodically threatened to close or disrupt traffic through the strait during periods of heightened conflict with Western nations. The country's naval forces have conducted military exercises in the area and have previously detained foreign vessels.

The United States and other Western powers maintain a naval presence in the region specifically to ensure freedom of navigation through the strait. The U.S. Fifth Fleet is based in Bahrain, across the Persian Gulf from Iran.

Neither the United States nor other Gulf nations have formally responded to Iran's published map. International maritime law governs passage through straits used for international navigation, but enforcement and interpretation of these rules remain contested in disputed waters.

The timing of Iran's announcement is significant as regional tensions remain elevated. The country has been involved in proxy conflicts across the Middle East and faces ongoing economic pressure from international sanctions.

Shipping insurance rates for vessels transiting the strait often fluctuate based on perceived threat levels from Iranian military activity. Any sustained increase in Iranian naval operations in the area could affect global oil prices and shipping costs.

The strait connects the Persian Gulf with the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea. Besides oil, significant volumes of liquefied natural gas pass through the waterway, making it essential infrastructure for global energy security.

Today, 7:00 PM ET

A massive tsunami that struck a remote fjord in Alaska in 2023 was the second tallest wave ever recorded, new research confirms. The wave reached 650 feet high, caused by a mountainside collapse triggered by melting glacier ice.

The event occurred in Dickson Fjord in September 2023. Scientists determined that roughly 25 million cubic meters of rock fell from a steep slope into the narrow waterway below. The resulting wave was taller than any skyscraper and left visible scars on the surrounding landscape.

Researchers linked the collapse directly to glacier retreat. The glacier that once supported the base of the mountain had been melting for years, destabilizing the rock face above. When the support finally gave way, the entire mountainside fell into the fjord.

The wave initially reached its peak height of 650 feet at the point of impact. It then traveled back and forth across the fjord for several days, gradually losing energy. Even days later, waves several meters high continued to slosh within the confined space.

No one was killed because the fjord is uninhabited and rarely visited. However, the event destroyed monitoring equipment and reshaped the local coastline. Scientists only learned of the full scale after analyzing seismic data and satellite imagery.

The tallest tsunami ever recorded was also in Alaska. In 1958, an earthquake triggered a landslide in Lituya Bay that created a wave reaching 1,720 feet. That event killed five people who were on boats in the bay.

The new findings raise concerns about similar risks in other regions where glaciers are retreating rapidly. As ice melts, mountains previously held stable can become vulnerable to collapse. Fjords in Alaska, Norway, Greenland, and New Zealand may face similar hazards.

Climate change is accelerating glacier melt worldwide. Temperatures in Alaska have risen faster than the global average, with glaciers losing mass at increasing rates. This creates conditions where rock faces lose their support and steep slopes become unstable.

Scientists are now working to identify other locations where retreating glaciers might trigger landslides into water. Early warning systems and monitoring could help protect populated areas near vulnerable coastlines.

Climate change intensifying hurricanes and typhoons

Tropical storms are becoming more powerful as ocean temperatures rise, though the total number of storms may not be increasing.

7:00 PM ET

Hurricanes and typhoons form over warm ocean water when conditions allow thunderstorms to organize into a rotating system. They require sea surface temperatures above 27 degrees Celsius, low wind shear, and sufficient distance from the equator for the Coriolis effect to create rotation.

Research shows these storms are becoming more intense as the climate warms. Warmer oceans provide more energy, leading to stronger winds and heavier rainfall. While scientists have not found a clear increase in the total number of storms, the proportion of major hurricanes has grown. Storms are also intensifying more rapidly and moving more slowly, both of which increase damage when they make landfall.
