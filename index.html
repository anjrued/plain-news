1:52 AM ET

A fossil site in North Dakota appears to have captured the day the asteroid struck Earth and ended the age of dinosaurs. The site contains fish that died within hours of the impact, with tiny glass beads from the collision lodged in their gills.

The beads represent material ejected into the atmosphere when the asteroid hit Earth approximately 66 million years ago. As this debris rained back down, it was inhaled by fish still alive in the immediate aftermath. The timing suggests these animals died within hours of the impact event.

The discovery provides direct physical evidence linking the moment of impact to the death of organisms far from the impact site. The asteroid is believed to have struck what is now the Yucatan Peninsula in Mexico, but its effects reached across the planet rapidly.

The glass beads, known as impact spherules, formed when rock vaporized by the collision condensed in the upper atmosphere and fell back to Earth as tiny glass droplets. Finding these spherules embedded in the biological tissues of fish provides a rare snapshot of the immediate consequences of the impact.

The North Dakota site has been studied for years, but the specific detail about impact spherules in fish gills adds new evidence about the timeline of mass extinction. The fish appear to have been breathing normally when the debris began falling from the sky.

This level of preservation is unusual in the fossil record. Most fossils form over longer time periods, making it difficult to pinpoint events to a single day. The combination of rapid burial and the presence of impact material allows scientists to reconstruct the sequence of events following the asteroid strike.

The asteroid impact is the leading explanation for the mass extinction that ended the Cretaceous period and killed off non-avian dinosaurs along with roughly three-quarters of plant and animal species on Earth. The North Dakota site offers a ground-level view of how quickly the disaster unfolded for organisms living thousands of miles from the impact zone.

The North Dakota site has been studied for years, but the specific detail about impact spherules in fish gills adds new evidence about the timeline of mass extinction. The fish appear to have been breathing normally when the debris began falling from the sky.

This level of preservation is unusual in the fossil record. Most fossils form over longer time periods, making it difficult to pinpoint events to a single day. The combination of rapid burial and the presence of impact material allows scientists to reconstruct the sequence of events following the asteroid strike.

The asteroid impact is the leading explanation for the mass extinction that ended the Cretaceous period and killed off non-avian dinosaurs along with roughly three-quarters of plant and animal species on Earth. The North Dakota site offers a ground-level view of how quickly the disaster unfolded for organisms living thousands of miles from the impact zone.
