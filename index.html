1:52 AM ET

A fossil site in North Dakota appears to have preserved a snapshot of the day the asteroid that killed the dinosaurs struck Earth 66 million years ago. Researchers have found evidence at the site that suggests the animals preserved there died within hours of the impact.

The discovery includes tiny glass beads lodged in the gills of fossilized fish. These beads formed from rock that was vaporized during the asteroid impact, then solidified as it fell back through the atmosphere. The presence of these spherules in the fish gills indicates the fish were alive when the debris rained down from the sky.

The Chicxulub impact occurred when an asteroid approximately six miles wide struck what is now the Yucatan Peninsula in Mexico. The collision released energy equivalent to billions of atomic bombs, triggering global wildfires, tsunamis, and a prolonged period of darkness that led to mass extinction.

The North Dakota site sits approximately 2,000 miles from the impact crater. Despite this distance, the fossil record shows rapid death and burial of the animals preserved there. The timing suggests these organisms perished during the immediate aftermath of the strike, rather than during the longer-term environmental collapse that followed.

Fossil sites typically preserve organisms that died over extended periods, making it difficult to link specific layers to individual events. This site offers a rare window into a single catastrophic moment. The preservation quality allows scientists to examine what happened in the hours following impact, rather than just the broad pattern of extinction.

The glass beads found at the site match the chemical signature of material ejected from the Chicxulub crater. This spherule layer has been identified at multiple locations around the world, marking the boundary between the Cretaceous and Paleogene periods. Finding the beads inside fish gills provides direct evidence that these animals were filtering water when the debris fell.

The discovery adds to the understanding of how the mass extinction unfolded. While scientists have long known that the asteroid impact caused the extinction of non-avian dinosaurs and approximately 75 percent of all species on Earth, sites like this one reveal the immediate sequence of events that followed the collision.
