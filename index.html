Iran has warned it will retaliate after U.S. military forces struck sites near the Strait of Hormuz, threatening to renew attacks against American forces. The Iranian government accused the United States of a grave violation of a ceasefire.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio said negotiations to end the war were continuing despite the renewed strikes. President Trump offered conflicting signals on peace talks, saying he wanted a good deal or no deal.

U.S. officials said they observed threats from Iran before launching the strikes on military sites. The attacks targeted infrastructure near the strategically important Strait of Hormuz, a critical waterway for global oil shipments.

The strikes mark a resumption of military action after a period of ceasefire. American casualties in the conflict have risen as military operations began again, though specific numbers were not disclosed.

The warnings from Tehran came as both nations appeared to be pursuing diplomatic negotiations while simultaneously engaging in military action. Rubio emphasized that talks to end the conflict remained ongoing despite the latest escalation.

The Strait of Hormuz has been a focal point of tensions between the United States and Iran. The waterway serves as a major transit route for oil tankers, making it strategically significant for global energy markets.

Iran has not specified what form its threatened retaliation might take or when it might occur. The country maintains that the U.S. strikes violated an existing ceasefire agreement between the two nations.

The renewed military action complicates diplomatic efforts to reach a lasting agreement. Trump has indicated he is seeking favorable terms in any potential deal with Iran, while Rubio maintains that diplomatic channels remain open.

The situation represents a volatile moment in U.S.-Iran relations, with both military confrontation and diplomatic negotiations occurring simultaneously. The international community is watching closely as both nations navigate between warfare and potential peace talks.

The surge followed strong analyst support, with UBS issuing a significantly raised price target for the stock. The chipmaker has benefited from growing demand for memory chips used in artificial intelligence systems.

Both the Trump administration and Wall Street analysts have backed the company's expansion plans. Micron manufactures dynamic random access memory and other chips essential for data centers and AI computing systems.

The valuation milestone reflects broader momentum in the semiconductor industry. Memory chip demand has increased as technology companies build out infrastructure for AI applications and cloud computing services.

Micron's stock performance contributed to wider market gains. The S&P 500 and Nasdaq reached new record highs during the same trading session, driven partly by enthusiasm around AI-related technology stocks.

Analysts attribute the rally to expectations of sustained demand for high-bandwidth memory products. These specialized chips command premium prices and higher profit margins compared to standard memory products.

The memory chip sector has experienced cyclical volatility in recent years, with prices fluctuating based on supply and demand dynamics. Industry observers note that AI-driven demand may provide more stable long-term growth compared to previous boom-and-bust cycles in the memory market.

Micron's achievement comes as the U.S. government has prioritized domestic semiconductor manufacturing. The company has announced plans for new fabrication facilities in the United States, supported by federal incentives aimed at reducing reliance on overseas chip production.

11:44 AM ET

The Dutch government has prevented a US company from acquiring a cloud service provider that hosts the country's digital identification system, citing concerns about risks to the public interest.

This decision reflects Europe's broader effort to decrease reliance on American technology infrastructure. The cloud company in question manages essential services for Dutch citizens, including the national digital ID platform that enables residents to verify their identity online when accessing government services and conducting private transactions.

Dutch officials used national security and public interest protections to block the proposed acquisition. The government has not disclosed the identity of the US company or financial details of the deal.

The blocking action demonstrates growing anxiety among European governments regarding foreign ownership of critical digital infrastructure. Several European nations have introduced or are considering restrictions on overseas ownership of technology services considered vital to national operations.

Cloud computing has become a particular area of focus for these protective measures. Across Europe, both governments and businesses depend heavily on cloud providers to store sensitive information and operate essential systems. Ownership of these platforms provides companies considerable control over data access, system availability, and security standards.

The Netherlands joins other European countries taking steps to protect their digital infrastructure. France and Germany have previously established rules for cloud providers serving government clients. The European Union has also discussed broader initiatives to strengthen digital sovereignty across member states.

Digital identification systems warrant special protection because they authenticate citizens accessing government benefits, filing tax returns, voting in some jurisdictions, and carrying out financial transactions. Any compromise or failure affecting these systems could impact millions of residents and damage public confidence in government services.

The Dutch digital ID system allows citizens to log into government websites and verify their identity with private companies without establishing separate accounts. Since its introduction, millions of Dutch residents have adopted the system for regular use.

This acquisition block occurs amid wider disagreements between the United States and European nations regarding technology regulation, data privacy, and control of digital infrastructure. European officials have raised concerns that dependence on American technology companies creates security vulnerabilities and restricts European policy independence.

The US company seeking to complete the acquisition has not made any public statements regarding the Dutch government's decision or whether it intends to challenge the ruling through legal channels.

The move highlights the tension between American technology companies seeking to expand their global reach and European nations working to maintain control over infrastructure they consider essential to national interests. As digital systems become increasingly central to government operations and public services, these conflicts over ownership and control are likely to persist.

Jazz saxophonist Sonny Rollins dies at 95

1:42 PM ET

Sonny Rollins, widely considered one of the greatest jazz saxophonists in history, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his website on Monday and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York, though no cause of death was disclosed.

Rollins was a towering figure in jazz who helped transform the genre from dance and ballad music into something far more expressive. Over a career spanning from the late 1940s onward, he released more than 60 albums and collaborated with jazz legends including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk and John Coltrane. He was among the last living representatives of the bebop generation that revolutionized American music.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he grew up in Harlem and received the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began studying saxophone at age seven, inspired by musical siblings and jazz pioneers like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. His high school band included future stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew and Art Taylor.

Rollins defined himself as someone who relied on feeling rather than calculation, an approach that made him fearless in breaking with convention. Miles Davis once wrote that Rollins "quickly became a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" for his aggressive innovation and fresh musical ideas.

His personal struggles with heroin addiction led to an armed robbery conviction in 1950. He spent ten months on Rikers Island but successfully overcame his addiction through rehabilitation in 1955. This turning point sparked extraordinary creative output. He released his debut album as a bandleader in 1953, followed by 17 more albums by decade's end, including landmark recordings like "Saxophone Colossus" (1956), featuring his signature tune "St Thomas," and "Freedom Suite" (1958), whose title track became a statement on freedom during the civil rights era.

In 1959, Rollins withdrew from public performance for three years to practice intensively, famously working up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge walkway. His 1962 comeback album, "The Bridge," reflected his renewed commitment to his craft.

Throughout subsequent decades, Rollins continued pushing musical boundaries, exploring avant-garde approaches, Latin music and fusion styles. He composed the soundtrack to the 1966 film "Alfie" and later used his platform to advocate for environmental causes, releasing the album "Global Warming" in 1998.

Rollins married twice, first to Dawn Finney in 1957, then to Lucille Pearson in 1965. Pearson died in 2004. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz," and President Barack Obama presented him with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, crediting Rollins with inspiring him to take creative risks.

Lavrov spoke with Rubio by phone and drew his attention to the warning, according to a Russian readout of the conversation.

The warning represents an escalation in rhetoric as Russia prepares what it characterized as forthcoming military action against the Ukrainian capital. The communication between the two foreign ministers comes as the conflict enters its fifth year, with Russia signaling its intention to intensify operations.

Separately, Dmitry Medvedev, deputy chair of Russia's Security Council and the country's former president, threatened European Union diplomats stationed in Kyiv. The EU had stated it would maintain its diplomatic presence in the Ukrainian capital unchanged despite Russian warnings.

Medvedev responded to the EU's position with a pointed message. He suggested the EU had diplomats to spare and needed to trim its headcount in Kyiv, framing the statement as a direct threat to European diplomatic personnel who remain in Ukraine's capital.

The dual warnings from senior Russian officials mark a coordinated effort to pressure both American and European diplomatic presence in Ukraine. Russia has not specified the timing or scope of the strikes it says are planned for Kyiv.

The State Department has not publicly responded to Lavrov's communication with Rubio. The United States maintains diplomatic operations in Ukraine and has not issued new evacuation guidance for American citizens in the country since the warning was delivered.

The threats come amid ongoing tensions between Russia and Western nations over support for Ukraine. The conflict began in 2022 and has resulted in significant military engagement and civilian casualties across Ukrainian territory.

Jazz saxophonist Sonny Rollins dies at 95

4:00 PM ET

Sonny Rollins, widely considered one of the greatest jazz saxophonists in history, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his website on Monday and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York, though no cause of death was disclosed.

Rollins was a towering figure in jazz who helped transform the genre from dance and ballad music into something far more expressive. Over a career spanning from the late 1940s onward, he released more than 60 albums and collaborated with jazz legends including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk and John Coltrane. He was among the last living representatives of the bebop generation that revolutionized American music.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he grew up in Harlem and received the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began studying saxophone at age seven, inspired by musical siblings and jazz pioneers like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. His high school band included future stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew and Art Taylor.

Rollins defined himself as someone who relied on feeling rather than calculation, an approach that made him fearless in breaking with convention. Miles Davis once wrote that Rollins "quickly became a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" for his aggressive innovation and fresh musical ideas.

His personal struggles with heroin addiction led to an armed robbery conviction in 1950. He spent ten months on Rikers Island but successfully overcame his addiction through rehabilitation in 1955. This turning point sparked extraordinary creative output. He released his debut album as a bandleader in 1953, followed by 17 more albums by decade's end, including landmark recordings like "Saxophone Colossus" (1956), featuring his signature tune "St Thomas," and "Freedom Suite" (1958), whose title track became a statement on freedom during the civil rights era.

In 1959, Rollins withdrew from public performance for three years to practice intensively, famously working up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge walkway. His 1962 comeback album, "The Bridge," reflected his renewed commitment to his craft.

Throughout subsequent decades, Rollins continued pushing musical boundaries, exploring avant-garde approaches, Latin music and fusion styles. He composed the soundtrack to the 1966 film "Alfie" and later used his platform to advocate for environmental causes, releasing the album "Global Warming" in 1998.

Rollins married twice, first to Dawn Finney in 1957, then to Lucille Pearson in 1965. Pearson died in 2004. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz," and President Barack Obama presented him with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, crediting Rollins with inspiring him to take creative risks.
