1:45 AM ET

The United States has carried out fresh military strikes against Iranian targets, focusing on missile launch sites and naval vessels. US Central Command described the operations as taken in "self-defense" as diplomatic negotiations continue in Qatar.

The strikes come as senior Iranian negotiators are in Qatar holding talks aimed at ending the conflict. Secretary of State Marco Rubio indicated that a potential Iran deal could take days to finalize, even as military operations proceeded.

British officials have indicated they have not ruled out joining future strikes against Iranian ballistic missile launch sites. Western officials expressed concern that Iran could otherwise deplete allied air defense interceptor stocks. US heavy bombers are expected to arrive at UK bases in Diego Garcia and Gloucestershire in the coming days, positioned to attack Iran's underground "missile cities."

One western official stated: "I wouldn't rule anything out at all because we just don't know what will happen day to day, week to week as this progresses."

Destroying Iran's ballistic missile stockpiles and launch capabilities represents a central objective of the joint US and Israeli bombing campaign. Many of these facilities are buried underground, requiring specialized bunker-buster munitions from B-2 or B-52 bombers, which could demand additional RAF support beyond simply providing base access.

Western officials believe Iran has "several more days'" worth of ballistic missiles remaining in its stockpile if it continues firing at current rates. However, ongoing US-Israeli attacks may limit Iran's ability to maintain this pace. Iran may also choose to conserve stocks to preserve military capabilities.

Gulf states and other regional allies have expended "quite a lot" of their Patriot air defense interceptors defending against Iranian attacks. The UAE has intercepted 876 drones and 175 ballistic missiles out of 941 drones and 189 missiles fired at it since Saturday, according to western officials.

The Trump administration is simultaneously pursuing broader regional diplomatic efforts. The president has urged additional countries to normalize relations with Israel as part of any potential Iran settlement, seeking to expand the Abraham Accords framework.

Initial UK reluctance to participate in the bombing campaign that began with the killing of Iran's supreme leader shifted when Prime Minister Keir Starmer partly changed course. Starmer said he would allow the US to destroy Iranian missiles "at source" from UK bases targeting "their storage depots or the launchers which are used to fire the missiles," describing this as a "specific and limited defensive purpose."

The situation remains fluid with military operations and diplomatic efforts proceeding on parallel tracks. Both Iranian and US officials have recently downplayed expectations for an imminent breakthrough in negotiations. Iran has stated that contradictory statements from the United States are hindering progress toward reaching an agreement.

Governor Mikie Sherrill of New Jersey arrived at Delaney Hall detention facility in Newark on Monday morning seeking to meet with families of detainees and listen to community advocates, but was unable to gain access to the immigration facility itself.

The visit came amid an expanding hunger strike that began Friday when detainee Martin Soto announced a work and food strike demanding the immediate release of elderly detainees, young people, and those with medical conditions. About 300 detainees had signed a letter alleging the facility suffered from poor conditions and inadequate medical care.

Tensions escalated over the weekend when immigration agents attempted to transfer Soto to another facility. His wife, Gabriela, said she witnessed guards pushing her husband into a van as he banged on the window and screamed. Demonstrators gathered outside Delaney Hall and blocked the entrance to prevent the transfer.

Early Monday morning around 1 a.m., ICE agents blocked roadways near the facility's back gate. When agents attempted to clear the area with force, clashes erupted between officers and protesters. According to reports, ICE agents deployed pepper spray and batons, pushing demonstrators onto sidewalks and into parked cars. At least one person was pepper-sprayed and another suffered a leg injury.

Despite the demonstrations, federal authorities successfully transferred Soto to the Elizabeth contract detention facility later that day. A federal judge had initially ordered that Soto not be moved out of state while his habeas petition proceeded through court, but immigration officials said they relocated him to another ICE facility.

The Department of Homeland Security defended the agents' actions, stating that approximately 125 demonstrators had formed a human chain around the facility's entrances and set up barricades. The agency said protesters struck ICE vehicles with unidentified objects, causing significant damage. Officials said ICE agents dispersed approximately 70 agitators and removed barricades to restore operations.

Gabriela Soto alleged that guards had retaliated against her husband because of her activism. She said agents held Martin in a cell for eight hours on Friday and questioned him about her protest efforts, asking whether she was organizing the strike.

As a result of the incident, the facility suspended visitation "out of an abundance of caution to ensure the continued safety of staff, detainees, and visitors," according to DHS.

Senator Andy Kim joined Governor Sherrill at the facility Monday morning. "Delaney Hall must be shut down immediately," Kim stated on social media. "ICE cannot be allowed to continue operating in this way."

GEO Group, which operates the facility, defended its operations and said detainees receive around-the-clock medical care, legal and family visitation, library access, translation services, and other support services.

Google has introduced its most significant redesign of the search box in nearly 30 years, fundamentally transforming how users interact with the search engine. The company announced the changes at its annual Google I/O developer conference, rolling out the updates globally to both desktop and mobile versions on Tuesday.

The redesigned search interface now accepts longer, more conversational queries than before, moving away from the keyword-based syntax that defined Google Search since its inception. Rather than returning a simple list of links, the new system directs users to engage with Google's AI chatbot, powered by the company's latest artificial intelligence model, Gemini 3.5. The visual presentation more closely resembles the AI Overviews that Google currently provides, featuring bulleted summaries and interactive elements.

Elizabeth Reid, who oversees the search product, called the changes "the biggest Google Search had seen in its nearly 30-year history." She stated that "Google Search is AI search."

Users can still access the traditional link-based search results by clicking a tab titled "Web." The AI-powered interface includes features that allow users to perform actions directly within Google's ecosystem, such as creating calendar invites or building spreadsheets. When users upload photos, videos, or documents to the Chrome browser's search bar, the interface automatically switches to AI mode.

According to Google executives, search queries reached an all-time high last month. The company's AI Mode, introduced a year ago, has experienced rapid growth, with queries to the chatbot-specific interface doubling every quarter since its debut.

For subscribers to Google's AI Pro and Ultra plans, the updated search will offer the ability to create "information agents," autonomous AI bots capable of performing detailed research and returning summaries or action plans. A feature called Gemini Spark can access information from users' Gmail, Google Calendar, and other Google services to perform research, shop, and plan trips and appointments.

The expansion of these AI agents from niche, business-focused applications to the broader consumer market represents a significant shift. Josh Woodward, who oversees Gemini's development, described the goal as creating "a universal assistant that's personal, proactive, persistent."

Google also announced the monthly user count for Gemini reaches 900 million, though this remains lower than ChatGPT's 900 million weekly users.

Alongside the search redesign, Google unveiled intelligent eyewear developed with Samsung, Warby Parker, and Gentle Monster. The voice-activated glasses, equipped with embedded cameras, will respond to Gemini commands and are scheduled for release in the fall. This marks Google's return to wearable technology after discontinuing Google Glass in 2015.

Yesterday, 11:32 PM ET

Sonny Rollins, widely recognized as one of the greatest jazz saxophonists in history, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his official website on Monday, with his publicist Terri Hinte confirming the news. He passed away at his home in Woodstock, New York, though no cause of death was disclosed.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, Rollins grew up in Harlem during a vibrant period for jazz. He earned his nickname Sonny from his grandmother and began playing saxophone at age seven, inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. His early bands included future stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew and Art Taylor.

Rollins became known for his genius approach to melody and improvisation. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting Rollins with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, President Barack Obama said the musician had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

His career spanned more than six decades, producing over 60 albums and collaborations with Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane and Dizzy Gillespie. Miles Davis himself praised Rollins as "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" who was "an aggressive, innovative player who always had fresh musical ideas."

Rollins' creative output was remarkable. His 1956 album "Saxophone Colossus" featured his signature tune "St Thomas," inspired by his mother's Caribbean birthplace. "Way Out West" from 1957 explored a piano-free style, while "Freedom Suite" in 1958 featured an emancipated 20-minute title track that became an argument for freedom during the civil rights movement.

The saxophonist's commitment to his craft was legendary. In 1959, he took a three-year break from performing and recording, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge to avoid disturbing his neighbors. This dedication inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge." He later took another sabbatical from 1969 to 1971, traveling to an Indian ashram to study yoga, philosophy and meditation.

Beyond jazz, Rollins composed and performed the soundtrack to the 1966 film "Alfie" and contributed uncredited soloing to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album "Tattoo You." In his later years, he fused funk and calypso into his playing and championed environmental causes through benefit concerts and his 1998 album "Global Warming."

Rollins was married twice, to Dawn Finney in 1957 and to Lucille Pearson from 1965 until her death in 2004. He leaves behind an unmatched legacy that continues to influence contemporary jazz musicians worldwide.

However, the decision has created compli...

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily halted deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo because of a widening Ebola outbreak in the country. The pause came after concerns that the flights could expose Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials to the virus and potentially bring the disease closer to the United States.

However, the decision has created complications for some detainees already sent to the region. At least one woman was moved to Kinshasa, the capital of the DRC, before officials announced the travel ban. Despite a judge's order for her return, administration officials have said they will not bring her back because of the Ebola-related restrictions on flights to the country.

The Ebola outbreak in Congo has raised alarm among public health officials, though experts say the move won't necessarily help prevent the spread of the disease. The hemorrhagic fever spreads through direct contact with bodily fluids of infected individuals and has historically resulted in high fatality rates in affected regions. Previous outbreaks in Congo have caused hundreds of deaths and required international public health responses.

The pause represents a notable shift in the administration's typical approach to deportation operations, which have generally proceeded regardless of conditions in destination countries. The Trump administration has conducted regular deportation flights as part of its immigration enforcement priorities, and human rights groups have documented a significant surge in these operations. According to monitoring by organizations tracking ICE flights, there was an 84 percent increase in deportation flights during 2025 compared to 2024.

The decision to halt flights to Congo reflects concern about protecting federal employees who would be required to travel there as part of deportation operations. ICE officers typically accompany deportees on flights and remain in destination countries during the removal process.

The administration has not specified how long the pause will remain in effect or what conditions would need to be met for flights to resume. The temporary halt applies specifically to deportation flights to Congo and does not affect removal operations to other countries.

Meanwhile, the Democratic Republic of Congo's World Cup team is still planning to travel to the United States for pre-tournament preparations despite the outbreak. The team cancelled its training schedule in Kinshasa due to health restrictions but intends to proceed with the American portion of its preparation.

The administration has previously responded to criticism of its deportation program by describing allegations of mistreatment and inhumane conditions as "categorically false." Officials have argued that detention centers used in the deportation process maintain "higher standards than most US prisons" and that restraints used during flights represent "long-standing, standard ICE protocol" designed to ensure safety.

Sonny Rollins, widely recognized as one of the greatest jazz saxophonists in history, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his official website on Monday, with his publicist Terri Hinte confirming the news. He passed away at his home in Woodstock, New York, though no cause of death was disclosed.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, Rollins grew up in Harlem during a vibrant period for jazz. He earned his nickname Sonny from his grandmother and began playing saxophone at age seven, inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. His early bands included future stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew and Art Taylor.

Rollins became known for his genius approach to melody and improvisation. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting Rollins with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, President Barack Obama said the musician had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

His career spanned more than six decades, producing over 60 albums and collaborations with Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane and Dizzy Gillespie. Miles Davis himself praised Rollins as "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" who was "an aggressive, innovative player who always had fresh musical ideas."

Rollins' creative output was remarkable. His 1956 album "Saxophone Colossus" featured his signature tune "St Thomas," inspired by his mother's Caribbean birthplace. "Way Out West" from 1957 explored a piano-free style, while "Freedom Suite" in 1958 featured an emancipated 20-minute title track that became an argument for freedom during the civil rights movement.

The saxophonist's commitment to his craft was legendary. In 1959, he took a three-year break from performing and recording, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge to avoid disturbing his neighbors. This dedication inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge." He later took another sabbatical from 1969 to 1971, traveling to an Indian ashram to study yoga, philosophy and meditation.

Beyond jazz, Rollins composed and performed the soundtrack to the 1966 film "Alfie" and contributed uncredited soloing to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album "Tattoo You." In his later years, he fused funk and calypso into his playing and championed environmental causes through benefit concerts and his 1998 album "Global Warming."

Rollins was married twice, to Dawn Finney in 1957 and to Lucille Pearson from 1965 until her death in 2004. He leaves behind an unmatched legacy that continues to influence contemporary jazz musicians worldwide.
