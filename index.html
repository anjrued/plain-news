Today, 7:00 PM ET

Scientists have refined their measurements of global sea level rise and confirmed that the rate of increase has been accelerating for more than 60 years. The findings address a critical gap in climate science and provide clearer evidence of how oceans are responding to warming temperatures.

Researchers used improved methods to analyze decades of satellite and tide gauge data. They found that while sea levels rose throughout the 20th century, the pace began increasing around 1960 and has continued to speed up since. The acceleration means coastal communities face faster changes than previously estimated.

The study improves on earlier work by accounting for measurement uncertainties and regional variations. Satellites provide detailed global coverage but only began monitoring in the 1990s. Tide gauges offer longer records but are unevenly distributed around coastlines. By combining both data sources with better statistical methods, scientists produced more reliable estimates of when and how quickly acceleration began.

Current measurements show global mean sea level rising at approximately 3.4 millimeters per year, compared to roughly 1.4 millimeters per year through most of the 20th century. The acceleration is driven primarily by thermal expansion as oceans warm and increased melting from ice sheets in Greenland and Antarctica.

The research matters for coastal planning and infrastructure decisions. Many projections and risk assessments depend on accurate historical baselines. If acceleration started earlier than thought, it suggests ocean systems are responding more quickly to temperature changes. This affects flood risk calculations, protective infrastructure design, and timelines for adaptation measures in vulnerable areas.

Scientists note that small differences in acceleration rates compound significantly over decades. A city planning sea walls for 2100 needs to know whether water will rise linearly or at an increasing rate. The confirmation of 1960s acceleration gives planners a longer period to study and more confidence in projections.

The findings also help validate climate models. When models correctly reproduce historical acceleration patterns, it increases confidence in their future predictions. Several models had suggested acceleration began around this period, and the improved measurements support those simulations.

Coastal regions worldwide already face increased flooding during storms and high tides. Faster sea level rise will intensify these impacts, particularly in low-lying areas and small island nations. The research provides governments and communities clearer information for making decisions about where and how to build, what areas to protect, and when relocation might become necessary.
