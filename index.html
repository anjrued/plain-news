Yesterday, 8:58 PM ET

A 21-year-old gunman was fatally shot by Secret Service officers after opening fire outside the White House on Saturday evening. The suspect, identified in media reports as Nasire Best, approached a security checkpoint near 17th Street and Pennsylvania Avenue, removed a pistol from a bag, and fired at officers stationed there before they returned fire and struck him.

Best was taken to a nearby hospital where he was later pronounced dead. A bystander was also struck during the exchange of gunfire, though authorities have not confirmed whether the person was hit by the suspect's shots or by return fire from Secret Service officers. No Secret Service agents were injured in the incident.

The shooting prompted a brief lockdown of the White House around 6 p.m., with President Donald Trump inside the building at the time. Trump later praised the "swift and professional action" of law enforcement on social media and described the gunman as having "a violent history" and a "possible obsession" with the White House.

Court records reveal that Best was previously known to the Secret Service. According to a July 2025 court filing, he had been observed multiple times the previous summer "walking around the White House complex inquiring how to gain access at various entry points." On June 26, 2025, he was involuntarily committed for obstructing vehicle entry to part of the complex. Less than two weeks later, on July 10, he was arrested for unlawful entry after ignoring warning signs and walking into a restricted area outside the White House.

During that arrest, Best claimed to be Jesus and told officers "that he wanted to get arrested," according to court documents. A judge issued a stay away order barring him from the White House area. A bench warrant was later issued after Best failed to attend a subsequent court hearing.

An unnamed law enforcement official described Best to the Washington Post as an emotionally disturbed person who was known to law enforcement. Trump suggested the incident underscores the importance of enhanced security measures, referencing plans to create what he called "the most safe and secure space of its kind ever built in Washington, DC."

The FBI is assisting the Secret Service and local police in investigating the shooting. Authorities are working to determine how Best approached the checkpoint armed and what prompted the confrontation. The area around the shooting scene was secured as investigators processed evidence and interviewed witnesses.

The incident marks a direct attack on Secret Service personnel protecting the White House complex. Security checkpoints around the White House are staffed by armed officers who screen visitors and monitor the perimeter as the first line of defense against unauthorized entry.

Donald Trump unilaterally extended a two-week ceasefire with Iran on Tuesday, announcing the decision on his Truth Social platform after hours of volatile messaging that included threats of imminent military action. The president said he would maintain the pause in hostilities until Iranian leaders submit a unified peace proposal.

"Based on the fact that the Government of Iran is seriously fractured," Trump wrote, he agreed to an extension at the request of Pakistan's Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif and Field Marshal Asim Munir. Trump directed the U.S. military to continue a blockade of Iranian ports while remaining prepared for potential strikes.

The announcement came after a chaotic day that saw Vice President JD Vance's planned trip to Islamabad postponed. Trump had previously stated the U.S. military was "raring to go" before reversing course on the ceasefire extension.

The decision drew sharp criticism from Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, speaker of the Iranian parliament and the Islamic regime's lead negotiator. Ghalibaf's adviser, Mahdi Mohammadi, dismissed the ceasefire extension as "a ploy to buy time for a surprise strike" and declared that "the time for Iran to take the initiative has come."

Earlier in the week, Trump had claimed the peace deal was "largely negotiated," including provisions to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. However, Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi had only declared the strait open briefly before it was closed again after the Revolutionary Guard Corps expressed anger over Trump's social media posts proclaiming American victory.

Pakistan's Prime Minister Sharif, who has served as the principal mediator, thanked Trump for the ceasefire extension and pledged to continue efforts toward a negotiated settlement. However, Iran has made clear it will not negotiate under threat, with Ghalibaf declaring that his country was preparing "to reveal new cards on the battlefield."

The ongoing U.S. Navy blockade of Iranian ports represents a major obstacle to resuming talks. Iran has demanded the blockade end before negotiations resume, but Trump and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent both indicated on Tuesday that the blockade would continue. Bessent warned that Iranian oil storage facilities would soon reach capacity if the blockade persists.

The closure of the Strait of Hormuz, a critical waterway through which roughly one-fifth of the world's oil and gas passes, has created severe disruptions to global energy markets. The International Energy Agency's head called the combined impact of the conflict and Russia's war with Ukraine "the biggest crisis in history" for global energy supplies.

Negotiations that began in Islamabad ten days ago produced no breakthrough on the strait's status or other key issues. Both sides have signaled willingness to reach an agreement, though significant disagreements remain unresolved.

A few minutes ago

Oil prices have fallen sharply, sliding below $100 per barrel for the first time in two weeks as reports emerged of potential progress toward a U.S.-Iran peace deal. Brent crude futures, the global oil benchmark, dropped 5.5% to just under $98 a barrel, reflecting market optimism about a possible resolution to recent tensions.

The price decline centers on hopes that an agreement could lead to the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, a critical chokepoint through which roughly a fifth of global petroleum flows. The strait's closure sent energy prices soaring after the U.S. and Israel launched missile strikes on Tehran on February 28. Trump stated on Saturday that an agreement would include reopening the strait, though he provided no additional details. Tehran also signaled progress on talks but indicated that nuclear weapons issues would not be part of an initial framework.

However, key obstacles remain. The U.S. and Iran continue to disagree over significant issues, including Iran's blockade of the strait. Analysts cautioned that previous negotiations have reached similar stages only to break down. Warren Patterson, head of commodities strategy at ING, told Reuters: "We've been at this stage before, only for talks to break down. Therefore, the market will likely be more cautious about overreacting."

The market reaction extended beyond oil. Stock markets rallied on the news, with Japan's Nikkei 225 index rising nearly 3% and hitting a record high above 65,000 points for the first time. The pan-European Stoxx 600 index climbed 0.8%. Several markets were closed Monday for public holidays, including in the U.S. and the UK.

Currency markets also shifted, with the dollar dipping 0.25% against a basket of major currencies. The British pound gained 0.5% to $1.3492, its highest level since May 14.

Analysts emphasized the broader economic implications. Stephen Innes, an independent analyst, noted that "Treasury futures rallied, gold climbed and equity futures pushed higher as investors started pricing the possibility that the world's most dangerous energy choke point may soon reopen to something resembling normal flow."

The potential impact on inflation concerns markets significantly. Higher oil prices have driven inflation fears globally and prompted expectations of interest rate increases from central banks. If oil prices stabilize at lower levels, this could ease some inflationary pressures. Markets currently expect the Bank of England to raise rates twice this year, a shift from earlier expectations of rate cuts before the tensions with Iran escalated.

Any sustained reduction in oil prices would likely benefit consumers and businesses struggling with elevated energy costs, while also potentially influencing monetary policy decisions worldwide.

Google announced a redesign of its search box, the first change to the core interface in 25 years. The company revealed the update at its I/O developer conference, where it introduced what it calls the "agentic Gemini era."

The search giant integrated its Gemini artificial intelligence system directly into the search experience. Users can now choose between traditional web search and AI-generated responses through the modified search interface. The change represents a shift in how the company presents information to users.

The redesign prompted immediate discussion about the future of internet search. Some observers described the move as marking the end of what they called the internet's golden age, when search primarily returned links to websites rather than AI-generated summaries.

Users who prefer traditional search results can disable AI features using a search modifier, according to reports. The option allows people to opt out of AI-generated content while still using Google's search engine.

The rollout encountered early technical problems. Google's updated search struggled to define common words including "disregard," "stop," and "ignore." The issue generated attention online as users discovered the malfunction and shared examples.

Analysts noted the change could affect how websites receive traffic from Google. The AI integration may reduce clicks to external sites if users get answers directly from AI summaries rather than following links to source material.

Google positioned the update as a practical improvement to search functionality. The company emphasized that users retain control over whether to use AI features or stick with conventional link-based results.

The timing coincides with increased competition in AI-powered search. Multiple companies have launched alternative search engines that incorporate AI technology, aiming to challenge Google's dominance in the search market.

Industry observers described the change as significant not only for its technical implications but also for what it signals about the direction of online information discovery. The modification to a 25-year-old interface reflects shifting expectations about how people want to find and consume information online.

Two-time NASCAR Cup Series champion Kyle Busch has died at age 41 following a severe illness that required hospitalization, NASCAR announced Thursday. The racing organization released a statement expressing deep sadness over the loss of one of the sport's most accomplished and competitive drivers.

"We are saddened and heartbroken to share the news of the passing of Kyle Busch, a two-time Cup champion and one of our sport's greatest and fiercest drivers," NASCAR said. The organization extended condolences to Busch's family, Richard Childress Racing, and the entire motorsports community. No specific cause of death was disclosed.

Busch's family announced his hospitalization earlier Thursday, noting that he would be unable to compete in the scheduled NASCAR race in Charlotte, North Carolina that weekend. His death came just days before the Coca-Cola 600, one of NASCAR's marquee events held on Memorial Day weekend.

The Las Vegas native had won NASCAR championships in 2015 and 2019, establishing himself as a generational talent. Busch accumulated 232 wins across NASCAR's three national series, more than any other driver in history. He was in his fourth season with Richard Childress Racing after previously winning titles with Joe Gibbs Racing.

In his early career, Busch stormed onto the Cup Series scene in 2005 by winning rookie of the year. Known by nicknames including "Rowdy" and "Wild Thing," he became a polarizing figure in the sport, recognized for his sharp competitive spirit and sometimes controversial behavior.

Busch's health issues began surfacing during a May 10 race at Watkins Glen, where he struggled with a severe sinus cold that worsened due to the intense G-forces at the upstate New York road course. Despite requesting medical assistance from the team doctor, Busch completed the race and finished eighth.

At the time of his death, Busch ranked 24th in Cup Series standings with only two top-10 finishes through 12 races in the current season. However, he demonstrated his continued competitive spirit by winning a truck series race at Dover just six days before his death.

A joint statement from Busch's family, Richard Childress Racing, and NASCAR described him as "a rare talent, one who comes along once in a generation" and noted his significant contributions to the sport. "Throughout a career that spanned more than two decades, Kyle set records in national series wins, won championships at NASCAR's highest level and fostered the next generation of drivers as an owner in the Truck Series," the statement said.

Busch is survived by his wife, Samantha, and their two children, Brexton and Lennix. The racing community united in paying tribute to the driver and his legacy during the racing weekend.

Yesterday, 11:28 AM ET

Russian filmmaker Andrei Zvyagintsev has made a dramatic return to international cinema at the Cannes Film Festival, premiering his first film in nine years and using the platform to directly challenge Vladimir Putin over the Ukraine conflict.

Zvyagintsev's new film, Minotaur, marks a significant moment in his career. The director spent much of his absence from filmmaking recovering from severe Covid-19 complications. After learning of Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, he was hospitalized in Germany with 90% lung damage and unable to move or feel his limbs for months. "It was in this state that I learned of the outbreak of the war in Ukraine," he said in a recent interview. "It was a shock; I felt immense pain and deep despair." He eventually spent 11 months in hospitals, slowly relearning to walk and hold a spoon before returning to filmmaking.

Minotaur is an adaptation of Claude Chabrol's 1969 erotic thriller, relocated to a medium-sized Russian provincial town. The film follows a business executive confronting both personal betrayal and professional crisis as his wife has an affair while he faces laying off employees. At Cannes, Zvyagintsev called on Putin to "put an end to this carnage," describing the war as "butchery."

The 62-year-old director is competing for the Palme d'Or against major international filmmakers including Pedro Almodóvar and Asghar Farhadi. His return to the red carpet represents a major moment, as his absence from cinema has been keenly felt. According to Julian Graffy, a professor of Russian film at University College London, "Since Zvyagintsev was the most important of the new wave of directors that flowered in the early 21st century, the loss of his voice has been felt most keenly of them all."

Zvyagintsev's films have long functioned as damning allegories of Russian apathy toward state oppression. His 2014 film Leviathan, which premiered at Cannes and won the festival's jury prize, depicted corruption involving local officials and the Orthodox Church. His 2017 film Loveless explored how fear of state authority shapes everyday life.

These works have drawn both critical acclaim and official scrutiny. While the Russian ministry of culture initially funded 35% of Leviathan's budget, then-minister Vladimir Medinsky later criticized the film and accused Zvyagintsev of concerning himself only with confrontation.

His public denunciation of the Ukraine war at Cannes places him among a small group of prominent Russian cultural figures willing to speak against the conflict while maintaining international careers. Many Russian artists have either left the country, remained silent, or expressed government support.

May 21, 10:12 AM ET

The Colorado Democratic Party has censured Governor Jared Polis for commuting the prison sentence of Tina Peters, a former county election official whose case has become central to Republican claims about the 2020 presidential election.

Peters, the former clerk in Mesa County, was convicted in 2024 of four felonies and three misdemeanors. In 2021, she allowed unauthorized individuals, including a former pro surfer named Conan Hayes, to access the county's voting equipment and copy sensitive data. Polis reduced Peters' sentence from eight and a half years to approximately four and a half years, making her eligible for parole release on June 1.

In his clemency letter, Polis wrote: "This is an extremely unusual and lengthy sentence for a first time offender who committed non-violent crimes." He had signaled his leanings toward clemency in March by comparing Peters' case to that of former state senator Sonya Jaquez Lewis, who received only probation and community service for submitting forged letters to the legislature. Polis argued that justice should be applied evenly across similar cases.

Colorado election officials strongly disagreed with this comparison and the clemency decision. Jena Griswold, the state's top election official, stated that the clemency "validates Trump's basest impulses and emboldens this lawless president." She emphasized that Polis had bypassed a court process and warned that the decision would embolden election denial movements nationwide.

"What it actually is is special treatment," Griswold said, noting that Peters should have waited for resentencing through the courts as directed by the Court of Appeals.

Matt Crane, executive director of the Colorado Clerks Association, called the decision a signal that "it's open season on our elections and election officials." He expressed that the group was "furious, disgusted, and deeply disappointed."

Peters has become a prominent figure among those promoting false claims about the 2020 election. Hayes, who accessed the voting systems, is affiliated with MyPillow CEO Mike Lindell, a leading spreader of election misinformation. Sensitive passwords and information from the county's voting equipment later were published online by right-wing personalities.

The trial judge, Matthew Barrett, had criticized Peters when sentencing her in 2024, saying "You are no hero. You're a charlatan who used, and is still using, your prior position in office to peddle a snake oil that's been proven to be junk time and time again."

Former President Donald Trump had repeatedly called for Peters' release and issued her a federal pardon, though that effort was largely symbolic since Peters faced state charges. Trump also instructed the Justice Department to seek her release.

The censure represents a significant political rift within Colorado's Democratic Party over how to balance criminal justice reform with protecting election security.
