Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest saxophonists in jazz history, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his website Monday and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was given.

Rollins transformed jazz saxophone over a career spanning more than seven decades. He became known for his powerful tone, melodic improvisations, and willingness to push the boundaries of the genre. His work influenced generations of musicians who followed.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He grew up in Harlem and started learning saxophone at age seven, inspired by family members and jazz heroes like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. Early in his career, he performed alongside Charlie Parker, Miles Davis, and Thelonious Monk during the bebop era.

Miles Davis wrote about how Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" because he was "an aggressive, innovative player who always had fresh musical ideas."

In the 1950s, Rollins recorded extensively, producing albums that remain essential listening for jazz students and enthusiasts. His 1956 album "Saxophone Colossus" earned him that nickname and featured the composition "St. Thomas," which became a jazz standard. The album showcased his ability to build solos that developed musical themes with logic and emotion.

Rollins struggled with heroin addiction in his early years. He was jailed for 10 months on Rikers Island in New York after committing armed robbery to fund his habit, but he kicked his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955. Getting clean sparked an astonishing burst of creativity.

In 1959, Rollins took a three-year break from recording and performing, practicing for up to 15 hours daily on the pedestrian walkway of the Williamsburg Bridge in New York. This practice session inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge." He later took another sabbatical between 1969 and 1971 to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout his career, Rollins remained restless with his own achievements. He continued exploring new musical directions, fusing his playing with funk, calypso, and R&B influences. He also composed music for the 1966 film "Alfie" and campaigned on environmental issues, releasing the 1998 album "Global Warming."

Rollins married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004. He received the Grammy Award for lifetime achievement in 2004 and the National Medal of the Arts in 2010. When presenting the medal, President Barack Obama said Rollins had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

A school minibus collided with a passenger train at a level crossing in northern Belgium on Tuesday morning, resulting in four deaths and leaving five children hospitalized with serious injuries.

The crash occurred just after 8am near the town of Buggenhout in Flanders, approximately 14 miles north of Brussels. The minibus was traveling to a special educational needs school when it attempted to cross the railway crossing despite lowered barriers and a flashing red light. The train, traveling from Bruges to Buggenhout, struck the vehicle. The train driver applied emergency brakes upon seeing the minivan but could not avoid the collision.

Belgium's transport minister Jean-Luc Crucke identified the victims as two children aged 15 and 12, a 27-year-old adult accompanying the children, and the 49-year-old minibus driver. Five other passengers, all children, were taken to hospital with serious injuries and were reported to be in stable condition.

The impact was severe enough to overturn the white minibus and catapult it into a nearby house driveway. Emergency services including ambulances and a helicopter responded to the scene.

The train was carrying approximately 100 passengers, none of whom were injured. One passenger experienced shock but required no medical treatment. Passengers were evacuated to a local fire station.

An investigation is underway to determine how the accident occurred. A spokesperson for Infrabel, the rail network operator, stated that authorities were working to understand the circumstances. The public prosecutor's office is leading the investigation into the collision.

The tragedy prompted responses from senior Belgian and European figures. Kurt Moens, the East Flanders politician responsible for the school, expressed deep condolences to the victims' families. Prime Minister Bart De Wever wrote on social media that he was "deeply moved by the horrific accident." Ursula von der Leyen, head of the European Commission, said she was heartbroken by the incident, stating that "Europe grieves with Belgium."

Belgium operates one of Europe's oldest railway networks with numerous level crossings. While the country has experienced accidents at these crossings historically, safety improvements have reduced incidents in recent years. According to Infrabel's latest annual report, five people died and nine were seriously injured at level crossings in 2024, with 30 accidents reported that year. This represented a historically low figure compared to the average of 45 to 50 accidents between 2008 and 2021.

Over the past two decades, Infrabel has removed 450 level crossings from the network as part of broader safety initiatives, leaving approximately 1,600 remaining across Belgium.

Gabbard cited personal reasons for her departure, writing that her husband Abraham "has recently been diagnosed with an extremely rare form of bone cancer." She added, "At this time, I must step away from public service to be by his side and fully support him through this battle."

However, Reuters reported that the White House forced Gabbard to resign, citing sources familiar with the matter. A spokesperson for Gabbard's office denied these reports as "100% false."

Gabbard's tenure as intelligence director was marked by significant challenges and tensions. Trump had been asking cabinet members last month whether he should replace her, according to people briefed on the discussions. She was largely sidelined as Trump launched attacks on Venezuela and Iran, excluded from key national security conversations on both countries.

The former Hawaii congresswoman and 2020 presidential candidate was an unconventional choice for the role. She lacked an intelligence background and her foreign policy views sometimes diverged from Trump's positions on overseas military intervention. Yet she worked to ingratiate herself to the president by promising to root out what she characterized as politicization in US spy agencies and by embracing his agenda of election denial.

Over time, her influence diminished within the administration. Trump said in a statement on his Truth Social platform that "after having done a great job, Tulsi Gabbard will be leaving the Administration on June 30th." He added that she "has done an incredible job, and we will miss her."

Gabbard's background includes military service in the Army National Guard and deployment to Iraq. She served in Congress representing Hawaii from 2013 to 2021 before running for the Democratic presidential nomination. She later became an independent and aligned with Trump politically.

The director of national intelligence position was created after the September 11 attacks to improve coordination among intelligence agencies. The role oversees 18 intelligence agencies and serves as the principal intelligence advisor to the president. The director manages the intelligence budget and coordinates intelligence activities across the government.

Aaron Lukas, the principal deputy director of national intelligence, will serve as acting director while the White House searches for a permanent replacement. The position has been held by both career intelligence officials and political appointees from both parties since its creation in 2005.

BP's board has removed chairman Albert Manifold with immediate effect, citing what the company described as serious concerns about governance standards, oversight and conduct. The oil giant announced the decision on Tuesday without providing further details about the specific issues involved.

Manifold held the position for only eight months, having been appointed to BP's board in October 2025. His removal represents a significant leadership shake-up at the FTSE 100 company during a period of strategic transformation. The announcement triggered a sharp market reaction, with BP shares initially falling 9% before recovering slightly to a 6% decline, making it the FTSE's biggest faller on the day.

Amanda Blanc, the senior independent director at BP and chief executive of insurance company Aviva, acknowledged Manifold's contributions while explaining the board's decision. "Albert has helped bring a welcome focus and pace to BP's transformation. However, the board has been surprised and disappointed to learn of governance oversight and conduct issues it deems unacceptable and has taken decisive action," she said.

During his tenure, Manifold oversaw significant changes to BP's strategic direction. He rapidly removed Murray Auchincloss as chief executive after less than two years in the role and appointed Meg O'Neill to lead the company in December. His appointment had been tasked with refocusing the oil company on fossil fuel extraction while moving away from renewable energy investments.

Manifold's departure marks the second instance in three years of a senior BP leader losing their position over conduct-related matters. In September 2023, Bernard Looney was forced out as chief executive for failing to disclose sexual relationships with colleagues. The company's board subsequently determined it had been misled, resulting in Looney being denied approximately 32 million pounds in pay and shares.

Ian Tyler has been appointed as interim chairman with immediate effect. Tyler brings extensive experience in corporate leadership, having previously served as chief executive of Balfour Beatty, a FTSE 250 infrastructure company. He currently serves as chair of Grafton Group, a FTSE 250 building supplies business, and holds a director position at Anglo American, a FTSE 100 mining company.

The leadership transition comes as BP navigates broader challenges facing the energy sector, including geopolitical tensions and commodity price volatility. The company has not provided information about the timeline for selecting a permanent chairman or whether it will pursue external candidates or consider internal appointments.

The removal of Manifold adds to the operational uncertainty facing BP as it implements its strategic shift back toward fossil fuel focus while managing investor confidence during a period of significant board changes.

NASA announced it has added missions to SpaceX's commercial crew contract because Boeing remains unable to certify its Starliner spacecraft for regular astronaut flights. The space agency did not disclose how many additional missions SpaceX received or provide a timeline for when Boeing might complete its certification process.

SpaceX has been NASA's primary crew transportation provider to the International Space Station since 2020, when its Crew Dragon spacecraft began regular missions. Boeing's Starliner program has faced repeated technical challenges and delays over several years, preventing it from achieving the operational status NASA originally envisioned when it awarded contracts to both companies.

The decision reflects NASA's need to maintain consistent crew rotation schedules for the International Space Station while Boeing works through its certification issues. By expanding SpaceX's contract, the agency ensures it can fulfill staffing requirements for the orbiting laboratory without gaps in coverage.

NASA initially selected both SpaceX and Boeing in 2014 under its Commercial Crew Program, intending to have two independent systems for transporting astronauts to low Earth orbit. The dual-provider approach aimed to ensure redundancy and prevent reliance on a single company for critical crew transportation.

Boeing conducted uncrewed and crewed test flights of Starliner, but the spacecraft has not yet been cleared for regular operational missions. The company has not provided a public estimate for when certification might be completed.

SpaceX has now conducted multiple crew rotation missions for NASA, establishing a track record of successful flights. The Crew Dragon spacecraft launches atop a Falcon 9 rocket and can carry up to seven passengers, though NASA missions typically fly four astronauts at a time.

The contract modification allows NASA to maintain its International Space Station crew schedules while Boeing continues development work. The space agency has not indicated whether it plans to continue pursuing dual providers or how long it will wait for Boeing to achieve certification.

Neither NASA nor Boeing released statements detailing specific technical issues preventing certification or outlining steps needed to resolve them. The additional SpaceX missions will support ongoing International Space Station operations and research activities.

After Cleveland held a 22-point lead in the fourth quarter of Game 1, Jalen Brunson led New York to a thrilling comeback victory in overtime. Brunson was named Eastern Conference Finals MVP after averaging 25.5 points and 7.8 assists across the series. Following Monday night's victory, the guard acknowledged his teammates' contributions. "It means a lot to get to the finals, but I wouldn't be here without my teammates, my coach, this staff, the fanbase. Without them none of this is possible," Brunson said.

Karl-Anthony Towns led the way in the clinching game with 19 points and 14 rebounds. The Knicks essentially put the game away with a devastating 20-0 run at the end of the first quarter and into the second quarter. Despite putting up 31 points, Cleveland's Donovan Mitchell could not spark a serious comeback.

The Knicks' path to the Finals included a sweep of the Philadelphia 76ers in the Eastern Conference semifinals and a 4-2 victory over the Atlanta Hawks in the first round. Their dominant postseason performance has generated significant excitement in New York, with high-profile fans including Spike Lee, Tracy Morgan, and Timothée Chalamet traveling to Cleveland to witness the clinching victory.

"Knicks fans are a specific species of human that should be studied, they're crazy," said guard Landry Shamet. "They fly out to Cleveland on a Monday, they're everywhere. Everywhere you walk in New York City that's what you hear, the buzz is unbelievable."

Cleveland coach Kenny Atkinson acknowledged his team's shortcomings. "They're playing better basketball. You got to give them credit. They're on a heater," he said.

The Knicks pursue their first NBA championship since 1973. The first home game of the NBA Finals will take place June 8, with courtside seats already commanding prices of up to 105,000 dollars on resale websites.

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily stopped removing detainees to the Democratic Republic of Congo amid a widening Ebola outbreak in the country, according to reporting by Politico. The pause comes as public health concerns mount about potential exposure to the virus during deportation operations.

However, experts question whether the temporary measure will meaningfully help prevent disease spread. The decision also leaves at least one woman in legal limbo. Officials moved her to Kinshasa, the capital of the DRC, but now refuse to return her despite a judge's order for her return, citing the Ebola travel restrictions.

The pause represents a shift in the Trump administration's aggressive deportation agenda, which has seen a significant expansion since the president took office. According to monitoring by human rights groups, ICE flights increased by 84 percent from 2024 to 2025. The administration has been conducting widespread removal operations as part of its hardline immigration enforcement strategy.

Ebola is a severe viral disease that spreads through direct contact with blood or bodily fluids from infected people or animals. The Democratic Republic of Congo has experienced multiple outbreaks in recent years, and the country's health infrastructure has been strained by recurring epidemic events, making containment efforts difficult.

ICE officials face direct exposure risks during deportation flights, as they accompany detainees throughout the removal process. The close quarters of aircraft and extended duration of international flights create conditions where disease transmission becomes more likely if infected passengers are onboard.

The administration has not announced how long the pause will remain in effect or what criteria will determine when flights might resume. The decision affects an unknown number of Congolese nationals who were scheduled for deportation.

The pause also comes amid growing scrutiny of the Trump administration's deportation flight operations. A group of 40 House Democrats recently described "grave concerns" about what they called a secretive program and demanded transparency from the Federal Aviation Administration. The lawmakers referenced investigations revealing that the Trump administration transported detained immigrants in ways that routinely violated their constitutional rights.

In a letter to the FAA administrator, the Democrats highlighted allegations that individuals were placed on flights without notice to counsel or family members, effectively disappearing from public view. They also raised concerns about reports of abuse and rights violations at detention facilities that serve as central nodes in the deportation program.

The lawmakers asked the FAA to provide detailed reports on all ICE air operations since Trump took office, including flight origin and destination data and passenger numbers. They also requested information on how the agency assesses humanitarian conditions on ICE flights, including the controversial use of full body restraints during deportations.

The Trump administration has previously defended its deportation operations and restraint practices as necessary for safety and consistent with legal standards.
