1:42 PM ET

A federal panel of three judges blocked Alabama from using a Republican-friendly congressional map in this year's midterm elections, ruling that the map was intentionally drawn to discriminate against Black voters.

The decision prevents Alabama from implementing a redistricting plan that lawmakers passed in 2023. This same court had previously found that plan violated voting rights protections. Alabama was then ordered to adopt a map with two majority-Black districts, both of which elected Democratic representatives.

The ruling came after the U.S. Supreme Court significantly weakened a major provision of the Voting Rights Act in a case called Louisiana v Callais in April. Following that decision, Alabama took the unusual step of moving up its congressional primary and attempted to use the 2023 map for the upcoming elections.

The three-judge panel, which included judges appointed by both Bill Clinton and Donald Trump, found that Alabama's legislature deliberately rejected requirements to provide fair representation to Black voters. "When the Legislature enacted the 2023 Plan, it made a calculated, purposeful decision to refuse to provide the remedy for discriminatory vote dilution that our order required," the judges wrote.

The panel stated that lawmakers knew a map without an additional majority-Black district would dilute Black voters' ability to participate in the political process, and they intentionally enacted that plan anyway. The court determined that Alabama specifically employed mechanisms to prevent Black voters in the Black Belt and Gulf Coast communities from electing representatives of their choice.

Davin Rosborough, deputy director of the voting rights project at the American Civil Liberties Union, which represented some plaintiffs, said the decision confirmed what voters already knew. "The Alabama legislature's repeated refusal to provide Black Alabamians with fair representation in Congress is racial discrimination," Rosborough said.

Alabama Attorney General Steve Marshall, a Republican, announced plans to appeal the decision to the U.S. Supreme Court. "I am disappointed, but not at all surprised, that the three-judge panel has again struck down Alabama's congressional map," Marshall said in a statement.

The Alabama case represents the first major test of whether the Supreme Court's weakened Voting Rights Act still allows challenges to maps drawn with intentional discrimination. Justice Samuel Alito stated in his majority opinion that such maps could still be challenged, though proving discriminatory intent remains an extremely high legal standard.

Alabama's redistricting effort was part of a broader Republican strategy across the South following the Supreme Court's April decision. Tennessee has already implemented a new map that eliminated a majority-Black congressional district in Memphis, while Louisiana and South Carolina are considering similar changes. These efforts have drawn widespread condemnation from Black leaders and civil rights groups.

Nvidia has officially discontinued its GeForce Control Panel, ending support for the graphics management tool after two decades. The company confirmed the retirement with the release of GeForce Graphics Drivers 610.47 WHQL, which no longer includes the legacy application.

The Control Panel was a foundational tool for PC gamers and graphics professionals, offering users the ability to adjust graphics settings, manage display configurations, and fine-tune GPU performance. Since its launch in the mid-2000s, it remained a standard utility across the industry.

Nvidia announced its plan to replace the Control Panel more than two years ago. The company has gradually migrated functionality from the old interface to its newer Nvidia App, consolidating features that were previously divided between the Control Panel and GeForce Experience. The unified platform now handles driver updates, game optimization, and system settings through a single interface.

The 610.47 WHQL driver release marks the first update that completely removes Control Panel support. Earlier driver versions had encouraged users to transition to the new application, but this update finalizes the shift. Users running older driver versions can still access the legacy tool, though it will receive no further updates or maintenance.

The transition reflects broader changes in how graphics card manufacturers approach software distribution. Modern GPU management tools increasingly prioritize streamlined interfaces and automatic optimization over manual configuration options.

For those who built workflows around the Control Panel over its 20-year history, the transition requires adaptation. Nvidia has published documentation to help users locate equivalent settings and features within the Nvidia App.

The retirement of the Control Panel represents a significant milestone in GPU software evolution. The application shaped PC gaming configuration practices for an entire generation of users and ranked among the most durable tools in the graphics card industry.

Nvidia's decision to consolidate software tools under a single application aligns with industry trends toward simplified user experiences. The Nvidia App serves as the company's primary vehicle for delivering driver updates and GPU management functionality to its user base going forward.

This discontinuation may affect enthusiasts and professionals who relied on specific Control Panel features for their particular workflows. However, the company's documentation efforts aim to minimize disruption during the transition period.

The shift to the Nvidia App represents the company's commitment to modernizing its software infrastructure while maintaining access to essential graphics management tools. As older driver versions eventually phase out of common use, the Control Panel will become increasingly obsolete in practice.

1:28 PM ET

Sonny Rollins, widely regarded as one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at age 95. His death was announced Monday on his website and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He passed away at his home in Woodstock, New York, on Monday afternoon. No cause of death was disclosed.

Rollins was born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930 and grew up in Harlem, earning the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began playing saxophone at age seven, inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. By high school, he was performing with musicians who would later become major jazz figures, including Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew.

With more than 60 albums released from the late 1940s onward, Rollins became one of the last surviving stars of the bebop generation. He collaborated with Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane and other jazz icons, helping transform jazz from primarily dance and ballad music into a strikingly expressive art form. He was known for taking jazz standards and his own compositions, then reshaping them through extended, innovative improvisations.

Rollins faced personal struggles early in his career. He battled heroin addiction and committed armed robbery in 1950 to fund his habit. After spending 10 months in jail on Rikers Island, he successfully overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955. This period marked the beginning of remarkable creative output. He released his debut album as bandleader in 1953 and recorded 17 more albums by decade's end, including landmarks like "Saxophone Colossus" (1956), featuring his signature tune "St Thomas," and "Freedom Suite" (1958).

In 1959, Rollins stepped back from recording and performing for three years, practicing for up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway. He returned in 1962 with "The Bridge." He took another sabbatical between 1969 and 1971 to study yoga, philosophy and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout his career, Rollins remained restless in his musical exploration, moving between avant-garde and fusion styles. He worked with Latin American music, composed and performed the soundtrack to the 1966 film "Alfie," and later incorporated funk and calypso into his work. He even contributed uncredited saxophone to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album "Tattoo You."

Beyond music, Rollins became an environmental advocate, performing benefit concerts and releasing the album "Global Warming" in 1998 to raise awareness about climate change. Rollins was married twice, to Dawn Finney briefly in 1957 and to Lucille Pearson from 1965 until her death in 2004.

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily halted the removal of detainees to the Democratic Republic of Congo as the country faces a widening Ebola outbreak, according to reporting by Politico. However, the decision comes with a significant caveat: the administration will not bring back individuals who have already been deported to the region.

The pause reflects operational concerns about protecting Immigration and Customs Enforcement personnel from exposure to the virus. Ebola is a severe viral disease that spreads through direct contact with blood and other bodily fluids from infected individuals. The disease is not airborne, but direct contact with sick people poses serious transmission risks.

The temporary nature of the pause means deportation flights could resume once conditions change, though the administration has not specified what benchmarks would trigger their resumption. The decision applies specifically to the Democratic Republic of Congo and does not affect deportation operations to other countries.

The Democratic Republic of Congo has experienced multiple Ebola outbreaks in recent years. Between 2018 and 2020, a major epidemic killed more than 2,200 people. Health authorities in the country have developed experience responding to the virus, though controlling outbreaks remains difficult in conflict-affected regions with limited infrastructure.

Public health experts have noted that while Ebola poses serious risks in outbreak zones, transmission requires direct contact with blood or bodily fluids from someone who is actively sick. The virus does not spread through casual contact, making some transmission scenarios less likely than others.

The decision reflects an unusual adjustment for an administration that has prioritized aggressive immigration enforcement. The Trump administration expanded ICE deportation flights significantly in 2025, with monitoring by human rights groups tracking an 84 percent increase from 2024. These operations have faced scrutiny from Democratic lawmakers, who have raised concerns about transparency and conditions on ICE charter jets.

The pause creates complications for at least one case, in which a woman was moved to Kinshasa, the capital of the Democratic Republic of Congo. A judge ordered her return, but administration officials now say they will not bring her back due to the Ebola travel restrictions, leaving her in a state of legal limbo.

The decision highlights tensions between the administration's enforcement priorities and public health concerns. While protecting federal employees from disease exposure is a legitimate operational consideration, the refusal to return individuals already deported suggests the pause applies selectively. The measure aims to prevent ICE personnel from entering disease-affected areas, but does not extend the same protection to deportees already in the region.

Rollins was born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930 and grew up in Harlem, earning the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began playing saxophone at age seven, inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. By high school, he was performing with musicians who would later become major jazz figures, including Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew.

With more than 60 albums released from the late 1940s onward, Rollins became one of the last surviving stars of the bebop generation. He collaborated with Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane and other jazz icons, helping transform jazz from primarily dance and ballad music into a strikingly expressive art form. He was known for taking jazz standards and his own compositions, then reshaping them through extended, innovative improvisations.

Rollins faced personal struggles early in his career. He battled heroin addiction and committed armed robbery in 1950 to fund his habit. After spending 10 months in jail on Rikers Island, he successfully overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955. This period marked the beginning of remarkable creative output. He released his debut album as bandleader in 1953 and recorded 17 more albums by decade's end, including landmarks like "Saxophone Colossus" (1956), featuring his signature tune "St Thomas," and "Freedom Suite" (1958).

In 1959, Rollins stepped back from recording and performing for three years, practicing for up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway. He returned in 1962 with "The Bridge." He took another sabbatical between 1969 and 1971 to study yoga, philosophy and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout his career, Rollins remained restless in his musical exploration, moving between avant-garde and fusion styles. He worked with Latin American music, composed and performed the soundtrack to the 1966 film "Alfie," and later incorporated funk and calypso into his work. He even contributed uncredited saxophone to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album "Tattoo You."

Beyond music, Rollins became an environmental advocate, performing benefit concerts and releasing the album "Global Warming" in 1998 to raise awareness about climate change. Rollins was married twice, to Dawn Finney briefly in 1957 and to Lucille Pearson from 1965 until her death in 2004.
