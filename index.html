Today, 6:00 PM ET

Iran has published a map asserting that its armed forces oversee more than 22,000 square kilometers of the Strait of Hormuz, escalating its claims over the strategic waterway through which nearly one-third of all seaborne oil passes.

The move represents a significant assertion of authority over a chokepoint critical to global energy supplies. The strait, at its narrowest point just 21 miles wide, connects the Persian Gulf with the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea. Approximately 21 million barrels of oil pass through it daily, making it one of the world's most important maritime routes.

Iran's claim comes amid ongoing tensions in the region and raises questions about freedom of navigation through international waters. The strait is governed by the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, which allows for innocent passage through territorial waters.

The published map marks a formal escalation in Iran's longstanding position regarding its influence over the waterway. Iranian officials have previously threatened to close the strait during periods of heightened conflict, though such closures would also severely impact Iran's own oil exports.

The claim affects shipping routes used by tankers from Gulf states including Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Kuwait, and Iraq. Any disruption to passage through the strait would have immediate effects on global oil prices and supply chains.

International maritime law typically recognizes a 12-nautical-mile territorial sea limit for coastal nations. Beyond that lies the contiguous zone and then international waters where freedom of navigation applies. Iran's specific legal basis for the published oversight area remains unclear.

The United States Fifth Fleet, based in Bahrain, maintains a presence in the region partly to ensure freedom of navigation through the strait. The US and its allies have consistently maintained that the waterway must remain open to international shipping.

Neither the US nor Gulf Arab states have yet issued formal responses to Iran's published map. The claim adds another layer of complexity to already strained relations between Iran and Western nations, particularly regarding Iran's nuclear program and regional influence.

Energy markets will likely monitor developments closely. Any perceived threat to shipping through the strait typically results in upward pressure on oil prices, affecting global economic conditions.

Kyle Busch, a two-time NASCAR Cup Series champion and the winningest driver across NASCAR's three national series, died Thursday at age 41 after what his family described as a severe illness.

A joint statement from the Busch family, Richard Childress Racing, and NASCAR confirmed his death but provided no additional details about the nature of his illness or how long he had been sick.

Busch won 63 Cup Series races during his career, along with 102 Xfinity Series victories and 66 Truck Series wins, for a total of 231 wins across NASCAR's top three divisions. No other driver in the sport's history has matched that total.

He claimed Cup Series championships in 2015 and 2019, both while driving for Joe Gibbs Racing. Busch joined Richard Childress Racing in 2023 after spending 15 seasons with Gibbs.

Known for his aggressive driving style and outspoken personality, Busch was one of NASCAR's most polarizing figures. He attracted devoted fans and vocal critics in equal measure throughout his two-decade career.

Busch began racing professionally in the Truck Series in 2001 at age 16. He moved to the Cup Series in 2005 and earned his first victory that same year at Auto Club Speedway in California.

His 2015 championship run was particularly remarkable. Busch missed the first 11 races of the season after suffering a compound fracture of his right leg and a broken left foot in a crash at Daytona. He returned to win five races and claimed the title in just 25 starts.

Busch is survived by his wife Samantha and their two children. NASCAR has not yet announced plans for memorial services or tributes.

The racing community responded with an outpouring of grief on social media Thursday evening, with drivers, team owners, and officials sharing memories and condolences. NASCAR issued a statement calling Busch "one of the most talented and accomplished drivers in our sport's history."

Busch's death marks the first loss of an active Cup Series driver since Dale Earnhardt died in a crash during the 2001 Daytona 500.

SpaceX has disclosed its financial information publicly for the first time in a filing that pitches an ambitious plan to build data centers in orbit. The company, which has remained private since its founding in 2002, opened its books as part of what appears to be preparation for a public offering.

The filing describes what SpaceX calls "the largest TAM in human history," referring to total addressable market. The company's new vision centers on using its Starlink satellite network and launch capabilities to create orbital computing infrastructure specifically designed for artificial intelligence processing.

The move comes as SpaceX's AI chatbot Grok continues to lag behind competing services from OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic. By positioning itself as infrastructure rather than just another AI service provider, SpaceX is betting it can compete with major tech companies on different terms. Orbital data centers would theoretically offer advantages in cooling, power from solar arrays, and reduced latency for global communications.

SpaceX has not released specifics on timeline, costs, or technical specifications for the proposed orbital data centers. The company currently operates the world's largest satellite constellation through Starlink, with over 5,000 satellites providing internet service. Building computing infrastructure in space would represent a significant expansion beyond communications.

The financial disclosure marks a dramatic shift in transparency for a company known for secrecy. SpaceX has previously shared limited information about revenues, which are primarily generated through launch services for government and commercial customers, and growing Starlink subscriber fees.

The orbital data center concept faces substantial technical and economic hurdles. Launching computing hardware into space is expensive, and maintaining or upgrading equipment in orbit presents challenges that ground-based facilities do not face. Heat dissipation in the vacuum of space, radiation hardening for electronics, and the cost per computational unit all require solutions that do not yet exist at scale.

Industry observers note that the filing's timing coincides with increased competition in the commercial space sector and AI infrastructure. Amazon, through its Project Kuiper satellite network, is also building space-based communications infrastructure, while tech giants continue to invest billions in terrestrial data centers for AI.

Today, 6:00 PM ET

A massive landslide in a remote Alaska fjord triggered a tsunami wave that reached 650 feet high, making it the second largest megatsunami ever recorded by modern science. The event occurred in Dickson Fjord in September 2023 when approximately 25 million cubic meters of rock collapsed into the water.

New research published by an international team of scientists reveals the collapse was caused by the thinning of a glacier at the base of a steep mountain slope. As the glacier melted, it no longer provided sufficient support for the rock face above, leading to catastrophic failure.

The initial wave was taller than a 60-story building. After the impact, water continued to slosh back and forth in the narrow fjord for nine days, creating seismic signals detected by monitoring stations around the world. Scientists were initially puzzled by the unusual seismic readings, which persisted far longer than typical earthquake signatures.

No deaths resulted from the tsunami because the fjord is uninhabited, but the wave destroyed monitoring equipment and research infrastructure on the fjord's shores. Had it occurred in a populated area, the consequences would have been catastrophic.

The largest recorded megatsunami occurred in 1958 at Lituya Bay, also in Alaska, when a landslide created a wave that reached 1,720 feet. That event killed five people.

Researchers warn that climate change is increasing the frequency of such events. As glaciers continue to retreat globally due to warming temperatures, mountain slopes that have been supported by ice for thousands of years are becoming unstable. Alaska, Greenland, and other regions with glaciated mountains face elevated risk.

The study used a combination of seismic data, satellite imagery, and computer modeling to reconstruct what happened. Scientists were able to determine the exact volume of rock that fell, the height of the wave, and how long water oscillated in the fjord.

This event adds to growing evidence that glacier loss poses risks beyond sea level rise. Unstable slopes near glaciers threaten coastal communities, shipping routes, and infrastructure in polar and mountain regions. Scientists emphasize the need for improved monitoring of glacier-adjacent slopes to identify areas at risk of collapse.
