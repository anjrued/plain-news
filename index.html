Yesterday, 11:24 PM ET

Firefighters in Orange County, California have successfully eliminated the threat of an explosion at a chemical storage tank that forced the evacuation of 40,000 residents across six cities, officials announced on Monday.

The crisis began Thursday when a storage tank at a GKN Aerospace facility in Garden Grove started leaking methyl methacrylate, a highly flammable chemical used in manufacturing resins and plastics. Damage to a valve created additional problems that prevented crews from fully containing the situation.

By Friday, the situation had escalated dramatically. Orange County Fire Authority Division Chief Craig Covey told reporters that the tank faced two possible outcomes: either it would fail and spill approximately 6,000 to 7,000 gallons of toxic chemicals into the parking lot, or it would experience thermal runaway and explode, potentially triggering explosions in nearby tanks containing fuel and other hazardous materials.

"This thing is going to fail, and we don't know when," Covey said at the time. "We're doing our best to figure out when or how we can prevent it."

Evacuation orders covered residents in Garden Grove, Cypress, Stanton, Anaheim, Buena Park and Westminster. The facility sits less than a mile from Disneyland, though the theme parks were not placed under evacuation orders. Emergency crews established containment barriers with sandbags to prevent any chemical spill from reaching storm drains, creeks or the ocean.

County Health Officer Dr Regina Chinsio-Kwong warned that heating methyl methacrylate releases harmful vapor that can cause respiratory problems, eye irritation, nausea and headaches.

Over the weekend, the damaged tank developed a crack, which authorities believed would relieve pressure and reduce explosion risk. Firefighters then launched an intensive overnight cooling and monitoring operation that successfully stabilized the tank and eliminated the threat of a catastrophic explosion.

The operation marked the resolution of what had been one of southern California's most serious recent public safety emergencies. Residents who had fled their homes were able to return, though the incident underscored the dangers posed by industrial chemical facilities near densely populated areas.

GKN Aerospace is a division of a British corporation that manufactures aircraft engines and other aviation components. The company's facility in Garden Grove had stored the hazardous methyl methacrylate in conditions that eventually led to the equipment failure and the subsequent emergency response.

The successful completion of the overnight stabilization effort brought relief to tens of thousands of residents whose lives had been disrupted by the evacuation orders.

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his official website on Monday afternoon at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in Harlem in 1930, he earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began playing saxophone at age seven, inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. His early bands included future stars Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew, setting the stage for a career that would reshape modern jazz.

Rollins became a central figure in the bebop movement alongside Miles Davis and Charlie Parker. Davis himself recognized Rollins' impact, noting he quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" known for his aggressive, innovative approach and constant stream of fresh ideas. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis later called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong.

His early career was interrupted by heroin addiction. In 1950, he committed armed robbery to support his habit, which he later described as a period when he was "really a despicable character." After serving ten months in jail on Rikers Island, he entered a rehabilitation program in 1955 and successfully overcame his addiction.

This recovery sparked remarkable creative output. Rollins released his debut album as a bandleader in 1953 and recorded 17 more albums by decade's end. His 1956 album "Saxophone Colossus," featuring the signature tune "St Thomas" with its calypso influences, became essential listening for understanding post-war jazz development. He followed with "Way Out West" in 1957, exploring a piano-free "strolling" style, and "Freedom Suite" in 1958, featuring a 20-minute title track that became an elegant argument for freedom during the civil rights movement.

Rollins was known for taking extended breaks to refine his artistry. In 1959, he stepped away from recording and performing for three years, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway to avoid disturbing neighbors. This period inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge." He took another sabbatical between 1969 and 1971, traveling to an Indian ashram to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation.

Throughout his career, Rollins continued evolving his sound, incorporating Latin American music, funk, calypso, and R&B influences. He remained active into his 80s, maintaining a rigorous touring schedule and releasing new recordings. He also championed environmental causes, releasing the 1998 album "Global Warming."

When President Barack Obama presented him with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, he credited Rollins with inspiring him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken." Despite numerous honors, Rollins remained focused on the music itself and the constant pursuit of artistic growth.

The decision reflects concerns about protecting Immigration and Customs Enforcement personnel and preventing the disease from reaching the United States. The pause specifically affects ICE r...

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily halted deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo as an Ebola outbreak spreads across the country, according to reporting by Politico. The decision reflects concerns about protecting Immigration and Customs Enforcement personnel and preventing the disease from reaching the United States.

The pause specifically affects ICE removal operations to Congo, though the administration has stated it will not return detainees who have already been deported to third countries in the disease-stricken region. At least one woman remains in limbo after officials moved her to Kinshasa, the capital of the DRC, and subsequently refused to bring her back due to the Ebola travel restrictions, despite a judge's order for her return.

Public health experts have questioned whether the flight pause will meaningfully help prevent disease transmission. Ebola spreads through direct contact with bodily fluids of infected individuals and does not transmit through the air, meaning the virus does not currently pose an immediate threat to most Americans.

The decision represents a rare instance where health considerations have interrupted deportation operations under the Trump administration, which has made accelerating removals a central priority. The administration expanded deportation flights to numerous countries in 2025, with human rights groups monitoring an 84 percent increase in ICE flights compared to 2024.

The pause comes amid broader scrutiny of the Trump administration's deportation program. Forty House Democrats recently sent a letter to the Federal Aviation Administration administrator expressing "grave concerns" about what they describe as secretive deportation operations. The lawmakers cited credible reports indicating that individuals have been placed on flights without notice to counsel or family members, effectively disappearing from public view when flights use systems that shield them from public tracking.

The letter references an investigation revealing that the Trump administration transported detained immigrants in ways that routinely violated their constitutional rights. The reporting identified allegations of abuse and rights violations at a private detention center in Alexandria, Louisiana, described as a central node in the deportation program.

The lawmakers asked the FAA to provide detailed reports of all ICE air operations since Trump took office, including flight origin and destination data and passenger numbers. They also requested information about how the agency assesses humanitarian conditions on ICE flights, particularly regarding the controversial use of full body restraints during deportation flights.

The Trump administration has previously defended its detention and transfer practices, describing them as standard protocols designed to ensure safety and arguing they comply with legal standards. The administration stated that claims of hidden or weaponized flights are categorically false.

The administration has not announced when deportation flights to Congo might resume. Such decisions typically depend on assessments of health risks and coordination with public health officials.

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his official website on Monday afternoon at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in Harlem in 1930, he earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began playing saxophone at age seven, inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. His early bands included future stars Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew, setting the stage for a career that would reshape modern jazz.

Rollins became a central figure in the bebop movement alongside Miles Davis and Charlie Parker. Davis himself recognized Rollins' impact, noting he quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" known for his aggressive, innovative approach and constant stream of fresh ideas. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis later called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong.

His early career was interrupted by heroin addiction. In 1950, he committed armed robbery to support his habit, which he later described as a period when he was "really a despicable character." After serving ten months in jail on Rikers Island, he entered a rehabilitation program in 1955 and successfully overcame his addiction.

This recovery sparked remarkable creative output. Rollins released his debut album as a bandleader in 1953 and recorded 17 more albums by decade's end. His 1956 album "Saxophone Colossus," featuring the signature tune "St Thomas" with its calypso influences, became essential listening for understanding post-war jazz development. He followed with "Way Out West" in 1957, exploring a piano-free "strolling" style, and "Freedom Suite" in 1958, featuring a 20-minute title track that became an elegant argument for freedom during the civil rights movement.

Rollins was known for taking extended breaks to refine his artistry. In 1959, he stepped away from recording and performing for three years, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway to avoid disturbing neighbors. This period inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge." He took another sabbatical between 1969 and 1971, traveling to an Indian ashram to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation.

Throughout his career, Rollins continued evolving his sound, incorporating Latin American music, funk, calypso, and R&B influences. He remained active into his 80s, maintaining a rigorous touring schedule and releasing new recordings. He also championed environmental causes, releasing the 1998 album "Global Warming."

When President Barack Obama presented him with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, he credited Rollins with inspiring him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken." Despite numerous honors, Rollins remained focused on the music itself and the constant pursuit of artistic growth.
