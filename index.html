Today, 10:00 PM ET

The United States government will invest $2 billion in quantum computing companies including IBM, marking one of the largest federal commitments to the emerging technology sector. The funding aims to accelerate development of quantum systems that could revolutionize computing power and maintain American competitiveness against China and other nations racing to build functional quantum computers.

Quantum computers use the principles of quantum mechanics to process information in fundamentally different ways than traditional computers. While still largely experimental, they promise to solve certain complex problems in minutes that would take conventional supercomputers thousands of years. Applications span drug discovery, financial modeling, cryptography, and artificial intelligence.

The investment comes as the quantum computing industry reaches a critical development phase. IBM and other major players have built increasingly stable quantum processors, but the technology remains expensive and difficult to scale. The federal funding will support both hardware development and the creation of quantum algorithms and software.

China has invested heavily in quantum research over the past decade, building dedicated laboratories and launching quantum communication satellites. The US investment reflects growing concern about falling behind in a technology with significant national security implications. Quantum computers could eventually break current encryption methods used to protect government and financial systems.

IBM will receive a substantial portion of the funding to expand its quantum computing facilities and research programs. The company has positioned itself as a leader in making quantum systems accessible through cloud computing platforms, allowing researchers and businesses to experiment with the technology without building their own machines.

Other recipients include smaller quantum computing firms developing different technological approaches. Some focus on trapped ion systems, while others work on superconducting circuits or photonic quantum processors. The government funding will support this diversity of methods as scientists determine which architectures scale most effectively.

The $2 billion represents a multi-year commitment rather than immediate spending. Funds will be distributed through grants and contracts as companies meet development milestones. Industry analysts view the investment as essential for preventing a brain drain of quantum scientists to better-funded foreign programs and for building the manufacturing infrastructure needed to produce quantum systems at scale.

European-Chinese Satellite Launches to Study Earth's Magnetic Shield

Today, 10:00 PM ET

A joint European-Chinese satellite launched Thursday on a Vega-C rocket to study Earth's magnetic field, a protective shield that guards the planet from harmful solar radiation. The mission could reshape scientific understanding of how this invisible barrier works and why it matters for life on Earth.

The satellite, called SMILE (Solar wind Magnetosphere Ionosphere Link Explorer), is designed to observe the interaction between solar wind and Earth's magnetosphere. These interactions create phenomena like the Northern Lights but also pose risks to satellites, power grids, and communications systems.

Earth's magnetic field acts as a buffer against charged particles streaming from the sun. When solar storms strike, they can compress and distort this field, sometimes allowing radiation to penetrate closer to the surface. Understanding these dynamics has become more urgent as society grows more dependent on satellite technology and electronic infrastructure vulnerable to space weather.

SMILE carries instruments built by both the European Space Agency and the Chinese Academy of Sciences. It will capture images of the magnetosphere's boundary and track how it responds to solar activity in real time. Previous missions have studied pieces of this system, but SMILE aims to provide a more complete picture of the entire interaction zone.

The satellite will orbit Earth in an elliptical path, swinging out far enough to observe the magnetosphere from outside while dipping close enough to sample conditions near the planet. This dual perspective should reveal patterns that ground-based observations and lower-orbit satellites cannot detect.

Scientists hope the data will improve forecasting of geomagnetic storms. These events can knock out power grids, disrupt GPS navigation, and damage satellites. A major storm in 1989 caused a nine-hour blackout across Quebec. As solar activity follows an 11-year cycle and is currently increasing toward its peak, better predictions could help operators protect critical systems.

The mission represents a rare example of sustained cooperation between European and Chinese space programs. Development began nearly a decade ago, with teams sharing technology and expertise despite broader geopolitical tensions.

SMILE is expected to operate for at least three years, collecting data through different phases of solar activity. Results will be shared openly with the international scientific community. The first images from orbit are anticipated within a few months after systems checks are complete.

Google has accidentally disclosed details of an unfixed security vulnerability in Chromium, the open-source browser engine that powers Chrome, Edge, and other major web browsers. The premature disclosure leaves millions of users potentially exposed until a patch becomes available.

The vulnerability was revealed when Google published information about the flaw before developers had completed a fix. Security researchers typically follow a coordinated disclosure process, where details remain private until patches are ready and distributed. This prevents attackers from exploiting known weaknesses while users remain unprotected.

Chromium serves as the foundation for the world's most popular web browsers. Google Chrome alone commands over 60 percent of the global browser market, while Microsoft Edge and other Chromium-based browsers account for additional millions of users. The scope of potential impact is substantial.

The nature of the specific vulnerability has not been fully detailed in available reports, likely to limit exploitation opportunities while fixes are being developed. However, the early disclosure creates a window where malicious actors could reverse-engineer the flaw and develop attacks before protective updates reach users.

This incident highlights the delicate balance security teams must maintain between transparency and protection. While public disclosure of vulnerabilities serves the broader security community by enabling independent verification and pressure for fixes, premature release can create more harm than good.

Google has acknowledged the accidental disclosure but has not provided a timeline for when a patch will be available. The company typically releases security updates on a regular schedule, but critical vulnerabilities can prompt expedited releases.

Browser makers downstream from Chromium, including Microsoft, Brave, Opera, and others, will need to incorporate Google's fix once it becomes available and push their own updates to users. This multi-step process means the window of vulnerability could extend beyond Google's initial patch.

Users cannot take direct action to protect themselves from this specific flaw beyond ensuring their browsers are set to update automatically. Security experts recommend users verify their browser settings allow automatic updates and restart browsers promptly when updates become available.

The incident underscores the complexity of managing security in widely-distributed open-source projects where coordination among multiple parties is essential but difficult to execute perfectly.
