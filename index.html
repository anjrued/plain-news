A few minutes ago

Firefighters in Orange County, California have eliminated the threat of a massive explosion from an overheating chemical tank, according to officials on Monday. However, thousands of residents remain displaced as crews continue managing the aftermath of the incident.

The chemical leak began Thursday afternoon at GKN Aerospace in Garden Grove, a facility that manufactures parts for commercial and military aircraft. The tank contained 6,000 to 7,000 gallons of methyl methacrylate, a highly flammable chemical used in plastic and resin production.

Officials initially warned of catastrophic risks. EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin told CNN that "the tank will fail," noting that the worst-case scenario would be an explosion triggering additional tank explosions in the surrounding area. This assessment prompted the evacuation of approximately 50,000 people from their homes as a precautionary measure.

The situation improved significantly over the weekend when firefighters discovered a potential crack in the tank late Saturday night. County Fire Chief TJ McGovern identified this development as potentially relieving pressure inside the tank, making an explosion less likely. Andrew Whelton, an engineering professor at Purdue University, explained the benefit: "Think of a soda can. If you leave it in a hot car it can explode. But if you put a hole in the can, the product is released and the can itself doesn't explode."

Firefighters had been continuously spraying the tank with water to cool the chemicals inside. Officials also added a neutralizing agent to a nearby tank on Friday to limit explosion risks. These cooling and monitoring efforts overnight allowed officials to declare the explosion threat eliminated by Monday.

Garden Grove Mayor Stephanie Klopfenstein described the briefing from the Orange County Fire Authority as "somewhat positive," reflecting the progress made in stabilizing the situation.

However, experts cautioned that dangers remained. Faisal Khan, head of the chemical engineering department at Texas A&M University, told the Associated Press that a reaction could still be occurring inside the tank despite cooling efforts. "Cooling is happening on the surface of the tank while runaway reaction may be occurring deep inside the tank," he said.

Air quality monitoring provided reassurance to residents. Officials detected no pollutants in the surrounding area. Jason Low from the South Coast Air Quality Management District reported that measurements showed "levels are completely normal."

A new, reduced evacuation zone has been established, with approximately 16,000 people still unable to return home. Officials have not provided a timeline for when all residents will be allowed to return.

Exposure to methyl methacrylate can cause serious respiratory problems, neurological damage, and irritation to skin, eyes and throat. However, Orange County health officials noted the chemical is easy to smell, meaning residents may notice it over a wide area without being harmed.

At least two lawsuits have been filed against GKN Aerospace by Garden Grove residents seeking damages for evacuation orders, exposure concerns, and property interference.

Google Removes Health Feature from AI Search as Safety Concerns Mount

Google has quietly discontinued "What People Suggest," an artificial intelligence feature that compiled crowdsourced health advice from internet users. The company initially launched the feature to showcase how AI could improve health outcomes globally, but has now scrapped it as part of what Google called a "broader simplification" of its search results page.

Three people familiar with the decision confirmed the removal to the Guardian. A Google spokesperson stated the feature had been turned down months ago and said the decision had nothing to do with the quality or safety of the tool itself.

The discontinuation comes as Google faces growing scrutiny over how it uses AI to deliver health information to millions of users. In January, an investigation found that Google's AI Overviews—AI-generated summaries displayed above traditional search results—contained false and misleading health information that put users at risk of harm. These summaries reach approximately 2 billion people monthly on the world's most visited website.

Google initially attempted to minimize concerns about the investigation's findings, arguing that its AI Overviews linked to reputable sources and recommended seeking expert advice. However, the company subsequently removed AI Overviews for some medical queries, though not all.

"What People Suggest" was unveiled in March at Google's "The Check Up" event in New York. The feature was designed to organize discussions from online communities into themes, allowing users to learn from people with similar medical experiences. Karen DeSalvo, then Google's chief health officer, wrote that while people value expert medical information, they also benefit from hearing real-world experiences from others facing similar conditions.

The feature was initially available only on mobile devices in the US. When asked where the company publicly announced the feature's removal, a Google spokesperson pointed to a blog post from November written by John Mueller, a search advocate at Google Switzerland. However, that post contains no mention of "What People Suggest."

Despite removing the feature, Google maintains that it continues to help users find reliable health information from multiple sources, including online forums that provide first-person perspectives. A Google spokesperson said these forums remain "incredibly useful" to people seeking health information.

Google's next "The Check Up" event is scheduled for this week, where Chief Health Officer Michael Howell and other staff will discuss new AI research and technological innovations aimed at addressing major health challenges.

The removal of "What People Suggest" reflects the challenges Google faces as it integrates AI more deeply into search while managing concerns about accuracy and safety in sensitive areas like healthcare.

9:41 PM ET

Sonny Rollins, one of the most influential saxophonists in jazz history, has died at age 95. The announcement was made on his website on Monday, described as coming "with deep sorrow and profound love." His publicist confirmed the news. Rollins died at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he grew up in Harlem and earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began learning saxophone at age seven, inspired by his siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. By high school, he was already playing alongside future stars including Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew.

Rollins became a central figure in the bebop movement that transformed jazz in the 1940s and 1950s. Miles Davis wrote about how Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" known for his aggressive, innovative approach and constant stream of fresh musical ideas.

His 1956 album "Saxophone Colossus" stands as a defining recording in modern jazz. The album featured "St. Thomas," his signature composition that blended Caribbean rhythms with bebop complexity and became a jazz standard. The song was named after his mother's birthplace in the Caribbean.

Rollins struggled with heroin addiction in the early 1950s. After committing armed robbery to support his habit, he was jailed for ten months on Rikers Island but successfully overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955. This turning point sparked extraordinary creative productivity. He released his debut album as bandleader in 1953 and recorded 17 more albums by decade's end, including "Way Out West" in 1957 and "Freedom Suite" in 1958.

Throughout his career, Rollins periodically withdrew from public performance to refine his craft. Starting in 1959, he took a three-year break from recording and performing, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the pedestrian walkway of the Williamsburg Bridge in New York. This practice ground inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge." Between 1969 and 1971, he took another sabbatical to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Rollins continued evolving his sound across decades, exploring Latin music, free improvisation, funk, and calypso. He composed and performed music for the 1966 film "Alfie" and later contributed uncredited saxophone to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album "Tattoo You." In his later years, he performed benefit concerts addressing climate change and released the 1998 album "Global Warming."

He was married to Lucille Pearson from 1965 until her death in 2004. Rollins received numerous honors including a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award, the National Medal of the Arts, and Kennedy Center recognition. President Barack Obama presented him with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011.

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily halted deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo as an Ebola outbreak spreads across the country, according to reporting by Politico.

The pause affects the removal of detainees slated for deportation to the DRC. The decision reflects concerns that the flights could expose Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials to the virus and potentially introduce the disease to the United States. ICE officials regularly accompany deportation flights, making exposure a practical concern for the agency.

However, experts have questioned whether the pause will meaningfully help prevent disease spread. At least one woman has been caught in limbo after officials moved her to Kinshasa, the capital of the DRC, only to refuse bringing her back because of the Ebola travel restrictions, despite a judge ordering her return.

The decision marks an intersection of disease control and immigration enforcement. The Democratic Republic of Congo has experienced recurring Ebola outbreaks in recent years. The virus causes severe illness with a high fatality rate, though it spreads through direct contact with bodily fluids rather than through the air.

The temporary pause comes as the Trump administration has significantly expanded its deportation operations. Human rights groups tracking ICE flights documented an 84 percent increase in deportations during 2025 compared to 2024. The administration's hardline immigration approach has made deportation flights a routine feature of its enforcement agenda.

The halt demonstrates the administration weighing competing priorities of immigration enforcement against health and safety concerns for federal personnel. Public health considerations have occasionally intersected with immigration policy, though such pauses remain uncommon.

The DRC World Cup team is proceeding with plans to travel to the United States despite the Ebola outbreak in their country. The team cancelled its training camp in Kinshasa due to health restrictions but has not altered its travel schedule for the World Cup.

The administration has not announced how long the deportation pause will last or what conditions would need to change for flights to resume. Officials have also not specified what benchmarks or public health metrics would trigger a restart of operations.

This action arrives amid broader scrutiny of the Trump administration's deportation flight operations. A group of 40 House Democrats recently sent a letter to the Federal Aviation Administration expressing "grave concerns" over the program, describing it as secretive and demanding transparency over allegations of mistreatment and inhumane conditions on ICE charter jets. The lawmakers referenced investigations revealing that detainees have been placed on flights without notice to counsel or family members and that the administration has sought to shield flights from public tracking systems, raising due process questions.

Sonny Rollins, Jazz Saxophone Legend, Dies at 95

10:00 PM ET

Sonny Rollins, one of the most influential saxophonists in jazz history, has died at age 95. The announcement was made on his website on Monday, described as coming "with deep sorrow and profound love." His publicist confirmed the news. Rollins died at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he grew up in Harlem and earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began learning saxophone at age seven, inspired by his siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. By high school, he was already playing alongside future stars including Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew.

Rollins became a central figure in the bebop movement that transformed jazz in the 1940s and 1950s. Miles Davis wrote about how Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" known for his aggressive, innovative approach and constant stream of fresh musical ideas.

His 1956 album "Saxophone Colossus" stands as a defining recording in modern jazz. The album featured "St. Thomas," his signature composition that blended Caribbean rhythms with bebop complexity and became a jazz standard. The song was named after his mother's birthplace in the Caribbean.

Rollins struggled with heroin addiction in the early 1950s. After committing armed robbery to support his habit, he was jailed for ten months on Rikers Island but successfully overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955. This turning point sparked extraordinary creative productivity. He released his debut album as bandleader in 1953 and recorded 17 more albums by decade's end, including "Way Out West" in 1957 and "Freedom Suite" in 1958.

Throughout his career, Rollins periodically withdrew from public performance to refine his craft. Starting in 1959, he took a three-year break from recording and performing, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the pedestrian walkway of the Williamsburg Bridge in New York. This practice ground inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge." Between 1969 and 1971, he took another sabbatical to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Rollins continued evolving his sound across decades, exploring Latin music, free improvisation, funk, and calypso. He composed and performed music for the 1966 film "Alfie" and later contributed uncredited saxophone to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album "Tattoo You." In his later years, he performed benefit concerts addressing climate change and released the 1998 album "Global Warming."

He was married to Lucille Pearson from 1965 until her death in 2004. Rollins received numerous honors including a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award, the National Medal of the Arts, and Kennedy Center recognition. President Barack Obama presented him with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011.
