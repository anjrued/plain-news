The decision means Flores and other Black coaches can pursue their claims that the league discriminated against them in hiring practices rather than being forced into private arbitration.

Flores, who coached the Dolphins from 2019 to 2021, filed the class-action lawsuit in 2022 alleging the NFL and its teams engaged in discriminatory hiring practices against Black coaches. The suit claims the league operates under a system that marginalizes Black candidates for head coaching and senior leadership positions.

The NFL had sought to move the case into private arbitration, arguing that employment disputes should be handled outside of public court proceedings. Lower courts rejected that effort, ruling the discrimination claims could proceed in federal court. The Supreme Court's decision not to intervene upholds those rulings.

Following the decision, the NFL issued a statement saying the league remains committed to diversity and inclusion in hiring. The organization pointed to its existing programs aimed at developing coaching talent from diverse backgrounds.

Flores's legal team released a statement calling the decision a victory for transparency and accountability. The attorneys said the public court process will allow for fuller examination of hiring practices across the league.

Flores currently serves as an assistant coach in the NFL after his head coaching tenure with Miami ended. His lawsuit names multiple teams and alleges he experienced sham interviews where hiring decisions had already been made before he was brought in to satisfy league diversity requirements.

The case will now return to lower court where discovery and potentially a trial will proceed. Other former coaches have joined the lawsuit as plaintiffs, expanding the scope beyond Flores's individual claims.

The NFL has faced ongoing scrutiny over the lack of diversity in head coaching positions despite the majority of players being Black. The league has implemented various initiatives over the years, including the Rooney Rule requiring teams to interview minority candidates, but critics say these measures have not produced meaningful change in hiring outcomes.

Sonny Rollins, widely regarded as one of the greatest saxophonists in jazz history, has died at 95. His death was announced on his website Monday and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He passed away at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he grew up in Harlem and earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. Inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller, he began playing saxophone at age seven. His early high school band included future jazz stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew, and Art Taylor.

Rollins became one of the last surviving figures from the bebop generation, a movement that transformed jazz from primarily dance and ballad music into a form of startling emotional expression. He was known as a genius of melody, capable of taking jazz standards or his own compositions and reshaping them through extended, sometimes epic improvisations.

His early career was interrupted by heroin addiction. In 1950, he committed armed robbery to fund his habit, a period he later described with regret. After serving ten months on Rikers Island, he successfully overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955. This turning point sparked remarkable creative output. He released his debut album as bandleader in 1953 and recorded 17 more albums by the end of the decade, including landmark recordings like Saxophone Colossus (1956), which featured his signature tune "St Thomas," and Freedom Suite (1958).

Between 1959 and 1962, Rollins took a three-year break from recording and performing, practicing up to fifteen hours daily on the pedestrian walkway of the Williamsburg Bridge to avoid disturbing neighbors. This period of intensive study culminated in his 1962 comeback album The Bridge. He took another sabbatical from 1969 to 1971 to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout his career, Rollins recorded more than 60 albums and collaborated with jazz giants including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, and John Coltrane. He worked across multiple styles, from bebop to hard bop, avant-garde, fusion, and Latin American music. In the 1980s and beyond, he continued experimenting with funk and calypso elements. He also composed music for the 1966 film Alfie and later became an environmental activist, releasing the album Global Warming in 1998.

Rollins was married twice: briefly to Dawn Finney in 1957, and to Lucille Pearson from 1965 until her death in 2004. He continued performing into his later years, maintaining his influence across generations of musicians and audiences worldwide.

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily halted the removal of detainees to the Democratic Republic of Congo as the country faces a widening Ebola outbreak, according to reporting by Politico. The pause was implemented due to concerns that deportation flights could expose Immigration and Customs Enforcement personnel to the virus.

The decision reflects a precautionary approach to protecting federal staff involved in deportation operations. ICE agents and personnel who accompany deportees on international flights would face potential exposure risks when traveling to areas experiencing active disease transmission.

However, the measure has created complications for some individuals already in the country. At least one woman has been left in limbo after officials moved her to Kinshasa, the capital of the DRC, only to later inform her they would not bring her back because of the travel restrictions. A judge had ordered her return, but the Ebola-related pause is preventing that from happening.

Public health experts have questioned the effectiveness of the pause in preventing disease spread. While Ebola spreads through direct contact with bodily fluids of infected individuals and has a high fatality rate when untreated, specialists say the deportation flight suspension alone won't address the underlying outbreak.

The pause applies specifically to flights bound for Congo and does not affect deportation operations to other countries. The Trump administration has continued deportation flights as part of its expanded immigration enforcement efforts. According to monitoring by human rights groups, ICE flights surged significantly during 2025, with an 84 percent increase from 2024.

The halt comes amid broader scrutiny of the administration's deportation program. A group of 40 House Democrats recently sent a letter to the Federal Aviation Administration expressing grave concerns about what they describe as secretive ICE charter operations. The lawmakers cited credible reports indicating that individuals have been placed on flights without notice to counsel or family members and called for greater transparency over flight operations.

The Democrats referenced a Guardian investigation based on leaked flight data, which revealed that the Trump administration transported detained immigrants in ways that routinely violated constitutional rights. Reports also identified allegations of abuse and rights violations at a private detention center in Alexandria, Louisiana, a key component of the administration's deportation infrastructure.

The Trump administration has previously defended its detention and deportation practices, describing claims of hidden flights as "categorically false" and arguing its detention centers maintain higher standards than most U.S. prisons.

The timing of when flights to Congo might resume depends on the trajectory of the outbreak and assessments of risk to U.S. personnel. Health officials have not publicly indicated whether they recommended the suspension or whether the decision originated within ICE or the Department of Homeland Security.

The Supreme Court declined to hear the NFL's appeal in a discrimination lawsuit brought by former Miami Dolphins head coach Brian Flores, allowing the case to move forward in federal court. The decision means Flores and other Black coaches can pursue their claims that the league discriminated against them in hiring practices rather than being forced into private arbitration.

Flores, who coached the Dolphins from 2019 to 2021, filed the class-action lawsuit in 2022 alleging the NFL and its teams engaged in discriminatory hiring practices against Black coaches. The suit claims the league operates under a system that marginalizes Black candidates for head coaching and senior leadership positions.

The NFL had sought to move the case into private arbitration, arguing that employment disputes should be handled outside of public court proceedings. Lower courts rejected that effort, ruling the discrimination claims could proceed in federal court. The Supreme Court's decision not to intervene upholds those rulings.

Following the decision, the NFL issued a statement saying the league remains committed to diversity and inclusion in hiring. The organization pointed to its existing programs aimed at developing coaching talent from diverse backgrounds.

Flores's legal team released a statement calling the decision a victory for transparency and accountability. The attorneys said the public court process will allow for fuller examination of hiring practices across the league.

Flores currently serves as an assistant coach in the NFL after his head coaching tenure with Miami ended. His lawsuit names multiple teams and alleges he experienced sham interviews where hiring decisions had already been made before he was brought in to satisfy league diversity requirements.

The case will now return to lower court where discovery and potentially a trial will proceed. Other former coaches have joined the lawsuit as plaintiffs, expanding the scope beyond Flores's individual claims.

The NFL has faced ongoing scrutiny over the lack of diversity in head coaching positions despite the majority of players being Black. The league has implemented various initiatives over the years, including the Rooney Rule requiring teams to interview minority candidates, but critics say these measures have not produced meaningful change in hiring outcomes.

Sonny Rollins, jazz saxophone pioneer, dies at 95

Sonny Rollins, widely regarded as one of the greatest saxophonists in jazz history, has died at 95. His death was announced on his website Monday and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He passed away at his home in...

3:00 PM ET

Sonny Rollins, widely regarded as one of the greatest saxophonists in jazz history, has died at 95. His death was announced on his website Monday and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He passed away at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he grew up in Harlem and earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. Inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller, he began playing saxophone at age seven. His early high school band included future jazz stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew, and Art Taylor.

Rollins became one of the last surviving figures from the bebop generation, a movement that transformed jazz from primarily dance and ballad music into a form of startling emotional expression. He was known as a genius of melody, capable of taking jazz standards or his own compositions and reshaping them through extended, sometimes epic improvisations.

His early career was interrupted by heroin addiction. In 1950, he committed armed robbery to fund his habit, a period he later described with regret. After serving ten months on Rikers Island, he successfully overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955. This turning point sparked remarkable creative output. He released his debut album as bandleader in 1953 and recorded 17 more albums by the end of the decade, including landmark recordings like Saxophone Colossus (1956), which featured his signature tune "St Thomas," and Freedom Suite (1958).

Between 1959 and 1962, Rollins took a three-year break from recording and performing, practicing up to fifteen hours daily on the pedestrian walkway of the Williamsburg Bridge to avoid disturbing neighbors. This period of intensive study culminated in his 1962 comeback album The Bridge. He took another sabbatical from 1969 to 1971 to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout his career, Rollins recorded more than 60 albums and collaborated with jazz giants including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, and John Coltrane. He worked across multiple styles, from bebop to hard bop, avant-garde, fusion, and Latin American music. In the 1980s and beyond, he continued experimenting with funk and calypso elements. He also composed music for the 1966 film Alfie and later became an environmental activist, releasing the album Global Warming in 1998.

Rollins was married twice: briefly to Dawn Finney in 1957, and to Lucille Pearson from 1965 until her death in 2004. He continued performing into his later years, maintaining his influence across generations of musicians and audiences worldwide.
