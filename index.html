Google has fundamentally redesigned its search box for the first time in nearly 30 years, marking what the company calls the biggest change in Google Search's history. The update, announced at Google I/O, integrates artificial intelligence directly into the search experience and rolls out globally to desktop and mobile users this week.

The redesign allows users to ask longer, more conversational questions rather than the keyword-based searches that have defined Google for decades. The new interface will accommodate queries phrased as natural questions, similar to how people talk to each other. Elizabeth Reid, who oversees the product, stated that "Google Search is AI search," emphasizing the scale of this transformation.

The changes are powered by Gemini 3.5, Google's latest artificial intelligence model. The new system will make longer text predictions based on what users type, allowing for more sophisticated and contextual responses. Search results will increasingly display visual elements and suggestions to interact directly with Google's AI chatbot, enabling users to perform actions like creating calendar invites or building spreadsheets within the search interface.

Rather than returning a list of links, results will more closely resemble visually enhanced versions of AI Overviews, the bulleted summaries Google Search currently provides. Users who prefer the traditional link-based format can still access it by clicking a "Web" tab.

The expansion builds on momentum from Google's AI Mode, introduced a year ago. Since then, queries to this chatbot-specific interface have doubled every quarter. Google reports that search queries reached an all-time high last month, with 900 million people now using the Gemini app monthly.

For paid subscribers, Google is introducing new capabilities. Information agents will perform autonomous research and return summaries or action plans. A generative user interface feature will create customized visuals and interactive dashboards based on user requests. Gemini Spark, a new agent feature, can access information from Gmail, Google Calendar, and other Google products to help with research, shopping, and planning.

Alongside the search redesign, Google announced intelligent eyewear developed with Samsung, Warby Parker, and Gentle Monster. The voice-activated glasses with embedded cameras will respond to Gemini commands and launch this fall. This marks Google's return to wearable technology more than a decade after Google Glass, which was shuttered in 2015 following public backlash.

The company also announced AI coding features for Gemini and new cybersecurity-focused AI tools. These developments reflect Google's broader strategy to integrate artificial intelligence across its product ecosystem as competition in consumer AI intensifies.

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonists in history, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his website on Monday at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he grew up in Harlem during a vibrant period for jazz. He earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother and began playing saxophone at age seven, inspired by his siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. His early high school band included future stars Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew.

Rollins became one of the last living connections to the bebop generation, helping transform jazz from predominantly dance and ballad music into a startlingly expressive art form. He recorded more than 60 albums from the late 1940s onward, collaborating with Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane and many others. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting Rollins with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, President Barack Obama credited him with inspiring him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Rollins was known for his melodic genius and willingness to break with convention. He once described himself as "primitive," driven more by feeling than calculation. This approach helped reshape jazz alongside other bebop pioneers, eventually loosening into hard bop and post-bop styles.

His early career faced obstacles when heroin addiction led to an armed robbery conviction in 1950. He spent 10 months on Rikers Island but successfully overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955. This turning point sparked a creative explosion. His debut album as bandleader came in 1953, followed by landmark recordings including "Saxophone Colossus" in 1956, featuring his signature tune "St Thomas," which referenced calypso music and his mother's Caribbean heritage. Other significant albums included "Way Out West" in 1957 and "Freedom Suite" in 1958.

In 1959, Rollins took a three-year break from recording and performing, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the pedestrian walkway of the Williamsburg Bridge to avoid disturbing neighbors. This period inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge." He took another sabbatical from 1969 to 1971, traveling to an Indian ashram to study yoga, philosophy and meditation.

Throughout the following decades, Rollins continued exploring new musical directions, incorporating funk, calypso and R&B influences while maintaining his jazz foundation. He also campaigned for environmental awareness, releasing the 1998 album "Global Warming" and performing benefit concerts addressing climate change.

Rollins married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004. He performed his final concert in 2012 at age 82.

Trump administration pauses deportation flights to Congo over Ebola outbreak

The Trump administration has temporarily halted deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo due to an expanding Ebola outbreak in the country, according to reporting by Politico. Officials suspended the flights over concerns that they could expose Immigration and Customs Enforcement personnel to the virus and potentially facilitate disease spread to the United State...

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily halted deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo due to an expanding Ebola outbreak in the country, according to reporting by Politico. Officials suspended the flights over concerns that they could expose Immigration and Customs Enforcement personnel to the virus and potentially facilitate disease spread to the United States.

The pause applies specifically to removals to Congo, while deportation operations to other countries continue. Immigration and Customs Enforcement has not announced changes to detention or removal proceedings for Congolese nationals currently in the United States.

However, public health experts question whether the pause will effectively prevent disease transmission. The move has already created complications for at least one woman who was transferred to Kinshasa, Congo's capital, before officials instituted the travel restrictions. Despite a judge's order for her return, administration officials say they will not bring her back because of the Ebola-related ban on flights to the country.

Ebola causes severe illness and has a high fatality rate. The virus spreads through direct contact with bodily fluids from infected people or contaminated materials. Outbreaks in Central Africa have killed thousands over past decades. The current outbreak has prompted health restrictions in Kinshasa, prompting Congo's World Cup team to cancel the Kinshasa portion of its pre-tournament training, though the team still plans to travel to the United States for preparation.

The temporary suspension reflects the Trump administration's broader focus on immigration enforcement. ICE conducts deportation flights as part of these efforts, transporting individuals ordered removed from the United States back to their countries of origin.

The decision also comes amid increased scrutiny of ICE's deportation flight operations. A group of 40 House Democrats recently wrote to the Federal Aviation Administration expressing "grave concerns" about the Trump administration's secretive deportation flight program. The lawmakers described "urgent need for transparency" and alleged "inappropriate and dangerous" efforts to shield flights from public scrutiny.

According to the letter, human rights groups tracking ICE flights identified an 84 percent increase from 2024. The lawmakers cited credible reports indicating individuals were placed on flights without notice to counsel or family members and expressed concern about due process violations.

The Associated Press previously reported that dozens of charter jets used for deportation flights received unusual FAA permission to block certain data, including tail numbers, from public flight tracking sites, making ICE air operations harder to monitor.

The Trump administration has dismissed these claims, describing allegations of "hidden" or "weaponized" flights as "categorically false" and asserting its detention centers maintain "higher standards than most US prisons."

Officials have not specified when deportation flights to Congo might resume.

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonists in history, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his website on Monday at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he grew up in Harlem during a vibrant period for jazz. He earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother and began playing saxophone at age seven, inspired by his siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. His early high school band included future stars Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew.

Rollins became one of the last living connections to the bebop generation, helping transform jazz from predominantly dance and ballad music into a startlingly expressive art form. He recorded more than 60 albums from the late 1940s onward, collaborating with Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane and many others. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting Rollins with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, President Barack Obama credited him with inspiring him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Rollins was known for his melodic genius and willingness to break with convention. He once described himself as "primitive," driven more by feeling than calculation. This approach helped reshape jazz alongside other bebop pioneers, eventually loosening into hard bop and post-bop styles.

His early career faced obstacles when heroin addiction led to an armed robbery conviction in 1950. He spent 10 months on Rikers Island but successfully overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955. This turning point sparked a creative explosion. His debut album as bandleader came in 1953, followed by landmark recordings including "Saxophone Colossus" in 1956, featuring his signature tune "St Thomas," which referenced calypso music and his mother's Caribbean heritage. Other significant albums included "Way Out West" in 1957 and "Freedom Suite" in 1958.

In 1959, Rollins took a three-year break from recording and performing, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the pedestrian walkway of the Williamsburg Bridge to avoid disturbing neighbors. This period inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge." He took another sabbatical from 1969 to 1971, traveling to an Indian ashram to study yoga, philosophy and meditation.

Throughout the following decades, Rollins continued exploring new musical directions, incorporating funk, calypso and R&B influences while maintaining his jazz foundation. He also campaigned for environmental awareness, releasing the 1998 album "Global Warming" and performing benefit concerts addressing climate change.

Rollins married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004. He performed his final concert in 2012 at age 82.
