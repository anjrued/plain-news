The United States military conducted strikes on targets in southern Iran, hitting missile launch sites and vessels used for laying mines. US Central Command described the operations as defensive in nature, emphasizing that the action did not signal an end to the seven-week ceasefire currently in place between the two countries.

Navy Captain Tim Hawkins stated that Centcom "continues to defend our forces while using restraint during the ongoing ceasefire." The strikes mark the second major military operation to occur during the ceasefire period.

Iranian state media reported explosions near Bandar Abbas, a port city on the Strait of Hormuz. A news website believed to be close to former Revolutionary Guard leadership identified four Guard troops it said were killed in strikes on boats. Iran's state news agency later stated the situation was "completely under control."

The timing of the strikes coincided with renewed diplomatic activity. A delegation of Iranian negotiators, including Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, traveled to Qatar to continue talks with the United States. The presence of Iran's central bank governor among the delegation has sparked speculation that discussions will focus on releasing frozen Iranian assets held in banks outside Iran.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio stated that despite the latest strikes, a peace deal remained possible. He said the Strait of Hormuz would open "one way or the other," addressing a key priority for the Trump administration as it approaches midterm elections and faces voter concerns about rising costs.

The negotiations reportedly center on reopening the Strait of Hormuz for commercial shipping, through which approximately one-fifth of the world's oil and liquefied natural gas typically flows. Iran has indicated that future management of the strait would be determined between Iran and Oman, with potential "fees for navigational services" imposed.

Trump has made significant concessions in recent negotiations. On social media, he announced that Iran's stockpile of highly enriched uranium could be destroyed within Iran under international oversight, potentially representing a major compromise. Previous rounds of talks had stalled over uranium disposition, with Iran previously willing only to dilute its enriched uranium, not transfer it to other countries.

Iran has made its own demands for any agreement. The country has insisted that a ceasefire in Lebanon must be included in any memorandum of understanding that would lead to reopening the strait. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu announced plans to intensify strikes against Hezbollah in Lebanon, complicating peace efforts despite an existing ceasefire agreement.

Trump warned that talks were progressing well but cautioned that fresh attacks would follow if negotiations failed. "It will only be a Great Deal for all, or no Deal at all," he wrote, signaling his determination to reach a resolution while maintaining military pressure.

Thousands of residents near Garden Grove, California have been cleared to return to their homes after officials determined that a leaking chemical storage tank no longer poses a risk of explosion. However, authorities stressed that the overall crisis remains active.

The hazardous chemical tank in Orange County developed a crack over the weekend, which officials said actually helped reduce the immediate danger. Firefighters worked overnight to cool and monitor the tank, ultimately eliminating the threat of a catastrophic blast. The successful stabilization effort allowed emergency managers to reduce the evacuation zone significantly.

According to BBC News, approximately 16,000 people remain unable to return home due to the new and reduced evacuation boundaries. The exact number of residents who have been allowed to go back was not specified in initial reports, though thousands were confirmed to be returning as evacuation maps were updated to reflect the smaller affected area.

Despite the positive development regarding the explosion risk, officials emphasized that the situation is far from resolved. The chemical leak itself continues to require attention from hazardous materials teams who are working to contain it. Safety measures remain in place around the facility.

The incident began when the storage tank developed a leak, releasing hazardous materials and triggering concerns about public safety in the densely populated Orange County region. As a precaution, authorities ordered evacuations while they assessed the full extent of the danger posed by the overheating tank.

Multiple emergency response agencies coordinated throughout the crisis, managing both the chemical hazard and the displacement of residents from nearby neighborhoods. Local officials provided regular updates about which areas remained under evacuation orders and when it was safe for residents to return home.

The cracking of the tank, while initially concerning, proved beneficial in relieving the internal pressure that had raised fears of an explosion. This development allowed firefighters to focus on cooling and monitoring operations overnight, ultimately achieving stabilization of the dangerous situation.

Garden Grove is located in northern Orange County, southeast of Los Angeles. The affected area includes residential neighborhoods situated near the chemical storage facility that leaked hazardous materials over the weekend.

While the elimination of the explosion threat represents significant progress, officials have not provided a timeline for when hazardous materials teams will fully resolve the chemical leak or when all safety restrictions will be lifted. The situation continues to require ongoing monitoring and containment efforts as authorities work toward complete resolution of the incident.

Oil prices dropped below $100 a barrel on Monday as markets responded positively to signs that the United States and Iran are moving closer to a peace agreement. Brent crude futures, the global benchmark, fell 6% to $97.43 a barrel, marking the lowest level in two weeks.

The decline reflects cautious optimism about ending the nearly three-month conflict between the US, Israel, and Iran. However, significant obstacles remain. While a framework for an agreement has been negotiated, the two nations still disagree on key issues, including Iran's blockade of the Strait of Hormuz. An Iranian government spokesperson emphasized that an agreement was "not imminent," tempering expectations of a quick resolution.

Warren Patterson, head of commodities strategy at ING, warned that markets should approach the situation carefully. "We've been at this stage before, only for talks to break down," he told Reuters. "Therefore, the market will likely be more cautious about overreacting."

The Strait of Hormuz became a critical flashpoint after the US and Israel launched missile strikes on Tehran on February 28. Iran's de facto closure of the waterway caused energy prices to spike dramatically. Even if negotiations succeed and the strait reopens, analysts expect a return to normal oil flows will take months while damaged infrastructure in Qatar and other locations is repaired.

Some shipping activity resumed on Monday. Two tankers carrying liquefied natural gas exited the strait headed to Pakistan and China, while a supertanker carrying Iraqi crude departed the Gulf for China after being stranded for almost three months. Despite these movements, Giovanni Staunovo, an analyst at UBS, cautioned that flows through the strait remain restricted and physical oil movement will be the key metric to watch.

The positive sentiment extended beyond energy markets. Japan's Nikkei rose nearly 3%, and the pan-European Stoxx 600 index gained 1%. Several major markets were closed Monday for a public holiday. Gold climbed 1.46% to $4,574 an ounce, while the dollar dipped 0.3% against a basket of major currencies.

Stephen Innes, an independent analyst, said investors began pricing in the possibility that "the world's most dangerous energy choke point may soon reopen to something resembling normal flow." This shift reflected how much inflation concern had been embedded in markets during the energy shock.

Oil prices remain significantly elevated compared to pre-war levels, when Brent crude traded around $70 a barrel. The potential deal carries substantial implications for global inflation and central bank policy. Higher oil prices have driven inflation expectations worldwide and shifted predictions from interest rate cuts to increases. Markets currently expect the Bank of England to raise rates twice this year.

9:41 PM ET

Sonny Rollins, widely regarded as one of the greatest jazz saxophonists in history, died Monday at his home in Woodstock, New York. He was 95. His death was announced on his website and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. No cause was given.

Rollins shaped modern jazz over more than seven decades, recording more than 60 albums and collaborating with jazz pioneers including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, and John Coltrane. He was among the last living stars of the bebop generation, the movement that transformed jazz from dance and ballad music into a form of startling artistic expression.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he grew up in Harlem and received the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began studying saxophone at age seven, inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. His high school band included future stars Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew, launching a career marked by innovation and technical brilliance.

Rollins became known for a willingness to break convention and embrace improvisation. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting him with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, President Barack Obama said Rollins had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

His early career was interrupted by heroin addiction. In 1950, he committed armed robbery to support his habit and spent 10 months in jail on Rikers Island. He overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955, which sparked extraordinary creative output. He released his debut album as bandleader in 1953 and recorded 17 more by decade's end, including landmark albums like "Saxophone Colossus" (1956) featuring the signature tune "St Thomas," and "Freedom Suite" (1958).

In 1959, Rollins took a three-year break from performing and recording, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway to avoid disturbing neighbors. This unconventional practice routine became legendary and inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge."

Throughout subsequent decades, Rollins explored diverse musical directions, incorporating Latin, funk, and calypso influences. He also contributed uncredited saxophone to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album "Tattoo You" and composed the soundtrack to the 1966 film "Alfie."

Beyond music, Rollins was an environmental advocate, organizing benefit concerts and releasing the 1998 album "Global Warming" to raise awareness about climate change.

He was married twice, to Dawn Finney briefly in 1957 and to Lucille Pearson from 1965 until her death in 2004. Rollins continued performing into his eighties, maintaining the creative energy that defined his approach to music.

Trump administration pauses deportation flights to Congo over Ebola outbreak

The decision reflects concerns about exposing Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials to the virus and preventing potential spread closer to the United States. The pause represents a notable shi...

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily halted the removal of detainees to the Democratic Republic of Congo due to a widening Ebola outbreak, according to reporting by Politico. The decision reflects concerns about exposing Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials to the virus and preventing potential spread closer to the United States.

The pause represents a notable shift in an administration that has prioritized aggressive immigration enforcement. ICE had been conducting removal flights to the central African nation as part of broader deportation operations targeting individuals without legal status in the United States.

However, the administration's approach to the pause reveals complications. While officials have stopped deportation flights to Congo, they will not return detainees who have already been deported to the country. At least one woman now faces an uncertain situation after being moved to Kinshasa, the capital of the DRC. Despite a judge's order for her return, officials say they will not bring her back because of the Ebola travel restrictions.

Ebola causes severe illness and frequently proves fatal. The virus spreads through direct contact with bodily fluids of infected people or animals. Health experts have expressed serious concerns about the outbreak's trajectory in Congo, where health infrastructure faces significant challenges in containing the disease when it emerges.

The administration determined that continuing deportation flights during an active outbreak could endanger federal personnel. ICE officials involved in transfer operations would face potential exposure risks when handing over individuals to Congolese authorities.

The decision also reflects concerns about the virus potentially reaching American soil through returning ICE officials or aircraft. While public health experts generally consider the risk of Ebola reaching the United States to be low, the administration chose to implement precautions given the disease's severity.

The Democratic Republic of Congo has experienced multiple Ebola outbreaks in recent years. International health organizations typically provide support during these crises, though resources remain limited.

The administration has not specified when deportation flights to Congo might resume. Timeline decisions will likely depend on how the outbreak develops and assessments from health officials about when operations can safely restart.

This marks an intersection between public health considerations and immigration enforcement policy under the Trump administration. The administration continues deportation operations to other countries where immediate health risks are not identified.

The pause also occurs amid broader scrutiny of ICE's deportation flight operations. A group of 40 House Democrats recently demanded FAA transparency regarding allegations of mistreatment and inhumane conditions on ICE charter jets, citing reports of detainees placed on flights without notice to counsel or family members.

Jazz Saxophonist Sonny Rollins Dies at 95

Sonny Rollins, widely regarded as one of the greatest jazz saxophonists in history, died Monday at his home in Woodstock, New York. He was 95. His death was announced on his website and confirmed by his publicist Terri H...

11:00 PM ET

Sonny Rollins, widely regarded as one of the greatest jazz saxophonists in history, died Monday at his home in Woodstock, New York. He was 95. His death was announced on his website and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. No cause was given.

Rollins shaped modern jazz over more than seven decades, recording more than 60 albums and collaborating with jazz pioneers including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, and John Coltrane. He was among the last living stars of the bebop generation, the movement that transformed jazz from dance and ballad music into a form of startling artistic expression.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he grew up in Harlem and received the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began studying saxophone at age seven, inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. His high school band included future stars Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew, launching a career marked by innovation and technical brilliance.

Rollins became known for a willingness to break convention and embrace improvisation. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting him with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, President Barack Obama said Rollins had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

His early career was interrupted by heroin addiction. In 1950, he committed armed robbery to support his habit and spent 10 months in jail on Rikers Island. He overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955, which sparked extraordinary creative output. He released his debut album as bandleader in 1953 and recorded 17 more by decade's end, including landmark albums like "Saxophone Colossus" (1956) featuring the signature tune "St Thomas," and "Freedom Suite" (1958).

In 1959, Rollins took a three-year break from performing and recording, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway to avoid disturbing neighbors. This unconventional practice routine became legendary and inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge."

Throughout subsequent decades, Rollins explored diverse musical directions, incorporating Latin, funk, and calypso influences. He also contributed uncredited saxophone to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album "Tattoo You" and composed the soundtrack to the 1966 film "Alfie."

Beyond music, Rollins was an environmental advocate, organizing benefit concerts and releasing the 1998 album "Global Warming" to raise awareness about climate change.

He was married twice, to Dawn Finney briefly in 1957 and to Lucille Pearson from 1965 until her death in 2004. Rollins continued performing into his eighties, maintaining the creative energy that defined his approach to music.
