California chemical tank no longer at risk of explosion after days-long crisis

A few minutes ago

Officials in Orange County, California announced that the threat of a catastrophic explosion from an overheating chemical tank has been eliminated after an intensive round-the-clock response operation. The breakthrough came after firefighters discovered a crack in the tank over the weekend that has been relieving pressure and reducing explosion risk.

The crisis began Thursday afternoon when a leak was detected at GKN Aerospace in Garden Grove, a facility that manufactures parts for commercial and military aircraft. The tank contained between 6,000 and 7,000 gallons of methyl methacrylate, a highly flammable chemical used to produce plastic and resin.

EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin warned that the tank "will fail" and outlined potential scenarios. The most catastrophic outcome would be an explosion that triggers additional tanks to explode, he said. The more likely scenario would involve a low-volume chemical release. These warnings prompted the evacuation of approximately 50,000 people from the surrounding area.

To prevent an explosion, firefighters continuously sprayed the tank with water to keep the temperature below 85 degrees Fahrenheit. On Friday, officials added a neutralizing agent to a nearby tank to further limit explosion risks.

The discovery of the crack provided a significant turning point. Garden Grove Mayor Stephanie Klopfenstein reported receiving "a somewhat positive briefing" after firefighters identified the damage late Saturday night. According to Orange County Fire Chief TJ McGovern, the crack could potentially allow authorities to gradually drain the chemicals from the tank.

Engineering experts explained why the crack was considered advantageous. Andrew Whelton, an engineering professor at Purdue University, used a soda can analogy to describe the situation: "If you leave it in a hot car it can explode. But if you put a hole in the can, the product is released and the can itself doesn't explode."

However, other experts cautioned that risks remained. Faisal Khan, head of the chemical engineering department at Texas A&M University, noted that while cooling efforts were underway, "runaway reaction may be occurring deep inside the tank" and explosion risks had not been completely eliminated.

President Trump approved an emergency declaration for the incident at Governor Gavin Newsom's request, providing federal assistance and funding for response efforts, evacuation operations, and ongoing monitoring.

Air quality monitoring showed no pollutants detected in the area, with officials reporting that measurements remained completely normal. Disneyland theme parks in nearby Anaheim were not under evacuation orders but said they were monitoring the situation and supporting affected employees.

At least two Garden Grove residents filed lawsuits against GKN Aerospace, citing evacuation orders, shelter-in-place directives, and exposure concerns. San Diego volunteers traveled to the affected area to provide temporary shelter and supplies to displaced residents.

The United States and Iran are moving toward an agreement to end their military conflict, though Iranian officials say significant hurdles must still be overcome. Negotiations are progressing, but disagreement persists over critical terms.

The emerging framework centers on reopening the Strait of Hormuz, a vital shipping channel for global oil supplies. Under the proposed agreement, Iran would reopen the strait and clear mines it deployed there, allowing ships to pass freely. In exchange, the US would lift its naval blockade on Iranian ports. According to reports, a 60-day ceasefire would accompany these measures, during which Iran could freely sell oil.

President Trump announced on Saturday that a memorandum of understanding had been "largely negotiated," but he walked back those claims on Sunday, saying talks were progressing in an "orderly and constructive" manner and that he had instructed his representatives not to rush. "It isn't even fully negotiated yet," he said.

Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmail Baghaei stated that negotiators had reached conclusions on "a large portion of the issues under discussion" but cautioned against assuming an agreement was imminent.

The proposed deal has sparked sharp criticism from Republican foreign policy hawks. Senator Roger Wicker, who chairs the Senate Armed Services Committee, called a rumored 60-day ceasefire a "disaster" that would undermine military achievements. Senator Lindsey Graham warned that accepting Iran's control over the strait would represent "a major shift of the balance of power in the region" and questioned why the war started if these outcomes were now acceptable.

Senator Ted Cruz expressed deep concern, arguing that allowing Iran to enrich uranium and develop nuclear weapons while gaining effective control over the strait would be "a disastrous mistake." He urged Trump to "continue to hold the line."

However, Trump's nuclear demands remain unaddressed in public discussions. While he has repeatedly insisted that Iran renounce nuclear ambitions as a "red line," the proposed deal does not mention Iran's nuclear program or uranium stockpile. Iranian officials have sought to negotiate nuclear matters at a later date.

Trump has also linked any Iran agreement to expanded participation in the Abraham Accords, which normalized relations between Israel and several Muslim-majority countries. He told Muslim leaders that joining the accords should be mandatory as part of an Iran deal.

Former Secretary of State Mike Pompeo criticized the proposal, comparing it unfavorably to the 2015 nuclear agreement negotiated by the Obama administration, which Trump abandoned. He called for opening the strait, denying Iran access to money, and reducing Iranian military capability.

Trump's current Secretary of State Marco Rubio hailed "significant" progress on the negotiations.

Neither government has released the full terms or announced a target date for completing negotiations.

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily halted the removal of detainees to the Democratic Republic of Congo as an Ebola outbreak spreads across the country, according to reporting by Politico. However, health experts question whether the pause will actually prevent disease transmission.

The decision reflects concerns about exposing Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials to the virus during deportation operations. By suspending flights, administration officials aim to reduce the risk that deportation activities could inadvertently bring Ebola closer to American territory.

The pause comes despite the administration's continued commitment to its broader immigration enforcement agenda, which has included increased deportations to multiple countries. Congo had been among the destinations for deportation flights before health concerns prompted the temporary halt.

The administration has not announced when deportation flights to Congo might resume. Officials say they will continue monitoring the outbreak and assessing when operations can safely continue.

The situation has created complications for some individuals already caught in the system. At least one woman was moved to Kinshasa, the capital of the DRC, and officials now say they will not bring her back because of the Ebola travel ban, despite a judge's order for her return.

The Ebola virus spreads through contact with bodily fluids of infected individuals and can cause severe illness with high fatality rates. The Democratic Republic of Congo has dealt with multiple Ebola outbreaks in recent years, and health authorities in the country work with international organizations to contain them, though response efforts face challenges in remote and conflict-affected areas.

This pause represents one of several recent controversies surrounding the Trump administration's deportation practices. Earlier this year, a group of 40 House Democrats expressed "grave concerns" over what they described as the administration's secretive deportation flight program. The lawmakers demanded that the Federal Aviation Administration address allegations of mistreatment and inhumane conditions on ICE charter jets.

In their letter to FAA administrator Bryan Bedford, the Democrats highlighted "credible reports" that individuals had been placed on flights without notice to their lawyers or family members. They expressed particular concern about flights shielded from public tracking systems, which they said raised "serious due process concerns."

According to monitoring by human rights groups, ICE flights surged during 2025, with an 84% increase from 2024. The lawmakers called for greater transparency about these operations, requesting detailed reports on all ICE air operations since Trump took office, including flight data and passenger numbers.

The Trump administration has previously rejected claims of hidden deportation flights, describing such characterizations as "categorically false" and arguing its detention centers maintain "higher standards than most US prisons."
