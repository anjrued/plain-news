A coal mine explosion in China has killed at least 90 people, making it the deadliest such incident in the country since 2009. The blast occurred at a coal mine, though specific details about the location and timing have not been provided in available reports.

The death toll marks a significant industrial disaster for China, which has worked to improve mine safety standards over the past two decades. The country experienced frequent deadly mining accidents in the 2000s, with thousands of workers killed annually in collapses, explosions, and gas leaks.

Since 2009, China has implemented stricter safety regulations and closed thousands of smaller, less safe mines. The measures led to a steady decline in mining fatalities, making this incident particularly notable as the worst in more than 15 years.

Coal remains a critical energy source for China, which relies on the fuel for approximately 60 percent of its electricity generation. The country operates thousands of coal mines, employing millions of workers across the industry. Despite safety improvements, mining continues to be among the most dangerous occupations in China.

Mine explosions typically result from the ignition of methane gas or coal dust, both of which can accumulate in underground mining operations. Proper ventilation systems and gas monitoring equipment are essential safety measures, but failures in these systems or violations of safety protocols can lead to catastrophic results.

The 2009 benchmark this incident surpasses likely refers to several major mining disasters that year. In that period, China recorded multiple accidents with death tolls exceeding 70 workers, prompting government pledges to further strengthen enforcement of safety regulations.

Chinese authorities typically launch investigations following major industrial accidents to determine causes and assign responsibility. Such investigations often result in criminal charges against mine operators or officials found to have violated safety standards or failed in oversight duties.

The scale of casualties in this incident will likely prompt renewed scrutiny of mine safety practices and enforcement mechanisms across China's coal industry.

Tulsi Gabbard is leaving her post as US director of national intelligence following a tumultuous stint in which she was largely sidelined as Donald Trump launched attacks on Venezuela and Iran.

In a letter to the US president, she said she would resign and leave her post on 30 June. "While we have made significant progress … I recognize there is still important work to be done," she wrote.

The White House forced Gabbard to resign, the Reuters news agency reported, citing a source familiar with the issue. However, a spokesperson for Gabbard's office said rumors that Gabbard had been forcibly ousted from office were "100% false".

In her resignation letter, Gabbard said her husband, Abraham, "has recently been diagnosed with an extremely rare form of bone cancer". She added: "At this time, I must step away from public service to be by his side and fully support him through this battle."

Gabbard was an unconventional choice for the role. The former Democrat lacked an intelligence background and her foreign policy views at times diverged with Trump's, particularly on questions of overseas military intervention. She also took unusual measures to ingratiate herself to the president by vowing to root out politicization across US spy agencies and embracing his agenda of election denial.

Over time, Gabbard became increasingly sidelined by the president, who excluded her from key national security conversations on Iran and Venezuela, according to people familiar with the office of the director of national intelligence.

Trump was asking cabinet members last month whether he should replace Gabbard, according to two people briefed on the discussions.

"Unfortunately, after having done a great job, Tulsi Gabbard will be leaving the Administration on June 30th," he wrote in a statement on his Truth Social platform on Friday. Gabbard "has done an incredible job, and we will miss her", the president said, adding that Aaron Lukas, principal deputy director of national intelligence, would serve as acting director of national intelligence.

Gabbard's tumultuous 15-month tenure as the nation's top intelligence official ended Friday when she submitted her resignation. During her time in the role, she took many actions seemingly meant to flatter Trump, even as her foreign policy views at times diverged with the president's, particularly on questions of overseas military intervention.

The resignation comes as Trump and secretary of state Marco Rubio have raised the specter of military intervention in Cuba and as the US faces mounting pressure over operations in Iran and Venezuela.
