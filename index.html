A few minutes ago

Donald Trump announced on Saturday that a peace deal with Iran "has been largely negotiated," potentially ending the military conflict that began in February between the US, Israel, and Iran.

The president posted on his social media platform Truth Social that "final aspects and details" of a "Memorandum of Understanding" are still under discussion and "will be announced shortly." As part of the agreement, Trump said the Strait of Hormuz will be reopened.

The announcement came after Trump held calls with a Pakistani mediator, Gulf allies, and Israel. Iran's Fars news agency, which has ties to the Islamic Revolutionary Guard, reported that the strait will remain under Iranian control.

According to multiple news reports, the initial framework does not include resolution of the nuclear weapons issue, though the deal includes provisions for two-month negotiations on Iran's nuclear program to follow.

However, Trump's optimistic announcement came just days after he posted a threatening message aimed at Tehran. Earlier, he wrote on social media: "For Iran, the Clock is Ticking, and they better get moving, FAST, or there won't be anything left of them. TIME IS OF THE ESSENCE!"

According to Axios, Trump is scheduled to meet with his national security team to discuss next steps regarding Iran. The president also spoke with Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu before an Israeli security cabinet meeting focused on Iran, Lebanon, and Gaza. There is growing speculation in Israel that fighting with Iran could resume if there are no signs of compromise in negotiations.

Regional tensions remain high despite talk of a deal. The United Arab Emirates blamed a fire near its nuclear power plant on a drone launched by Iran or one of its proxies, describing the incident as a "dangerous escalation."

Three months into the conflict with Iran, the Trump administration has oscillated between strike threats and diplomatic overtures. Neither approach has yet yielded a clear resolution to the crisis.

The potential agreement represents a significant shift after months of military action. The war began in February and has involved both US and Israeli forces operating against Iran. The closure of the Strait of Hormuz, a critical waterway for global oil shipments, has been one of the most economically disruptive aspects of the conflict.

Trump's announcement suggests that intensive diplomatic efforts may be bearing fruit, though the deal remains unfinalized. The coming days will reveal whether the framework can hold and whether both sides can reach agreement on remaining details. The fate of negotiations over Iran's nuclear program in the months ahead will likely determine the durability of any peace agreement.

6:53 PM ET

The White House was briefly placed under lockdown on Saturday evening after a sustained exchange of gunfire erupted near a security checkpoint, leaving the suspected gunman dead and a bystander wounded.

According to the US Secret Service, the incident began shortly after 6pm local time when a man approached a checkpoint at a White House gate near 17th Street and Pennsylvania Avenue NW. The suspect took out a pistol and opened fire at officers stationed at the checkpoint.

Secret Service officers protecting the president and vice president returned fire, striking the suspect. He was transported to an area hospital where he later died from his injuries. No Secret Service personnel were injured during the exchange.

A bystander on the street was also shot during the incident. The Secret Service said it remains unclear whether the civilian was struck by the suspect's initial gunfire or during the subsequent exchange between officers and the gunman.

President Donald Trump was at the White House during the shooting but was not impacted by the incident. He said earlier that he was in the Oval Office but did not immediately comment on the gunfire.

Multiple reporters at the White House witnessed the dramatic scene unfold. Selina Wang, a White House correspondent for ABC News, shared video on social media showing the sound of gunfire interrupting her as she recorded a report on US negotiations with Iran. In the footage, Wang could be seen diving for cover as what she described as dozens of gunshots rang out.

"We were told to sprint to the press briefing room, where we are holding now," Wang said.

Chris Flanagan, an anchor for local news channel DC News Now who took cover in the White House briefing room, reported that approximately 30 shots were fired. A CBS News producer estimated at least 20 shots had been fired during the exchange.

The Secret Service issued a statement at approximately 6:45pm local time confirming they were aware of reports of shots fired near 17th Street and Pennsylvania Avenue NW and were working to verify information with personnel on the ground. The security lockdown was lifted after the situation was contained.

FBI Director Kash Patel posted that the FBI was on the scene supporting the Secret Service response and would update the public as more information became available.

The shooting comes less than three weeks after another incident in which a man brandishing a gun fired at a Secret Service officer near the route of Vice President JD Vance's motorcade outside the White House. In that incident, a civilian witness was wounded in the leg and officers returned fire, striking the suspect.

Saturday's gunfire also occurred nearly a month after what law enforcement described as an attempted assassination of the president on April 25 during the annual White House Correspondents' Association dinner at a Washington hotel.

SpaceX has confidentially filed for an initial public offering with US regulators, setting the stage for what could become one of the most valuable stock market debuts in history. According to reports from Bloomberg and the Wall Street Journal, Elon Musk's aerospace company could seek a valuation exceeding $1.75 trillion when it goes public.

The confidential filing allows the Securities and Exchange Commission time to review SpaceX's financial disclosures before they become available to investors and the general public. Bloomberg reported the IPO could occur as soon as June, during what is expected to be a strong year for high-profile public offerings.

SpaceX has emerged as the most valuable company in Musk's technology portfolio over the past decade. The firm has secured extensive and lucrative contracts with the US government for defense and space operations. NASA now relies on SpaceX rockets for the majority of its launches, making the space agency heavily dependent on the private company.

Beyond rocket manufacturing, SpaceX has established itself as a major force in satellite communications through its Starlink subsidiary. The satellite internet service is estimated to generate more than half of SpaceX's total revenue and has expanded Musk's geopolitical influence globally. Ukraine's military has relied on Starlink for combat operations and strategic planning, demonstrating the service's importance beyond commercial applications.

In a strategic move ahead of the IPO, SpaceX acquired Musk's artificial intelligence company xAI in February. The acquisition included plans to develop solar-powered datacenters in space, potentially addressing the computing and energy requirements driven by the AI industry boom. The AI firm, which owns the social media platform X and produces the Grok chatbot, was valued at $250 billion in the deal despite lagging behind competitors like OpenAI's ChatGPT and Anthropic's Claude.

SpaceX has recently shifted its priorities toward orbital datacenters and supporting NASA's upcoming moon landing missions. Musk's long-discussed ambitions to establish a civilization on Mars have become less prominent in the company's public messaging.

The company's financial details, which have remained closely guarded even from many investors, are expected to be disclosed closer to the IPO date. Share pricing and additional listing information will become public after SEC review of the filing is complete. SpaceX is working with several major international banks, including UK-based Barclays, to coordinate orders for the public offering.

A successful IPO would represent a significant achievement for Musk, who is already the world's wealthiest individual. The public offering could push him closer to becoming the first trillionaire. The listing comes after a turbulent year that included controversies surrounding federal government restructuring, Tesla protests, and investigations into his Grok chatbot regarding inappropriate image generation.

NASA is exploring options that could end the California Institute of Technology's management of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory for the first time in the facility's history. The review comes as the space agency evaluates its relationship with the research center, which Caltech has operated since JPL's founding.

The Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Pasadena has been managed by Caltech under a contract with NASA since the laboratory's establishment. JPL serves as NASA's primary center for robotic exploration of the solar system and has overseen numerous planetary missions and space technology developments.

NASA's consideration of alternative management structures represents a significant shift in how the agency operates one of its most prominent research facilities. The laboratory employs thousands of scientists and engineers who work on space missions, including Mars rovers, asteroid studies, and deep space exploration projects.

The potential change in management comes during a period when NASA is reassessing its operational partnerships and organizational structures. JPL has maintained its status as a federally funded research and development center throughout its history, with Caltech serving as the operating contractor.

Caltech's stewardship of JPL has spanned decades and includes oversight of major scientific achievements in planetary science and space technology. The university's connection to the laboratory has been a defining feature of both institutions, shaping research priorities and scientific collaboration.

The review process will examine different models for managing the laboratory's operations and research programs. NASA has not announced a timeline for making a final decision on JPL's future management structure.

Any change in JPL's management would affect not only the laboratory's daily operations but also its long-term research direction and its relationships with academic and industry partners. The facility's work includes developing spacecraft, managing active missions throughout the solar system, and advancing technologies for future space exploration.

NASA continues to evaluate how best to structure its relationship with JPL as the agency plans future missions and allocates resources across its various centers and programs.

The U.S. Senate has confirmed billionaire private astronaut Jared Isaacman as NASA's 15th administrator, marking a significant leadership change for the space agency. The confirmation vote passed 67-30, installing an advocate of Mars missions and close ally of SpaceX CEO Elon Musk at the helm of America's civilian space program.

Isaacman's path to confirmation proved unusually turbulent. President Donald Trump initially nominated him for the position, then withdrew the nomination in May during a public dispute with Musk, and finally re-nominated him in recent months. Trump cited Isaacman's donations to Democratic candidates as the reason for the initial withdrawal, posting the explanation on his Truth Social platform.

According to the Wall Street Journal, Isaacman worked behind the scenes to secure his position, tapping allies within the administration to promote his candidacy. His persistence paid off during his second confirmation hearing, where he told senators that NASA must accelerate its efforts to beat China back to the moon this decade.

The new administrator built his fortune by founding Shift4, a payment processing startup that now handles billions of transactions annually. His credibility in space exploration comes from personal experience. He has flown on two SpaceX missions, both self-funded with all-civilian crews. The most recent mission in 2024 achieved a historic milestone when his crew conducted the first spacewalk ever performed by a private company.

Isaacman's confirmation ended a power struggle over NASA's future. Acting NASA chief Sean Duffy, who simultaneously leads the U.S. Transportation Department, had been lobbying to move the space agency under his department's permanent control. Duffy congratulated Isaacman on Twitter/X after the confirmation, wishing him success and referencing plans to return to the moon in 2028 and beat China.

The leadership battle had created friction with Musk. Duffy's consolidation efforts coincided with his public statements that SpaceX had fallen behind on NASA contracts. Duffy looked toward competitors, particularly Jeff Bezos's Blue Origin, as potential alternatives. This stance put him at odds with both Musk and Isaacman, who maintains close ties to SpaceX.

Isaacman now faces the challenge of maintaining America's competitive edge in space exploration while managing complex relationships between NASA and private space companies. His confirmation signals a continued emphasis on commercial partnerships and ambitious goals, including the stated 2028 lunar return and longer-term Mars aspirations.

The 67-30 vote margin suggests bipartisan support despite the turbulent nomination process and Isaacman's political donations history. His hands-on space experience distinguishes him from many previous NASA administrators who came from primarily political or bureaucratic backgrounds.

Two-time NASCAR Cup Series champion Kyle Busch has died at age 41 after battling a severe illness that rapidly progressed, according to statements from NASCAR and his family.

The racing organization announced Busch's death on Thursday in a social media post expressing sadness over losing one of the sport's greatest competitors. NASCAR extended condolences to the Busch family, Richard Childress Racing, and the motorsports community.

On Saturday, the family released additional details about the cause of death. According to Dakota Hunter, vice president of Kyle Busch Companies, a medical evaluation revealed that Busch died after severe pneumonia progressed into sepsis, resulting in rapid and overwhelming complications.

The family had announced Busch's hospitalization earlier on Thursday, stating he would not compete in the scheduled NASCAR race in Charlotte, North Carolina that weekend. He had been planning to participate in Sunday's Coca-Cola 600.

During a NASCAR Cup Series race on May 10 at Watkins Glen, Busch had radioed his crew requesting medical assistance from Dr. Bill Heisel and a shot after the race. The television broadcast reported he was struggling with a sinus cold that worsened due to intense G-forces and elevation changes at the upstate New York road course. Despite feeling unwell, Busch finished the race in eighth place.

At the time of his death, Busch ranked 24th in the Cup Series standings with two top-10 finishes across 12 races this season. The Las Vegas native won championships in 2015 and 2019. He was in his fourth season with Richard Childress Racing after previously winning titles with Joe Gibbs Racing.

Busch holds the record for most wins across NASCAR's three national series with 232 victories. Just six days before his death, he won the trucks race at Dover on Friday.

A joint statement from the Busch family, Richard Childress Racing, and NASCAR called him a future Hall of Famer and a rare, once-in-a-generation talent. The statement praised his fierce passion, immense skill, and deep care for the sport and its fans.

Known by nicknames "Rowdy" and "Wild Thing," Busch was a polarizing figure recognized for post-race altercations, frequent feuds with other drivers, and sometimes outlandish behavior. The multi-talented driver made his Cup Series debut in 2005, winning rookie of the year honors. His sharp wit and competitive spirit created a devoted fan base known as "Rowdy Nation."

Busch is survived by his wife Samantha and their children Brexton and Lennix.

Romanian director Cristian Mungiu has claimed his second Palme d'Or at the Cannes Film Festival, nearly two decades after his first win. The 58-year-old filmmaker's English-language debut, Fjord, took home the prestigious award at the 79th edition of the festival, making Mungiu the 10th director in history to receive the top prize twice.

Fjord stars Renate Reinsve and Sebastian Stan as Romanian religious parents who move to Norway, where they face accusations of child abuse. The film follows Mungiu's 2007 triumph with 4 Months, 3 Weeks and 2 Days, his searing abortion drama that first put him in the exclusive club of two-time Palme winners. He joins an elite group that includes Francis Ford Coppola, Michael Haneke, Ken Loach, and Ruben Östlund, among others.

The Grand Prix went to Andrey Zvyagintsev for Minotaur, described as a dark satire exploring corruption and infidelity in contemporary Russia. Valeska Grisebach's The Dreamed Adventure, a Bulgaria-set drama about an archaeologist, earned third place with the Jury Prize.

The director award was shared between Pawel Pawlikowski for Fatherland and the duo of Javier Calvo and Javier Ambrossi for The Black Ball. In an unusual move, both acting prizes were split between pairs of performers. Virginie Efira and Tao Okamoto shared the best actress award for their roles in Ryusuke Hamaguchi's care drama All of a Sudden, while Valentin Campagne and Emmanuel Macchia took the best actor prize for the queer World War I drama Coward.

This year's festival was notably subdued, marked by disappointing showings from established auteurs and a lack of Hollywood glamour. The two American films in competition, James Gray's Paper Tiger starring Adam Driver and Scarlett Johansson, and Ira Sachs' AIDS musical The Man I Love with Rami Malek, were both overlooked by the jury.

US distributor Neon has already acquired Fjord, continuing their remarkable streak of securing the Palme winner for seven consecutive years. The company previously distributed Sean Baker's Anora, which won the Palme two years ago before sweeping the 2025 Oscars.

Park Chan-wook presided over this year's jury, which included Demi Moore, Stellan Skarsgård, Chloé Zhao, and Paul Laverty. During the closing ceremony, Isabelle Huppert paid tribute to Barbra Streisand, one of three honorary Palme d'Or recipients this year alongside Peter Jackson and John Travolta. Streisand, unable to attend due to a knee injury, sent a video message celebrating cinema's ability to unite people across the world.

In the sidebar competitions, Sandra Wollner's Everytime won Un Certain Regard, while Marie Clémentine Dusabejambo's Ben'Imana took the Camera d'Or for best first film.

A man who opened fire on Secret Service officers at a White House security checkpoint died after being shot by agents who returned fire, federal officials confirmed Saturday evening.

The White House was briefly placed under lockdown after multiple reporters heard a sustained volley of gunshots shortly after 6pm local time. The incident occurred near a checkpoint at a White House gate close to 17th Street and Pennsylvania Avenue NW.

According to a Secret Service statement, the man approached the checkpoint, drew a pistol, and began shooting at officers. Secret Service personnel returned fire, striking the suspect. He was transported to a local hospital where he later died.

A bystander on the street was also wounded during the exchange. The Secret Service said it remains unclear whether the bystander was hit by the suspect's initial gunfire or during the subsequent shootout between the gunman and officers. No Secret Service personnel were injured in the incident.

President Donald Trump was at the White House during the shooting but was not affected, according to officials. Trump later stated he was in the Oval Office during the incident but did not immediately comment on the gunfire.

ABC News White House correspondent Selina Wang captured the moment gunfire erupted while she was recording a report on US negotiations with Iran. Video shared on social media showed Wang diving for cover as what sounded like dozens of shots rang out. She reported that journalists were instructed to sprint to the press briefing room for safety.

Local news anchor Chris Flanagan, who also took cover in the White House briefing room, estimated that approximately 30 shots were fired. A CBS News producer similarly reported hearing at least 20 shots during the incident.

FBI Director Kash Patel confirmed that the FBI was on scene supporting the Secret Service response and pledged to update the public as more information became available. The security lockdown was lifted after the situation was contained.

The shooting marks the second violent incident near the White House in recent weeks. Less than three weeks earlier, a man brandishing a gun fired at a Secret Service officer near the route of Vice President JD Vance's motorcade outside the White House. In that incident, a civilian bystander was wounded in the leg, and officers returned fire, striking the suspect.

Saturday's gunfire also occurred nearly a month after what law enforcement described as an attempted assassination of the president on April 25 as he attended the annual White House Correspondents' Association dinner at a Washington hotel.

The investigation into Saturday's shooting continues as authorities work to determine the suspect's identity and motive.

6:29 PM ET

The World Health Organization has declared an Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Uganda a "public health emergency of international concern" as the death toll reaches 88 with more than 300 suspected cases.

WHO Director General Dr Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus announced the emergency declaration on Sunday before convening a formal emergency committee. The outbreak involves the Bundibugyo virus, one of three strains that can cause Ebola virus disease and the least common of them.

The situation is particularly concerning because there are no approved vaccines, treatments, or specific tests for the Bundibugyo strain. The speed of the WHO's declaration likely reflects the gravity of the outbreak, according to experts.

Dr Jean Kaseya, director general of the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention, expressed alarm at the situation. "Currently, I'm on panic mode because people are dying. I don't have medicines. I don't have [a] vaccine to support countries," he told Sky News.

The Africa CDC announced the outbreak publicly on Friday but said it remains unclear when it began. The organization is convening experts and is likely to announce a continental public health emergency in the coming days.

There have been 80 suspected deaths, eight laboratory-confirmed cases and 246 suspected cases in the DRC's Ituri province, which borders Uganda and South Sudan. Two cases, including one death, have been reported in Uganda's capital, Kampala, apparently in people who had traveled from the DRC.

The WHO warned that signs "all point towards a potentially much larger outbreak than what is currently being detected and reported, with significant local and regional risk of spread." The organization noted significant uncertainties about the true number of infected people and geographic spread.

On Sunday, a laboratory confirmed an additional Ebola case in Goma, a city in a separate part of the DRC affected by conflict and controlled by the Rwanda-backed M23 militia. Professor Jean-Jacques Muyembe, director of the Congolese National Institute for Biomedical Research, said the case involved a woman whose husband died of Ebola in Bunia and who traveled to Goma while already infected.

Journalists in Ituri's capital, Bunia, found residents fearful of the unusually high number of burials. "Every day, people are dying and this has been going on for about a week. In a single day, we bury two, three or even more people," said Jean Marc Asimwe, a Bunia resident. "At this point, we don't really know what kind of disease it is."

Kaseya emphasized that the outbreak highlights the need for vaccine and medicine manufacturing capacity in Africa, calling it an "equity issue." He warned that Western countries should understand that when Africa is affected, they are also at risk because people travel internationally every day.

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention said it was supporting the safe withdrawal of a small number of Americans directly affected by the outbreak.

Fjord stars Renate Reinsve and Sebastian Stan as Romanian religious parents who move to Norway, where they face accusations of child abuse. The film follows Mungiu's 2007 triumph with 4 Months, 3 Weeks and 2 Days, his searing abortion drama that first put him in the exclusive club of two-time Palme winners. He joins an elite group that includes Francis Ford Coppola, Michael Haneke, Ken Loach, and Ruben Östlund, among others.

The Grand Prix went to Andrey Zvyagintsev for Minotaur, described as a dark satire exploring corruption and infidelity in contemporary Russia. Valeska Grisebach's The Dreamed Adventure, a Bulgaria-set drama about an archaeologist, earned third place with the Jury Prize.

The director award was shared between Pawel Pawlikowski for Fatherland and the duo of Javier Calvo and Javier Ambrossi for The Black Ball. In an unusual move, both acting prizes were split between pairs of performers. Virginie Efira and Tao Okamoto shared the best actress award for their roles in Ryusuke Hamaguchi's care drama All of a Sudden, while Valentin Campagne and Emmanuel Macchia took the best actor prize for the queer World War I drama Coward.

This year's festival was notably subdued, marked by disappointing showings from established auteurs and a lack of Hollywood glamour. The two American films in competition, James Gray's Paper Tiger starring Adam Driver and Scarlett Johansson, and Ira Sachs' AIDS musical The Man I Love with Rami Malek, were both overlooked by the jury.

US distributor Neon has already acquired Fjord, continuing their remarkable streak of securing the Palme winner for seven consecutive years. The company previously distributed Sean Baker's Anora, which won the Palme two years ago before sweeping the 2025 Oscars.

Park Chan-wook presided over this year's jury, which included Demi Moore, Stellan Skarsgård, Chloé Zhao, and Paul Laverty. During the closing ceremony, Isabelle Huppert paid tribute to Barbra Streisand, one of three honorary Palme d'Or recipients this year alongside Peter Jackson and John Travolta. Streisand, unable to attend due to a knee injury, sent a video message celebrating cinema's ability to unite people across the world.

In the sidebar competitions, Sandra Wollner's Everytime won Un Certain Regard, while Marie Clémentine Dusabejambo's Ben'Imana took the Camera d'Or for best first film.
