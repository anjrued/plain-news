A federal court panel blocked Alabama from using a Republican-drawn congressional map, ruling that it was created with the intentional purpose of discriminating against Black voters. The decision prevents the state from implementing the 2023 map in the 2026 elections and represents another significant legal setback in Alabama's ongoing redistricting battles.

The three-judge panel, which included one Clinton appointee and two Trump appointees, found that Alabama's Republican-controlled legislature deliberately rejected court-ordered remedies to protect Black voting power. "We again cannot understand the 2023 Plan as anything other than intentionally discriminatory," the judges wrote in their opinion.

The case stems from a legal battle that began in 2021, when Black voters sued Alabama over its congressional map, arguing it diluted their voting influence. A court eventually ordered the state to create a new map with two majority-Black districts. When the legislature passed the 2023 plan instead, it contained only one majority-Black district, repeating the dilution problem the court had identified.

The judges noted that the legislature was fully aware of its discriminatory action. "The Legislature well knew that a plan without an additional Black-opportunity district would dilute Black Alabamians' opportunity to participate in the political process, and it intentionally enacted that very plan," they wrote.

The ruling is significant because it tests the limits of a recent US Supreme Court decision that weakened the Voting Rights Act. In April, the Supreme Court gutted a major provision of the law in a case called Louisiana v Callais. That decision prompted Alabama to move up its congressional primary and attempt to use the blocked 2023 map despite the ongoing legal challenge.

However, the federal panel determined that even under the Supreme Court's new standard, which allows challenges to maps drawn with intentional discriminatory intent, Alabama's actions crossed the line. This case will likely become the first test of whether the Supreme Court's recent ruling actually permits states to use maps explicitly designed to discriminate based on race.

Alabama's effort was part of a broader Republican strategy across the South following the Supreme Court's decision. Tennessee implemented a new map that eliminated a majority-Black district in Memphis. Louisiana is also moving to remove a majority-Black district, and South Carolina may follow. These efforts have drawn widespread criticism from Black leaders and civil rights organizations who say Republicans are repealing protections for minority voters.

The state must now redraw its congressional map before the 2026 elections. Alabama officials have not announced whether they will appeal the decision to the Supreme Court, though legal observers expect them to do so. The new map will affect all seven of Alabama's congressional seats and could potentially alter the partisan makeup of the state's House delegation.

BP has removed its chairman Albert Manifold following what the board described as "serious concerns" about governance standards, oversight, and conduct. The decision took effect immediately on Tuesday, marking the end of Manifold's brief tenure in the role after just eight months.

The company's announcement triggered a sharp market reaction, with BP shares initially falling 9 percent before recovering slightly to a 6 percent decline, making the stock the FTSE 100's worst performer on the day.

Amanda Blanc, BP's senior independent director and chief executive of insurance company Aviva, said the board had been "surprised and disappointed" by the issues that emerged. "Albert has helped bring a welcome focus and pace to BP's transformation. However, the board has been surprised and disappointed to learn of governance oversight and conduct issues it deems unacceptable and has taken decisive action."

Reports indicate that Manifold's conduct was viewed as overly aggressive by other board members. Directors also felt he attempted to exercise control more like an executive than a chairman, exceeding the boundaries of the role. Senior colleagues reportedly felt belittled by his approach, and newly installed chief executive Meg O'Neill objected to some of his interventions.

Manifold joined BP in October 2025 after leading Irish building materials company CRH. He was tasked with advancing the company's strategic shift back toward fossil fuel extraction while abandoning renewable energy investments. Shortly after arriving, he removed chief executive Murray Auchincloss, who had served for less than two years, and brought in O'Neill as his replacement in December.

This represents the second senior leadership departure at BP for conduct-related reasons in three years. Bernard Looney, Manifold's predecessor's predecessor, was forced out in September 2023 after failing to disclose sexual relationships with colleagues when he became chief executive.

Manifold had already faced investor criticism during his brief time as chairman. At his first annual meeting, 18 percent of votes were cast against his re-election after he blocked a shareholder resolution from Follow This, an environmental-focused investor group that sought reporting on how BP would protect shareholder value if oil and gas demand declines.

Mark van Baal, director of Follow This, said BP must ensure its new chairman brings expertise in governance and climate transition risk. "The new chair must bring real expertise in governance, climate risk and transition risk, otherwise nothing changes," he said.

The board has appointed Ian Tyler as interim chairman. Tyler is a former chief executive of infrastructure company Balfour Beatty and currently chairs building supplies company Grafton Group and serves as a director at mining company Anglo American.

This marks BP's third chairmanship change in two years, following Helge Lund's removal in 2025 amid investor pressure including from hedge fund Elliott Advisors.

Sonny Rollins, widely regarded as one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his website on Monday and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York, on Monday afternoon. No cause of death was disclosed.

Rollins was a towering figure in jazz history and one of the last surviving stars of the bebop generation. He fundamentally changed the direction of jazz alongside Miles Davis and Charlie Parker, transforming it from primarily dance and ballad music into something startlingly expressive and innovative. His genius lay in his melodic invention and improvisation, taking both jazz standards and his own compositions and reshaping them through extended, sometimes epic solos.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in Harlem, New York in 1930, he earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began playing saxophone at age seven, inspired by musically talented siblings and jazz heroes like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. His early high school band included future jazz stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew and Art Taylor. Rollins described his approach as "primitive," explaining he went "with my feelings more than my brain," an attitude that enabled him to break with convention and embrace improvisation.

Miles Davis wrote about how Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" because he was "an aggressive, innovative player who always had fresh musical ideas." Branford Marsalis later called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting Rollins with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, President Barack Obama said Rollins had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Rollins' prolific recording career began in 1953 and produced over 60 albums. His landmark recordings included "Saxophone Colossus" in 1956, featuring his signature tune "St Thomas," and "The Bridge" in 1962, which followed a famous three-year sabbatical during which he practiced for up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge's pedestrian walkway.

Beyond his musical innovations, Rollins was known for his artistic integrity and continuous evolution. He explored diverse styles including Latin music, avant-garde jazz, fusion and R&B throughout his career. He also composed the soundtrack to the 1966 film "Alfie" and campaigned for environmental awareness, releasing the album "Global Warming" in 1998 and organizing benefit concerts.

Rollins remained active well into his later years, touring and recording into his eighties. His death marks the loss of one of the last direct links to jazz's golden age and represents an immeasurable loss to American music.

The Trump administration has temporarily halted deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo due to a widening Ebola outbreak in the country. The pause was announced amid concerns that the flights could expose Immigration and Customs Enforcement personnel to the virus and potentially help transport the disease closer to the United States.

However, public health experts dispute whether the measure will actually prevent disease spread. The pause applies specifically to removal flights bound for Congo, though the administration has not announced similar suspensions for deportation flights to other countries. The announcement did not specify how long the suspension would remain in effect.

The decision has created complications for at least one woman who was moved to Kinshasa, the capital of the DRC, before officials announced the travel restrictions. Despite a judge's order for her return, officials say they will not bring her back because of the Ebola-related travel ban.

The Congo Ebola outbreak has also affected other international travel and events. The country's World Cup team cancelled the Kinshasa portion of its pre-tournament training due to health restrictions related to the outbreak, though the team has stated it still plans to travel to the United States for its World Cup preparations.

The intersection of public health emergencies and immigration enforcement has forced federal officials to navigate complex policy decisions. Deportation flights typically involve close quarters and international travel, creating potential vectors for disease transmission that officials must weigh against enforcement priorities.

ICE regularly conducts removal flights to countries worldwide as part of federal immigration enforcement operations. The agency coordinates with foreign governments to accept deported nationals, but health crises can disrupt these operations. The temporary Congo pause demonstrates how international health emergencies can force operational adjustments even in areas designated as high-priority policy.

This suspension comes as the Trump administration faces broader scrutiny of its deportation flight operations. A group of 40 House Democrats recently expressed serious concerns about the administration's secretive deportation flight program, demanding the Federal Aviation Administration provide transparency regarding ICE charter operations. The lawmakers cited credible reports of individuals placed on flights without notice to counsel or family members, raising due process concerns.

The letter from Democratic lawmakers, led by New Jersey congressman Rob Menendez, also referenced investigations revealing that the Trump administration transported detained immigrants in ways that routinely violated constitutional rights. The lawmakers asked the FAA for detailed reports on all ICE air operations since Trump took office, including flight origin and destination data.

The Trump administration has previously described claims of hidden or weaponized deportation flights as categorically false and argued its detention centers maintain higher standards than most U.S. prisons. The administration has also characterized its use of restraints on ICE flights as standard protocol designed to ensure safety.

Industry observers view the change as a response to the growing threat from AI chatbots and other alternative ways of accessing information. The traditional search box, largely unchanged since 1998, had become a defining feature of the internet itself.

The new interface integrates AI responses directly into search results, changing the experience from a list of links to a more conversational interaction. This represents a fundamental shift in Google's approach to organizing and presenting information.

The redesign affects hundreds of millions of daily search users worldwide. Google processes billions of searches each day, making any change to the search box interface one of the most significant updates in internet history.

Google framed the changes as the beginning of a new era for search, suggesting this is the first step in a longer transformation. The company indicated that artificial intelligence will play an increasingly central role in how search works going forward.

The timing of the announcement, 25 years after the search box first appeared, underscores both the longevity of the original design and the magnitude of the current changes. The search box had remained largely consistent through decades of other technological change.

BP has removed its chairman Albert Manifold following what the board described as "serious concerns" about governance standards, oversight, and conduct. The decision took effect immediately on Tuesday, marking the end of Manifold's brief tenure in the role after just eight months.

The company's announcement triggered a sharp market reaction, with BP shares initially falling 9 percent before recovering slightly to a 6 percent decline, making the stock the FTSE 100's worst performer on the day.

Amanda Blanc, BP's senior independent director and chief executive of insurance company Aviva, said the board had been "surprised and disappointed" by the issues that emerged. "Albert has helped bring a welcome focus and pace to BP's transformation. However, the board has been surprised and disappointed to learn of governance oversight and conduct issues it deems unacceptable and has taken decisive action."

Reports indicate that Manifold's conduct was viewed as overly aggressive by other board members. Directors also felt he attempted to exercise control more like an executive than a chairman, exceeding the boundaries of the role. Senior colleagues reportedly felt belittled by his approach, and newly installed chief executive Meg O'Neill objected to some of his interventions.

Manifold joined BP in October 2025 after leading Irish building materials company CRH. He was tasked with advancing the company's strategic shift back toward fossil fuel extraction while abandoning renewable energy investments. Shortly after arriving, he removed chief executive Murray Auchincloss, who had served for less than two years, and brought in O'Neill as his replacement in December.

This represents the second senior leadership departure at BP for conduct-related reasons in three years. Bernard Looney, Manifold's predecessor's predecessor, was forced out in September 2023 after failing to disclose sexual relationships with colleagues when he became chief executive.

Manifold had already faced investor criticism during his brief time as chairman. At his first annual meeting, 18 percent of votes were cast against his re-election after he blocked a shareholder resolution from Follow This, an environmental-focused investor group that sought reporting on how BP would protect shareholder value if oil and gas demand declines.

Mark van Baal, director of Follow This, said BP must ensure its new chairman brings expertise in governance and climate transition risk. "The new chair must bring real expertise in governance, climate risk and transition risk, otherwise nothing changes," he said.

The board has appointed Ian Tyler as interim chairman. Tyler is a former chief executive of infrastructure company Balfour Beatty and currently chairs building supplies company Grafton Group and serves as a director at mining company Anglo American.

This marks BP's third chairmanship change in two years, following Helge Lund's removal in 2025 amid investor pressure including from hedge fund Elliott Advisors.

Jazz saxophonist Sonny Rollins dies at 95

Sonny Rollins, widely regarded as one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his website on Monday and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in...

11:00 AM ET

Sonny Rollins, widely regarded as one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his website on Monday and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York, on Monday afternoon. No cause of death was disclosed.

Rollins was a towering figure in jazz history and one of the last surviving stars of the bebop generation. He fundamentally changed the direction of jazz alongside Miles Davis and Charlie Parker, transforming it from primarily dance and ballad music into something startlingly expressive and innovative. His genius lay in his melodic invention and improvisation, taking both jazz standards and his own compositions and reshaping them through extended, sometimes epic solos.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in Harlem, New York in 1930, he earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began playing saxophone at age seven, inspired by musically talented siblings and jazz heroes like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. His early high school band included future jazz stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew and Art Taylor. Rollins described his approach as "primitive," explaining he went "with my feelings more than my brain," an attitude that enabled him to break with convention and embrace improvisation.

Miles Davis wrote about how Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" because he was "an aggressive, innovative player who always had fresh musical ideas." Branford Marsalis later called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting Rollins with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, President Barack Obama said Rollins had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Rollins' prolific recording career began in 1953 and produced over 60 albums. His landmark recordings included "Saxophone Colossus" in 1956, featuring his signature tune "St Thomas," and "The Bridge" in 1962, which followed a famous three-year sabbatical during which he practiced for up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge's pedestrian walkway.

Beyond his musical innovations, Rollins was known for his artistic integrity and continuous evolution. He explored diverse styles including Latin music, avant-garde jazz, fusion and R&B throughout his career. He also composed the soundtrack to the 1966 film "Alfie" and campaigned for environmental awareness, releasing the album "Global Warming" in 1998 and organizing benefit concerts.

Rollins remained active well into his later years, touring and recording into his eighties. His death marks the loss of one of the last direct links to jazz's golden age and represents an immeasurable loss to American music.

The Knicks' path through the Eastern Conference has been unusually dominant. They swept the Philadelphia 76ers in the conference semifinals and defeated the Atlanta Hawks 4-1 in the first round. The victory over Cleveland extends their winning streak to 11 games, the third longest in a single postseason in NBA history.

Jalen Brunson emerged as the star of the Eastern Conference finals, averaging 25.5 points and 7.8 assists across the four-game series. He was named the Larry Bird Trophy winner as Eastern Conference finals MVP. After the final game, Brunson credited his teammates and coaching staff for the team's success. "It means a lot to get to the finals, but I wouldn't be here without my teammates," Brunson said on ESPN. His father Rick played for the Knicks during their last Finals appearance.

Teammate Josh Hart praised Brunson's leadership, noting his ability to keep the team grounded regardless of the margin. "He's an even keel, he doesn't let us get too high or too low," Hart said.

Monday's Game 4 was decided early when the Knicks went on a 20-0 run at the end of the first quarter and into the second. Karl-Anthony Towns led New York with 19 points and 14 rebounds. Cleveland's Donovan Mitchell scored 31 points but could not spark his team to a competitive challenge.

Cavaliers coach Kenny Atkinson acknowledged the Knicks' superior performance. "They're playing better basketball. You got to give them credit. They're on a heater," he said.

The Knicks will face either the defending champion Oklahoma City Thunder or the San Antonio Spurs in the Finals. The Western Conference finals were tied at 2-2, with Game 5 scheduled for Tuesday.

The Finals have already attracted significant attention in New York. High-profile celebrities including Spike Lee, Tracy Morgan and Timothée Chalamet attended Monday's game in Cleveland. Knicks guard Landry Shamet commented on the fan enthusiasm. "Knicks fans are a specific species of human that should be studied, they're crazy. They fly out to Cleveland on a Monday, they're everywhere," he said.

The first home game of the Finals will take place on June 8. Courtside seats for that matchup are already being listed at $105,000 on resale sites, reflecting the enormous demand from fans eager to see their team's first championship push since 1973.
