The United States military carried out strikes on Iranian targets, including missile launch sites and boats near the Strait of Hormuz, as negotiations toward a potential peace deal continue. US Central Command characterized the operations as self-defense actions.

The timing of the strikes reflects the volatile state of current US-Iran relations. Just hours before the military action, President Trump announced that negotiations were moving forward and paused Project Freedom, his initiative to reopen the Strait of Hormuz, to allow space for peace talks.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio stated that a potential agreement could take days to finalize. "The operation is over," Rubio said about the initial military campaign, nicknamed Epic Fury. "We're done with that stage of it."

However, Iranian officials have expressed skepticism about the pace of progress. Foreign ministry spokesperson Esmail Baghaei said it is correct that negotiators have reached conclusions on many issues, but cautioned against expectations of an imminent agreement. "To say that this means the signing of an agreement is imminent, no one can make such a claim," he stated.

Qatar has become the key intermediary in talks. Senior Iranian negotiators traveled to Doha for discussions focused on the Hormuz situation and other points of contention between Washington and Tehran. The negotiations address the reopening of the waterway through which much of the world's oil passes, frozen Iranian assets held abroad, and other security matters.

Trump has linked any Iran agreement to expansion of the Abraham Accords, the normalization agreements between Israel and several Arab nations. Trump asked Muslim leaders to sign peace deals with Israel once a resolution to the Iran conflict is reached. This dual-track approach aims to achieve both an Iran deal and broader Middle East diplomatic arrangements.

The proposed framework under discussion would address the Strait of Hormuz passage, frozen Iranian assets, and potentially other security matters. Both sides have downplayed the immediate likelihood of a breakthrough, though Trump expressed optimism about progress being made.

Earlier in the week, tensions spiked when the US military reported destroying Iranian boats and intercepting cruise missiles and drones near the strait. Iran denied these claims. The United Arab Emirates reported coming under attack from Iranian missiles and drones, which Iran also denied.

Trump threatened severe consequences if Iran attacked US vessels involved in reopening the strait. "If they don't agree, the bombing starts, and it will be, sadly, at a much higher level and intensity than it was before," he wrote on Truth Social.

The current situation remains tense despite the ceasefire that has technically been in place since early April. Oil prices have fluctuated based on negotiations, trading around 109 to 114 dollars per barrel depending on optimism about a deal.

Firefighters in Orange County, California have eliminated the threat of an explosion from a damaged chemical tank, allowing authorities to begin lifting evacuation orders for thousands of residents affected by the emergency.

The hazardous chemical tank, located in the southern California region, had cracked over the weekend, causing initial concerns about a potential explosion. However, officials determined that the crack actually helped relieve pressure in the tank, reducing the risk of a catastrophic blast.

Firefighters successfully stabilized the overheating tank through overnight cooling and continuous monitoring operations. The combination of these efforts proved effective in eliminating the immediate explosion danger, according to Orange County fire officials.

Despite the positive development regarding the explosion threat, authorities emphasized that the overall crisis remains ongoing. The situation continues to require active management and oversight as officials assess the full scope of the chemical leak and its impact on surrounding communities.

Evacuation orders have been partially lifted, permitting some residents to return to their homes. However, not all residents affected by the emergency have been cleared to leave their evacuation locations. Thousands of people had been displaced from their homes during the initial response to the chemical tank failure.

The incident prompted a significant emergency response across multiple Orange County communities, one of California's most densely populated regions. Local emergency management teams coordinated the large-scale evacuation and are now overseeing the phased return of residents to their neighborhoods.

Officials have not yet announced a complete timeline for when all evacuation orders will be fully lifted or when the chemical tank situation will reach final resolution. The damaged tank continues to be monitored closely as authorities work to ensure the safety of nearby residents and communities.

Local emergency management continues to urge residents to follow official guidance as the response effort progresses. The coordination between firefighting teams, emergency management officials, and law enforcement agencies remains focused on both addressing the immediate hazard and supporting residents through the recovery process.

The chemical leak represents a significant challenge for Orange County's emergency response infrastructure. The successful elimination of the explosion threat marks an important milestone in the crisis response, though officials stress that residents should remain alert and prepared to follow any additional instructions from local authorities.

As the situation continues to develop, officials will provide updates on evacuation order changes and the overall status of the damaged chemical tank. Residents in affected areas are encouraged to monitor official channels for the latest information regarding when they can safely return to their homes.

SpaceX has filed for an initial public offering on the US stock market, moving forward with plans to become a publicly traded company. The rocket and satellite operations company confidentially submitted its filing last month, allowing regulators time to review financial disclosures before public disclosure.

The company plans to list on the Nasdaq exchange under the symbol SPCX, with a proposed valuation of approximately $1.75 trillion. SpaceX is seeking up to $80 billion in investment and is expected to begin trading around June 12, marking one of the most closely watched and highly valued listings in market history.

SpaceX operates multiple revenue-generating businesses. The company dominates the commercial rocket launch market and has signed extensive, lucrative contracts with the US government for defense and space travel. NASA relies heavily on SpaceX rockets for the majority of its launches. Through its Starlink subsidiary, SpaceX has become a major satellite communications operator, ranking among the largest satellite operators globally. The satellite internet service is estimated to generate over half of SpaceX's revenue.

The company expanded its operations in February by acquiring Elon Musk's artificial intelligence firm xAI, valued at $250 billion in the transaction. SpaceX cited plans to build solar-powered datacenters in space to support AI computing and energy demands. The company has recently shifted focus toward orbital datacenters and supporting NASA's upcoming moon landing missions.

Details about SpaceX's finances, which have remained tightly held secrets even from most investors, are now becoming public through the SEC filing. The company is working with prominent international banks, including Barclays in the UK, to coordinate orders for the IPO.

The public offering comes as SpaceX stands as the most valuable enterprise in Musk's tech portfolio. Over the past decade, the company has grown to become a dominant force in both space travel and satellite communications, securing its position as the world's most prominent rocket maker.

The timing coincides with other major tech IPOs. Rival company OpenAI is also planning to go public later this year at an immense valuation, having recently closed a funding round of $122 billion. AI firm Anthropic is preparing its own IPO as well.

For Musk, already the world's richest person, a successful SpaceX public offering would significantly expand his wealth and bring him closer to becoming the world's first trillionaire.

The exact share price and number of shares to be offered will be finalized after the Securities and Exchange Commission completes its review of the registration statement. SpaceX will begin trading on the Nasdaq once regulators approve the filing and the company finalizes pricing with underwriters.

Google Quietly Removes AI Health Feature After Safety Concerns

Google has discontinued "What People Suggest," an artificial intelligence feature that crowdsourced health advice from ordinary users online. The company had framed the feature as demonstrating "the potential of AI to transform health outcomes across the globe" when it launched, but has since scrapped it without public announcement.

A Google spokesperson confirmed the removal, characterizing it as part of a "broader simplification" of the search results page. The company insisted the decision had "nothing to do with the quality or safety of the feature."

The feature's removal comes amid growing scrutiny of Google's use of artificial intelligence to deliver health information to millions of users. In January, the Guardian reported that people faced potential harm from false and misleading health information in Google AI Overviews, summaries generated by artificial intelligence that appear above traditional search results. These summaries reach approximately 2 billion people monthly on the world's most visited website.

Google initially downplayed the Guardian's investigation, noting that AI Overviews linked to reputable sources and recommended seeking expert advice. The company subsequently removed AI Overviews for some medical queries, though not all.

At a Google event called "The Check Up" in March, the company unveiled "What People Suggest" as a new medical feature. Karen DeSalvo, then Google's chief health officer, wrote that while people valued reliable medical information from experts, they also appreciated hearing from those with similar experiences. The feature used artificial intelligence to organize perspectives from online discussions into themes, allowing users to see how others with conditions like arthritis managed their health.

"What People Suggest" was initially available only on mobile devices in the United States. According to three people familiar with the decision, the feature has since been discontinued.

When asked where the removal was announced publicly, a Google spokesperson pointed to a November blog post by John Mueller, a search advocate at Google Switzerland. The post makes no mention of "What People Suggest."

The spokesperson maintained that the feature's discontinuation was unrelated to safety concerns, adding that Google continues to help users find reliable health information from various sources, including forums with firsthand perspectives.

Google plans to hold another "The Check Up" event this week, where Chief Health Officer Michael Howell and other staff will discuss how the company is combining new artificial intelligence research, technological innovations, and partnerships to address major global health challenges.

The quiet removal of "What People Suggest" highlights tensions between Google's efforts to integrate artificial intelligence throughout its products and growing concerns about the accuracy and safety of AI-generated health information.

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonist improvisers of all time, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his website on Monday at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided.

Rollins spent more than seven decades transforming jazz through his innovative approach to melody and improvisation. He was among the last surviving figures from the bebop generation, a movement that took jazz into startlingly expressive new territory. Born Walter Theodore Rollins in Harlem in 1930, he earned his nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began learning saxophone at age seven, inspired by musical siblings and jazz icons like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller.

His early career was marked by both brilliance and struggle. He recorded his debut album as bandleader in 1953 and released 17 more recordings by the end of the 1950s, including landmark albums like "Saxophone Colossus" (1956) and "Freedom Suite" (1958). However, heroin addiction derailed his progress in the early 1950s. After committing armed robbery to support his habit, he served 10 months on Rikers Island but successfully recovered through a rehabilitation program in 1955.

His comeback sparked exceptional creativity. Rollins became known for his bright, catchy melodic lines that he would deconstruct and refashion into sometimes epic improvisations. Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong.

In 1959, Rollins took a three-year break from public performance and recording, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway in New York. This period, meant partly to avoid disturbing neighbors, inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge," which became legendary in jazz history.

Throughout the following decades, Rollins continued evolving his sound. He explored avant-garde and fusion styles, incorporated Latin American and funk influences, and even contributed uncredited saxophone to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album "Tattoo You." He took another sabbatical between 1969 and 1971 to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Rollins composed numerous jazz standards including "Oleo," "Doxy," and "Airegin." He also wrote and performed the soundtrack to the 1966 Michael Caine film "Alfie." In later years, he campaigned on climate issues, releasing the 1998 album "Global Warming."

Rollins received major honors including a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award, Kennedy Center Honor, and the National Medal of the Arts. When presenting him the 2010 National Medal of the Arts in 2011, President Barack Obama said Rollins had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

He married twice, to Dawn Finney briefly in 1957 and to Lucille Pearson in 1965, remaining married until her death in 2004.

Trump administration pauses deportation flights to Congo over Ebola outbreak

The pause reflects health concerns about potential exposure to Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials involved in the removal operations. However, public health experts question whether the measure will effectively prev...

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily halted deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo as an Ebola outbreak spreads across the country. The pause reflects health concerns about potential exposure to Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials involved in the removal operations.

However, public health experts question whether the measure will effectively prevent disease spread. The temporary nature of the suspension means deportation flights could resume once health conditions change, though the administration has not specified what criteria must be met before operations resume.

The pause creates complications for some individuals already in the deportation pipeline. At least one woman has been moved to Kinshasa, the capital of the DRC, and officials now say they will not bring her back because of the Ebola travel restrictions, despite a judge's order requiring her return. This situation highlights tensions between immigration enforcement and public health protocols.

The decision to pause Congo flights comes as the Trump administration continues to significantly expand its deportation flight operations overall. Human rights groups tracking these flights have documented an 84 percent increase in ICE air operations during 2025 compared to the previous year. These expanded operations have drawn scrutiny from lawmakers and advocacy organizations.

Democratic members of Congress have raised serious concerns about the administration's deportation flight program. Forty House Democrats sent a letter to the Federal Aviation Administration calling for transparency and accountability. The lawmakers described the program as secretive and pointed to allegations of mistreatment and inhumane conditions aboard ICE charter jets.

According to reporting by the Guardian based on leaked flight data, detained immigrants have been transported in ways that routinely violated their constitutional rights. The letter references investigations identifying allegations of abuse at detention centers involved in the deportation program.

The lawmakers specifically asked the FAA to investigate how the administration assesses humanitarian conditions on these flights. They raised questions about the controversial use of full body restraints during deportation operations and whether such restraints affect emergency evacuation procedures.

The Trump administration has previously defended its detention and deportation practices, describing restraint use as a long-standing safety protocol and arguing that its detention centers maintain higher standards than most American prisons. Officials have also described claims of hidden flights as categorically false.

New Jersey Congressman Rob Menendez, who led the congressional letter, emphasized the importance of public understanding. "We want people to understand what is happening on their dime," he said, noting that violations of due process and legal rights occur regularly in these operations.

The Congo flight pause represents a temporary accommodation to immediate health concerns rather than a broader policy shift regarding deportation operations or transparency around them.

Google Quietly Removes AI Health Feature After Safety Concerns

Google has discontinued "What People Suggest," an artificial intelligence feature that crowdsourced health advice from ordinary users online. The company had framed the feature as demonstrating "the potential of AI to transform health outcomes across the globe" when it launched, but has since scrapped it without public announcement.

A Google spokesperson confirmed the removal, characterizing it as part of a "broader simplification" of the search results page. The company insisted the decision had "nothing to do with the quality or safety of the feature."

The feature's removal comes amid growing scrutiny of Google's use of artificial intelligence to deliver health information to millions of users. In January, the Guardian reported that people faced potential harm from false and misleading health information in Google AI Overviews, summaries generated by artificial intelligence that appear above traditional search results. These summaries reach approximately 2 billion people monthly on the world's most visited website.

Google initially downplayed the Guardian's investigation, noting that AI Overviews linked to reputable sources and recommended seeking expert advice. The company subsequently removed AI Overviews for some medical queries, though not all.

At a Google event called "The Check Up" in March, the company unveiled "What People Suggest" as a new medical feature. Karen DeSalvo, then Google's chief health officer, wrote that while people valued reliable medical information from experts, they also appreciated hearing from those with similar experiences. The feature used artificial intelligence to organize perspectives from online discussions into themes, allowing users to see how others with conditions like arthritis managed their health.

"What People Suggest" was initially available only on mobile devices in the United States. According to three people familiar with the decision, the feature has since been discontinued.

When asked where the removal was announced publicly, a Google spokesperson pointed to a November blog post by John Mueller, a search advocate at Google Switzerland. The post makes no mention of "What People Suggest."

The spokesperson maintained that the feature's discontinuation was unrelated to safety concerns, adding that Google continues to help users find reliable health information from various sources, including forums with firsthand perspectives.

Google plans to hold another "The Check Up" event this week, where Chief Health Officer Michael Howell and other staff will discuss how the company is combining new artificial intelligence research, technological innovations, and partnerships to address major global health challenges.

The quiet removal of "What People Suggest" highlights tensions between Google's efforts to integrate artificial intelligence throughout its products and growing concerns about the accuracy and safety of AI-generated health information.

Jazz saxophonist Sonny Rollins dies at 95

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonist improvisers of all time, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his website on Monday at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided. Rolli...

2:00 AM ET

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonist improvisers of all time, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his website on Monday at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided.

Rollins spent more than seven decades transforming jazz through his innovative approach to melody and improvisation. He was among the last surviving figures from the bebop generation, a movement that took jazz into startlingly expressive new territory. Born Walter Theodore Rollins in Harlem in 1930, he earned his nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began learning saxophone at age seven, inspired by musical siblings and jazz icons like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller.

His early career was marked by both brilliance and struggle. He recorded his debut album as bandleader in 1953 and released 17 more recordings by the end of the 1950s, including landmark albums like "Saxophone Colossus" (1956) and "Freedom Suite" (1958). However, heroin addiction derailed his progress in the early 1950s. After committing armed robbery to support his habit, he served 10 months on Rikers Island but successfully recovered through a rehabilitation program in 1955.

His comeback sparked exceptional creativity. Rollins became known for his bright, catchy melodic lines that he would deconstruct and refashion into sometimes epic improvisations. Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong.

In 1959, Rollins took a three-year break from public performance and recording, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway in New York. This period, meant partly to avoid disturbing neighbors, inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge," which became legendary in jazz history.

Throughout the following decades, Rollins continued evolving his sound. He explored avant-garde and fusion styles, incorporated Latin American and funk influences, and even contributed uncredited saxophone to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album "Tattoo You." He took another sabbatical between 1969 and 1971 to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Rollins composed numerous jazz standards including "Oleo," "Doxy," and "Airegin." He also wrote and performed the soundtrack to the 1966 Michael Caine film "Alfie." In later years, he campaigned on climate issues, releasing the 1998 album "Global Warming."

Rollins received major honors including a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award, Kennedy Center Honor, and the National Medal of the Arts. When presenting him the 2010 National Medal of the Arts in 2011, President Barack Obama said Rollins had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

He married twice, to Dawn Finney briefly in 1957 and to Lucille Pearson in 1965, remaining married until her death in 2004.
