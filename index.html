Tulsi Gabbard is leaving her post as US director of national intelligence following a tumultuous stint in which she was largely sidelined as Donald Trump launched attacks on Venezuela and Iran.

In a letter to the US president, she said she would resign and leave her post on 30 June. "While we have made significant progress … I recognize there is still important work to be done," she wrote.

The White House forced Gabbard to resign, the Reuters news agency reported, citing a source familiar with the issue. However, a spokesperson for Gabbard's office said rumors that Gabbard had been forcibly ousted from office were "100% false".

In her resignation letter, Gabbard said her husband, Abraham, "has recently been diagnosed with an extremely rare form of bone cancer". She added: "At this time, I must step away from public service to be by his side and fully support him through this battle."

Gabbard was an unconventional choice for the role. The former Democrat lacked an intelligence background and her foreign policy views at times diverged with Trump's, particularly on questions of overseas military intervention. She also took unusual measures to ingratiate herself to the president by vowing to root out politicization across US spy agencies and embracing his agenda of election denial.

Over time, Gabbard became increasingly sidelined by the president, who excluded her from key national security conversations on Iran and Venezuela, according to people familiar with the office of the director of national intelligence.

Trump was asking cabinet members last month whether he should replace Gabbard, according to two people briefed on the discussions.

"Unfortunately, after having done a great job, Tulsi Gabbard will be leaving the Administration on June 30th," he wrote in a statement on his Truth Social platform on Friday. Gabbard "has done an incredible job, and we will miss her", the president said, adding that Aaron Lukas, principal deputy director of national intelligence, would serve as acting director of national intelligence.

Gabbard's tumultuous 15-month tenure as the nation's top intelligence official ended Friday when she submitted her resignation. During her time in office, she took actions seemingly meant to flatter Trump despite her foreign policy views at times diverging with his, particularly on questions of overseas military intervention.

The resignation marks another departure from Trump's cabinet during a period of military operations and heightened international tensions. Aaron Lukas will now take over the role in an acting capacity as the administration continues its operations in Venezuela and Iran.

Yesterday, 7:55 PM ET

NASA is evaluating whether to end Caltech's management of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, a relationship that has lasted since the laboratory's founding in 1936. The review comes as the space agency examines its operations and institutional partnerships.

The Jet Propulsion Laboratory has been operated by the California Institute of Technology under contract with NASA since the federal space agency's creation. JPL manages many of NASA's robotic space missions and serves as a primary center for planetary exploration and Earth science research.

The potential change in management would mark a significant shift in how one of NASA's most important research facilities operates. JPL has been responsible for numerous missions, including the Mars rovers and various deep space exploration programs.

Caltech has managed the facility through decades of space exploration milestones. The laboratory's work has included developing spacecraft, conducting planetary science research, and operating NASA's Deep Space Network, which communicates with spacecraft throughout the solar system.

The review reflects NASA's broader assessment of its institutional structure and operational partnerships. The space agency periodically evaluates its relationships with outside organizations that manage its facilities and programs.

If NASA decides to change JPL's management structure, it would need to determine an alternative approach for operating the facility. Options could include direct federal management or contracting with a different organization.

The laboratory employs thousands of scientists, engineers, and support staff who work on various space missions and research projects. Any management change would need to account for the facility's ongoing operations and its role in current and planned NASA missions.

No final decision has been announced regarding JPL's future management structure. The review process will examine various factors including operational efficiency, scientific output, and alignment with NASA's long-term goals for space exploration and research.

The rapper's influence extended beyond his own recordings. "It Takes Two" has been sampled and referenced by numerous artists over the decades and remains a touchstone of late-1980s hip-hop. The track's enduring popularity helped establish sampling as a legitimate artistic practice in popular music.

Base was part of a generation of hip-hop artists who bridged the gap between the genre's early pioneers and its explosion into mainstream culture in the 1990s. Alongside contemporaries like DJ Jazzy Jeff and the Fresh Prince, Tone Loc, and Young MC, he helped prove that hip-hop could achieve commercial success without compromising its essential character.

The circumstances of his death have not been disclosed. Base leaves behind a legacy centered on one of hip-hop's most enduring party anthems, a song that introduced millions of listeners to the genre and remains instantly recognizable nearly four decades after its release.
