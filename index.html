A few minutes ago

Donald Trump claimed on Saturday that a peace deal with Iran "has been largely negotiated" after discussions with Pakistani mediators, Gulf allies and Israel, potentially signaling an end to the war launched by the US and Israel in February.

The president wrote on his social media platform that "final aspects and details" of a "memorandum of understanding" were still being discussed and "will be announced shortly." Trump said the Strait of Hormuz would be opened as part of the deal.

However, Trump's optimistic assessment appeared at odds with statements from mediators and Iran itself. Pakistan's prime minister expressed hopes of hosting more talks "very soon," while Iran's Fars news agency reported that the Strait of Hormuz would remain under Iranian control.

The announcement came after Trump spoke with Benjamin Netanyahu before an Israeli security cabinet meeting focused on Iran, Lebanon and Gaza. There has been growing speculation in Israel that fighting with Iran could resume if there are no signs of compromise.

As the deadlocked war enters its fourth month, international observers have warned of mounting humanitarian, economic and geopolitical consequences. Comparisons to previous US military quagmires in Iraq, Afghanistan and Vietnam have become increasingly common. Some analysts now warn that the negative global impact from this conflict could prove more permanently damaging than those past US interventions.

Regional tensions remain high following a recent incident in which the United Arab Emirates blamed a fire near its nuclear power plant on a drone launched by Iran or one of its proxies. The UAE described the incident as a "dangerous escalation."

According to Axios, Trump is scheduled to meet with his national security team to discuss next steps regarding Iran. The president had previously posted on social media expressing impatience with the pace of peace talks, warning Iran that "the Clock is Ticking" and urging them to "get moving, FAST, or there won't be anything left of them."

The conflict has created what observers describe as an ever-expanding catastrophe, with millions being dragged into starvation and economic disruption rippling across global markets. Critics have questioned who will restrain Trump if he decides to escalate military action further.

Reports from CNN, the New York Times and other outlets confirmed Trump's claims about progress toward a deal, though they also noted continued uncertainty about the final terms. The BBC reported that Tehran has signaled progress on talks but indicated that the key issue of nuclear weapons is not part of the initial framework being discussed.

The proposed agreement would reportedly include a two-month period for negotiations on Iran's nuclear program, though specific details remain unclear as talks continue.

A few minutes ago

A gunman died after approaching a White House security checkpoint and opening fire on officers Saturday evening, federal officials confirmed. The incident prompted a brief lockdown of the White House while President Donald Trump was present.

The shooting occurred shortly after 6 p.m. local time when a man approached a checkpoint at a White House gate near 17th Street and Pennsylvania Avenue NW. According to the Secret Service, the suspect drew a pistol and fired at officers. Secret Service personnel returned fire, striking the suspect, who was transported to a hospital where he later died.

A bystander was also shot during the incident. The Secret Service said it remains unclear whether the bystander was struck by the suspect's initial gunfire or during the subsequent exchange between the gunman and officers. No Secret Service personnel were injured, and the president was not affected by the incident.

The sustained volley of gunshots sent journalists in the area running for cover. Selina Wang, a White House correspondent for ABC News, shared video on social media showing the moment gunfire interrupted her as she recorded a report on US negotiations with Iran. In the clip, Wang dove for cover as what sounded like dozens of shots rang out. She and other journalists were told to sprint to the press briefing room.

Chris Flanagan, an anchor for DC News Now who took cover in the White House briefing room, reported that about 30 shots were fired. A CBS News producer estimated at least 20 shots had been fired. The security lockdown was lifted after the situation was contained.

Hours after the shooting, Trump praised the swift and professional action of law enforcement officials in a social media post. The president noted that the incident came a month after a shooting at the White House Correspondents' Association dinner at a Washington hotel. He added that it demonstrates the importance of creating the most safe and secure space of its kind ever built in Washington, in an apparent reference to his ballroom plans.

The suspect has not been officially named, but multiple US media outlets reported that he had a history of mental health issues. He had been arrested last year trying to enter via a different White House checkpoint without authorization and had been issued a stay away order.

FBI Director Kash Patel confirmed the FBI was on the scene supporting the Secret Service response to the shots fired near White House grounds.

The shooting comes less than three weeks after a previous incident in which a man brandishing a gun fired at a Secret Service officer near the route of Vice President JD Vance's motorcade outside the White House. That incident wounded a civilian witness in the leg.

Yesterday, 4:00 AM ET

SpaceX has filed for an initial public offering, with financial documents revealing the company is positioning its Starlink satellite internet service as its primary business focus rather than rocket launches. The filing marks a significant shift in how Elon Musk's space company presents its business priorities to potential investors.

The IPO documents show SpaceX betting on Starlink as its path to future growth and profitability. Starlink provides internet service through a network of thousands of low-orbit satellites, serving customers in remote and underserved areas around the world.

The filing comes as SpaceX has expanded Starlink's customer base and infrastructure substantially in recent years. The satellite internet service now operates in dozens of countries and has attracted both residential and commercial customers.

SpaceX's rocket business, which includes launching commercial satellites and NASA missions, has been the company's public face since its founding. The Falcon 9 rocket has become the most frequently launched orbital rocket globally, and SpaceX holds contracts with NASA for crew and cargo missions to the International Space Station.

However, the IPO filing indicates the company sees Starlink as offering greater long-term revenue potential through recurring subscription fees compared to the per-launch payment model of the rocket business. Satellite internet represents a large addressable market of customers without reliable broadband access.

The decision to go public follows years of speculation about when SpaceX might pursue an IPO. Musk has previously stated he wanted to wait until Starlink's revenue became more predictable before taking the company public. The filing suggests SpaceX believes that threshold has been reached.

SpaceX continues to develop its Starship rocket, designed for missions to the Moon and Mars. The company recently conducted a test flight of Starship V3, which sources described as mostly successful despite remaining a work in progress. That test flight occurred on the same day as the IPO filing.

The IPO will give public investors their first opportunity to own shares in SpaceX, which has been privately held since its founding in 2002. The filing does not yet specify how many shares will be offered or at what price range.

The investigation examined the technical issues that led to the launch failure. Blue Origin has been working to develop New Glenn as part of its efforts to establish a presence in the commercial satellite launch market.

The rocket is intended to serve both commercial and government customers, with contracts including missions for NASA and satellite operators. New Glenn features a reusable first stage, which Blue Origin plans to land on a ship at sea after launch.

Blue Origin, founded by Jeff Bezos, has been developing the New Glenn system for several years. The rocket stands taller than the company's New Shepard vehicle and is designed to carry significantly heavier payloads to orbit.

The completion of the investigation marks a step in the company's process of addressing the technical challenges that caused the failure. Blue Origin has not announced a timeline for the next New Glenn launch attempt.

The failure investigation is part of standard aerospace industry practice following launch anomalies. Companies typically conduct thorough technical reviews to identify root causes and implement corrective measures before resuming flight operations.

New Glenn is named after John Glenn, the first American to orbit Earth. The rocket's development has been closely watched within the space industry as Blue Origin works to expand its role in commercial spaceflight.

The US Senate confirmed Isaacman in a 67-30 vote, ending an unusual nomination process that saw President Donald Trump initially nominate him, then withdraw the nomination, before renominating him again. The confirmation came two weeks after Isaacman told senators during his second hearing that NASA must accelerate its efforts to beat China back to the moon this decade.

Isaacman's path to confirmation was marked by political turbulence. His nomination was previously pulled back in May during a public dispute between Elon Musk and Trump. At the time, Trump posted on his Truth Social platform that Isaacman's donations to Democratic candidates were the reasons behind withdrawing the nomination. According to the Wall Street Journal, Isaacman tapped allies within the administration to promote his candidacy when he re-emerged as a candidate in recent months.

The confirmation resolved a bureaucratic battle over NASA's organizational structure. Sean Duffy, who served as acting NASA chief while also leading the US transportation department, had been lobbying to move the space agency under his department's permanent control. Duffy's consolidation efforts sparked conflict with Musk around the same time that the transport secretary said SpaceX had fallen behind on its contracts with NASA. Duffy had looked to competitors, particularly Jeff Bezos's Blue Origin, for alternatives.

Despite the earlier tensions, Duffy congratulated Isaacman on Twitter, wishing him success and referencing NASA's goal to return to the moon in 2028 and beat China.

Isaacman brings a unique background to NASA leadership. His fortune came from founding Shift4, a payment processing startup that now handles billions of transactions annually. He is also an experienced space traveler, having flown on two SpaceX missions that he funded himself with all-civilian crews. His most recent flight was in 2024, a mission notable for conducting the first spacewalk by a private company.

As an advocate of Mars missions and an ally of SpaceX CEO Elon Musk, Isaacman's confirmation signals a continued emphasis on ambitious deep space exploration goals. His tenure begins as NASA works toward its 2028 lunar return target, with the administrator emphasizing the urgency of maintaining American leadership in space amid increasing international competition, particularly from China's expanding space program.

Romanian director Cristian Mungiu has won the Palme d'Or at the Cannes Film Festival for his English-language debut "Fjord," marking his second time receiving the prestigious award. The 58-year-old filmmaker previously won the top prize 19 years ago for his abortion drama "4 Months, 3 Weeks and 2 Days."

"Fjord" stars Renate Reinsve and Sebastian Stan as Romanian religious parents who move to Norway, where they face accusations of child abuse. The film has already been acquired by US distributor Neon, marking the seventh consecutive year the company has taken home the top prize from Cannes.

Mungiu now joins an exclusive group as the 10th director to receive two Palmes d'Or. The list includes notable filmmakers such as Francis Ford Coppola, Michael Haneke, Ken Loach, the Dardenne brothers, and Ruben Östlund.

The runner-up Grand Prix was awarded to Andrey Zvyagintsev's "Minotaur," described as a dark satire examining corruption and infidelity in contemporary Russia. Valeska Grisebach's "The Dreamed Adventure," a Bulgaria-set drama about an archaeologist, took third place with the Jury prize.

The director award was split between Pawel Pawlikowski for "Fatherland" and Javier Calvo and Javier Ambrossi for "The Black Ball." Both the actor and actress awards were also shared between pairs of performers. Virginie Efira and Tao Okamoto won best actress for Ryusuke Hamaguchi's care drama "All of a Sudden," while Valentin Campagne and Emmanuel Macchia received the best actor prize for the queer First World War drama "Coward."

The 79th Cannes Film Festival was notably subdued, marked by disappointing returns from various auteurs and a lack of Hollywood glamour. The two American films in competition, James Gray's "Paper Tiger" starring Adam Driver and Scarlett Johansson, and Ira Sachs' AIDS musical "The Man I Love" starring Rami Malek, were both overlooked by the judges.

Park Chan-wook presided over this year's jury, which included Demi Moore, Stellan Skarsgård, Chloé Zhao, and Paul Laverty. During the closing ceremony, Isabelle Huppert paid special tribute to Barbra Streisand, who was one of three honorary Palme d'Or recipients this year alongside Peter Jackson and John Travolta. Streisand was unable to attend due to a knee injury but sent a video message to the audience. She concluded by saying, "In this crazy, volatile world that seems more fractured every day, it's reassuring to see the compelling movies at this festival, by artists from many countries. Film has that magical ability to unite us, opening our hearts and minds."

The report highlights how residents in the affected area are dealing with both the health threat and confusion caused by inaccurate information circulating about the disease. The combination of fear and misinformation has created additional challenges for public health officials working to contain the outbreak.

Ebola is a severe viral disease that can cause fever, body aches, and in many cases, internal and external bleeding. The virus spreads through direct contact with bodily fluids of infected individuals. Previous Ebola outbreaks have demonstrated how quickly misinformation can spread alongside the disease itself, complicating containment efforts.

Public health responses to Ebola typically include isolation of infected patients, contact tracing, and community education about transmission and prevention. However, these efforts can be undermined when communities receive conflicting or false information about the disease, its spread, or appropriate protective measures.

The current situation underscores ongoing challenges in managing disease outbreaks in communities where access to accurate health information may be limited. Health officials have consistently emphasized that clear, factual communication is essential during infectious disease crises to maintain public cooperation with containment measures.

Local residents near the epicenter face both immediate health risks and the broader challenge of distinguishing accurate public health guidance from misinformation. The concerns expressed by these communities reflect a pattern seen in previous infectious disease outbreaks, where fear and unreliable information can spread as rapidly as the pathogen itself.

The report from CNN provides a window into how communities at the center of health crises experience the dual challenges of disease threat and information confusion, highlighting the human dimension of public health emergencies.
