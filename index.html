9:39 AM ET

Iran has threatened retaliation following U.S. military strikes in southern Iran, even as the Trump administration maintains that peace talks are progressing. The strikes have raised global oil prices and cast doubt on prospects for an imminent end to hostilities.

President Trump described the strikes as defensive and said negotiations with Iran are proceeding well despite the military action. However, Iranian officials have warned that U.S. military bases in the Middle East are no longer safe following the attacks.

The strikes represent a significant setback for diplomatic efforts between the two countries. Trump had previously promised a peace deal with Iran, but the renewed military action has dimmed hopes for a quick resolution to the conflict.

Global oil markets reacted to the escalation, with prices rising in response to uncertainty about Middle East stability. The energy market volatility reflects concerns that continued military exchanges could disrupt oil supplies from the region.

Iranian Supreme Leader Ayatollah Khamenei said the United States will not have a haven for its bases in the Middle East, suggesting potential attacks on American military installations. His comments indicate Iran views the recent strikes as requiring a forceful response.

The timing of the strikes coincides with what had been described as ongoing negotiations between Washington and Tehran. The apparent contradiction between military action and diplomatic engagement has raised questions about the viability of peace talks.

Observers note that prospects for an imminent end to the Iran conflict have faded as attacks have restarted. The cycle of strikes and threatened retaliation suggests both sides remain far from a diplomatic breakthrough despite Trump's public optimism about negotiations.

The situation in the Middle East remains volatile as Iran considers its response to the U.S. strikes. How Tehran chooses to retaliate, and whether it targets American military assets in the region as Khamenei suggested, will determine whether the conflict escalates further or leaves room for renewed diplomatic efforts.

A few minutes ago

BP's board has removed chair Albert Manifold with immediate effect, citing "serious concerns" about governance standards, oversight and conduct at the oil company. Manifold served in the role for only eight months before his abrupt departure.

The FTSE 100 company announced the decision on Tuesday without disclosing specific details about the nature of the concerns. Amanda Blanc, senior independent director at BP and chief executive of insurance company Aviva, said the board had been "surprised and disappointed" to learn of the issues it deemed unacceptable.

"Albert has helped bring a welcome focus and pace to BP's transformation," Blanc said in a statement. "However, the board has been surprised and disappointed to learn of governance oversight and conduct issues it deems unacceptable and has taken decisive action."

The announcement triggered a significant market reaction. BP's share price dropped 9 percent immediately following the news before recovering slightly to a 6 percent decline, making it the FTSE's biggest faller on the day.

Manifold was appointed to BP's board in October 2025 with a mandate to oversee the company's strategic shift toward increased fossil fuel extraction and away from renewable energy investments. During his brief tenure, he moved quickly to remove chief executive Murray Auchincloss after less than two years in the role and hired Meg O'Neill to replace him in December.

The removal marks the second occasion in three years that a senior BP leader has lost his position due to conduct-related issues. In September 2023, former chief executive Bernard Looney was forced out for failing to disclose sexual relationships with colleagues when appointed to the role. Looney was subsequently denied 32 million pounds in pay and shares after the board determined it had been misled.

Ian Tyler, a board member and former chief executive of infrastructure company Balfour Beatty, has been appointed as interim chair. Tyler currently serves as chair of building supplies company Grafton Group and as a director at mining company Anglo American.

The immediate nature of Manifold's departure and the board's decisive action underscore BP's commitment to maintaining governance standards at the highest levels of leadership. The company has faced significant scrutiny over governance practices given its role in global energy markets and its influence on environmental policy. The leadership transition comes as BP continues navigating a volatile energy market and managing its operational challenges across Europe and beyond.

Investors and industry observers are watching closely to see how effectively the board manages this transition and whether additional details about the circumstances emerge in coming weeks.

Google announced a major redesign of its search interface on Tuesday, integrating artificial intelligence directly into the core search experience. According to Elizabeth Reid, who oversees the product, the changes represent "the biggest Google Search had seen in its nearly 30-year history."

The transformation moves away from Google's traditional text box and list of links toward AI-generated responses designed to answer queries directly. The search bar will now accommodate longer and more specific queries than before, resembling questions people would ask one another rather than Search's typical syntax. Results will feature more visual elements and suggestions to interact with Google's AI to perform tasks like creating calendar invites or building spreadsheets.

The updates, which rolled out globally to desktop and mobile versions on Tuesday, are powered by Google's new artificial intelligence model, Gemini 3.5. Users can still access the original version of search by clicking a tab titled "Web," allowing them to view traditional link-based results if preferred.

The redesign reflects growing demand for AI-powered search. Google Search queries reached an all-time high last month, with chatbot-specific queries doubling every quarter since the company introduced AI Mode a year ago. However, Google's Gemini app remains behind competitors, with 900 million monthly users compared to ChatGPT's 900 million weekly users.

Beyond basic search, Google is expanding AI capabilities through new features. Subscribers to its AI Pro and Ultra plans can create "information agents," autonomous bots that perform in-depth research and return summaries or action plans. A "generative user interface" feature will create customized visuals and interactive dashboards based on user requests. Gemini Spark, another agent feature, can access information from Gmail, Google Calendar, and other Google products to research, shop, and plan trips and appointments.

The shift addresses a fundamental change in how people seek information online. The company is departing from its long-standing approach of directing users to external websites, instead providing complete answers within search results themselves. This change has raised concerns among traditional web publishers and content creators about reduced traffic to their sites.

At its annual developer conference, Google I/O, the company also unveiled "intelligent eyewear" developed with Samsung, Warby Parker, and Gentle Monster. The glasses, arriving in fall, will respond to voice commands and include embedded cameras. This marks Google's return to the smart glasses market more than a decade after its first Google Glass product was shuttered following widespread criticism.

The announcements position Google as an aggressive competitor in consumer AI, challenging OpenAI's market dominance while fundamentally reshaping how billions of people search online daily.

This represents the Knicks' third consecutive playoff sweep. They previously swept the Philadelphia 76ers in the conference semifinals and defeated the Atlanta Hawks 4-2 in the first round. "Knicks fans are a specific species of human that should be studied, they're crazy. They fly out to Cleveland on a Monday, they're everywhere," he said.

The Knicks' domination of Game 4 set the tone early, as New York went on a 20-0 run at the end of the first quarter and into the second. Donovan Mitchell scored 31 points for Cleveland, but it proved insufficient to mount a serious challenge.

Cavaliers coach Kenny Atkinson acknowledged the gap between the teams. "They're playing better basketball. You got to give them credit. They're on a heater," he said.

The Finals will represent the Knicks' first championship series appearance since 1999, when they lost to the San Antonio Spurs. New York's last NBA championship came in 1973. The first Knicks home game of the Finals is scheduled for June 8, with courtside seats already commanding prices of $105,000 on resale sites.

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his website on Monday and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York, though no cause was given.

Rollins was born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930 and grew up in Harlem. His grandmother gave him the nickname Sonny. He began playing saxophone at age seven, inspired by his siblings who played piano and violin, as well as jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. As a teenager, he played in bands with future stars Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew, and later collaborated with established musicians including Bud Powell.

The saxophonist's career spanned more than seven decades. He released more than 60 albums beginning in the late 1940s, working with jazz legends Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane, Dizzy Gillespie, and many others. Rollins was recognized as a genius of melodic invention whose bright, distinctive lines could be unpicked and refashioned through improvisation into epic solos.

His early life included struggles with heroin addiction. In 1950, he committed armed robbery to support his habit, later describing himself as "really a despicable character." He was imprisoned for ten months on Rikers Island but successfully overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955.

Following his recovery, Rollins experienced a burst of creativity. His landmark albums included "Saxophone Colossus" in 1956, which featured his signature tune "St. Thomas," named after his mother's Caribbean birthplace. "Way Out West" in 1957 explored a piano-free style, while "Freedom Suite" in 1958 featured a 20-minute title track that became an argument for freedom during the civil rights movement.

In 1959, Rollins took a three-year break from recording and performing, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the pedestrian walkway of the Williamsburg Bridge in New York City. This practice retreat inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge." He took another sabbatical between 1969 and 1971 to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout his career, Rollins continually evolved his musical style, exploring avant-garde and fusion directions, Latin music, and R&B-influenced material. He also composed music for the 1966 film "Alfie" and added uncredited soloing to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album "Tattoo You."

In his later years, Rollins focused on larger concert stages and championed environmental causes, releasing the 1998 album "Global Warming." He was twice married, to Dawn Finney in 1957 and to Lucille Pearson from 1965 until her death in 2004.

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily halted deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo as the country faces a widening Ebola outbreak, according to reporting by Politico. The pause was implemented due to concerns that continuing the operations could expose Immigration and Customs Enforcement personnel to the virus.

The decision reflects broader worries about potential disease transmission risks associated with the flights. The Democratic Republic of Congo has experienced recurring Ebola outbreaks, which cause severe hemorrhagic fever and carry high fatality rates. Ebola spreads through direct contact with bodily fluids of infected individuals or contaminated materials, rather than through the air like respiratory illnesses.

However, public health experts have questioned whether the pause will actually prevent disease spread. The administration's approach has drawn criticism because officials say they will not return individuals who have already been deported to the country, even when ordered to do so by judges. At least one woman remains in limbo after being moved to Kinshasa, the capital, with officials refusing to bring her back despite a court order for her return.

The temporary suspension applies specifically to flights bound for Congo. The administration has not announced similar pauses for deportation flights to other countries, and officials have not clarified how long the suspension will remain in effect or what conditions would need to be met for flights to resume.

The Congo national soccer team had previously planned to travel to the United States for pre-World Cup training but cancelled the Kinshasa portion of its preparations due to health restrictions related to the outbreak. The team still intended to proceed with its scheduled trip to the United States.

The pause comes amid broader scrutiny of the Trump administration's expanded deportation flight program. A group of 40 House Democrats recently wrote to the Federal Aviation Administration expressing grave concerns about the secretive nature of ICE charter operations. The lawmakers cited reports indicating that individuals have been placed on flights without notice to their attorneys or family members, raising serious due process questions. They also referenced investigations revealing allegations of abuse and rights violations at detention centers central to the deportation program.

According to monitoring by human rights groups, ICE flights increased 84 percent from 2024 to 2025 under the Trump administration. The lawmakers requested detailed reports from the FAA regarding all ICE air operations, including flight origins, destinations, and passenger numbers.

The Trump administration has previously dismissed claims of hidden or weaponized deportation flights as categorically false, arguing that its detention centers maintain higher standards than most U.S. prisons. The administration has also described its use of full body restraints during deportation flights as standard protocol designed to ensure safety.

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his website on Monday and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York, though no cause was given.

Rollins was born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930 and grew up in Harlem. His grandmother gave him the nickname Sonny. He began playing saxophone at age seven, inspired by his siblings who played piano and violin, as well as jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. As a teenager, he played in bands with future stars Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew, and later collaborated with established musicians including Bud Powell.

The saxophonist's career spanned more than seven decades. He released more than 60 albums beginning in the late 1940s, working with jazz legends Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane, Dizzy Gillespie, and many others. Rollins was recognized as a genius of melodic invention whose bright, distinctive lines could be unpicked and refashioned through improvisation into epic solos.

His early life included struggles with heroin addiction. In 1950, he committed armed robbery to support his habit, later describing himself as "really a despicable character." He was imprisoned for ten months on Rikers Island but successfully overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955.

Following his recovery, Rollins experienced a burst of creativity. His landmark albums included "Saxophone Colossus" in 1956, which featured his signature tune "St. Thomas," named after his mother's Caribbean birthplace. "Way Out West" in 1957 explored a piano-free style, while "Freedom Suite" in 1958 featured a 20-minute title track that became an argument for freedom during the civil rights movement.

In 1959, Rollins took a three-year break from recording and performing, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the pedestrian walkway of the Williamsburg Bridge in New York City. This practice retreat inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge." He took another sabbatical between 1969 and 1971 to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout his career, Rollins continually evolved his musical style, exploring avant-garde and fusion directions, Latin music, and R&B-influenced material. He also composed music for the 1966 film "Alfie" and added uncredited soloing to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album "Tattoo You."

In his later years, Rollins focused on larger concert stages and championed environmental causes, releasing the 1998 album "Global Warming." He was twice married, to Dawn Finney in 1957 and to Lucille Pearson from 1965 until her death in 2004.
