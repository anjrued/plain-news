1:45 AM ET

The United States has carried out additional military strikes targeting Iranian missile facilities and naval vessels in southern Iran. US Central Command characterized the operations as self-defense measures in response to threats against American forces.

The timing of these strikes coincides with ongoing diplomatic negotiations aimed at ending hostilities between the two nations. Secretary of State Marco Rubio stated that an agreement could potentially be reached within days, even as military operations continued.

Iranian negotiators are currently in Qatar participating in talks focused on resolving the military conflict. Despite diplomatic progress, both sides have recently downplayed expectations for an immediate breakthrough. Iranian officials have pointed to contradictory statements from Washington as complications in the negotiation process, while American officials have similarly cautioned against anticipating a swift resolution.

The parallel approach of conducting military strikes while simultaneously pursuing diplomatic talks represents an unusual strategy for conflict resolution. President Trump has linked any potential agreement with Iran to broader regional peace objectives. He has called for expanding the Abraham Accords, which normalized relations between Israel and several Arab nations, as part of a comprehensive settlement with Tehran.

Trump reportedly asked Muslim leaders to sign a peace deal with Israel once the conflict with Iran concludes. Trump also stated on Saturday that any agreement would include the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, though he provided no additional details about the terms.

The strikes follow a pattern of previous American military operations in the region. US officials have consistently described these actions as defensive rather than offensive operations designed to escalate the conflict.

Oil markets have responded positively to signs of potential peace negotiations. Prices slid on hopes that a US-Iran deal could stabilize the region and address energy security concerns.

The current military operations underscore the complex nature of the situation, where negotiators believe they are close to a resolution despite continued hostilities. The strikes on missile sites and boats reflect ongoing security concerns that have not been resolved through diplomatic channels.

International observers continue to monitor developments for indications of whether the situation will move toward escalation or de-escalation. The combination of military action and peace efforts suggests both sides are pursuing multiple approaches to achieve their objectives.

The negotiations represent a significant diplomatic initiative to address the fundamental security concerns driving the conflict between the United States and Iran. Whether the military operations will support or hinder the diplomatic process remains uncertain, as both tracks proceed simultaneously.

Governor Mikie Sherrill was denied access to Delaney Hall, an immigration detention facility in New Jersey, as a hunger strike among detainees expanded and protesters clashed with federal agents outside the center.

Sherrill demanded entry to the facility, but ICE officials refused to allow her inside. The confrontation occurred as demonstrators gathered outside the detention center to protest immigration enforcement practices. Protesters alleged that US immigration agents deployed pepper spray and batons against them during the demonstration on Monday.

The hunger strike was announced by Martin Soto, a detainee at the facility. ICE officials stated they were able to transfer Soto to the Elizabeth contract detention facility despite protesters' attempts to prevent the move. The strike has drawn attention from elected officials and advocacy groups concerned about conditions in immigration detention centers.

At least one Senate Democrat was pepper sprayed during the protest outside the facility. The clash between demonstrators and federal agents reflects escalating tensions over ICE detention practices.

The denial of access to a sitting governor represents a significant standoff between state officials and federal immigration authorities. While governors typically have broad authority to inspect facilities within their states, federal facilities operate under different jurisdictions.

Governor Sherrill's efforts to challenge ICE operations extend beyond her attempt to access Delaney Hall. She recently signed an executive order that prohibits immigration officers from accessing non-public portions of state-owned property without a judicial warrant. The order also prevents immigration agents from using state property as bases for enforcement operations.

During a news conference, Sherrill stated: "We have seen dozens, even hundreds of videos of Donald Trump's Immigration and Customs Enforcement agents abusing their power, smashing cars, teargassing families, gunning down American citizens and violating our most basic rights."

In response to growing scrutiny of ICE activities, New Jersey officials launched an online portal for residents to share videos and photos of enforcement operations. The state attorney's office will review materials submitted through the portal. Acting State Attorney General Jennifer Davenport said: "While we cannot guarantee any particular action by our office in response to reports on the portal, we will review every report with care."

Civil rights advocates have supported Sherrill's initiatives. Amol Sinha, executive director of the ACLU New Jersey, stated: "ICE is out of control, endangering our communities, and must be stopped." He praised the governor for using her executive power to hold ICE accountable and called on other governors to take similar action.

Sherrill's push back against federal immigration enforcement comes as the Trump administration has ramped up immigration enforcement operations, with particular focus on Democratic-led states. Immigration agents have killed two US citizens during recent enforcement operations.

Traders are betting that an agreement to end the nearly three-month US-Israeli war on Iran could increase global oil supply and ease tensions in the Middle East.

Trump also said that more countries should normalize ties with Israel as part of any Iran agreement, noting that a deal is largely negotiated and urging faster progress. "For Iran, the Clock is Ticking, and they better get moving, FAST, or there won't be anything left of them. TIME IS OF THE ESSENCE!" Trump wrote on social media.

Iran's foreign ministry spokesperson, Esmaeil Baqaei, said exchanges were "continuing through the Pakistani mediator," without providing specifics. Hours after Trump's statements about negotiations moving forward, the US military announced strikes against Iranian positions, which appeared to temper expectations of an imminent peace agreement.

However, Republican lawmakers have issued rare criticism of Trump over the planned deal, describing it as a "disaster" and questioning why the administration launched the war in the first place. Allies of Trump who supported his decision to order the war alongside Israel have urged him to "hold the line" despite mounting economic costs.

The stock market rally comes during a holiday-shortened trading week. Stock markets opened lower in Europe, with the Stoxx Europe 600 dropping 0.7%, while Asian markets showed mixed results. Japan's Nikkei fell about 1%, Hong Kong's Hang Seng index also declined 1%, and Shanghai's SSE Composite slipped 0.1%, though South Korea's Kospi closed 0.3% higher.

Lower oil prices could help ease inflation pressures, potentially giving central banks more flexibility in interest rate decisions. Global bond markets have been volatile, with the 10-year US Treasury yield hitting 4.631% before paring back to 4.599%.

The path to a final agreement continues to face obstacles from both Republican opposition and mixed signals from negotiators on key sticking points.

Yesterday, 11:32 PM ET

Sonny Rollins, widely recognized as one of the greatest jazz saxophonists in history, has died at age 95. His death was announced Monday on his official website and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was disclosed.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. Raised in Harlem, he began playing saxophone at age seven, inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. He quickly became immersed in the vibrant local jazz scene, forming a high school band that included future stars Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew.

Rollins became a defining figure of the bebop era, playing alongside Charlie Parker, Miles Davis, and Thelonious Monk. Miles Davis himself noted how Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" for his aggressive, innovative approach. Rollins described his style as driven by feeling rather than technique, a willingness to break conventions that helped reshape jazz's direction.

His early career was interrupted by heroin addiction. In 1950, he committed armed robbery to fund his habit and spent 10 months in jail on Rikers Island. He successfully overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955, which sparked remarkable creative output. His 1956 album "Saxophone Colossus" became a landmark recording, featuring the signature tune "St Thomas," which drew from calypso influences connected to his mother's Caribbean heritage.

Throughout the late 1950s and 1960s, Rollins produced significant albums including "Way Out West" and "Freedom Suite." He became known for extended practice sessions, famously practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway starting in 1959, partly to avoid disturbing neighbors. He returned to performing in 1962 with "The Bridge."

Between 1969 and 1971, Rollins took another sabbatical to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram. He continued evolving his sound through subsequent decades, incorporating funk, Latin music, and R&B influences. He also composed and performed the soundtrack to the 1966 Michael Caine film "Alfie."

Rollins maintained an active career into his later years, shifting performances away from nightclubs toward larger venues. He became an environmental advocate, organizing benefit concerts and recording the 1998 album "Global Warming."

He married twice: briefly to Dawn Finney in 1957, then to Lucille Pearson in 1965, remaining married until her death in 2004. Rollins received numerous honors including the National Medal of the Arts presented by President Barack Obama, the Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award, and the Kennedy Center honor.

Trump administration pauses deportation flights to Congo over Ebola outbreak

The pause aims to protect Immigration and Customs Enforcement personnel from potential exposure to the virus. However, the decision has created complications for individuals already affected by the po...

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily halted the removal of detainees to the Democratic Republic of Congo due to a widening Ebola outbreak, according to reporting by Politico. The pause aims to protect Immigration and Customs Enforcement personnel from potential exposure to the virus.

However, the decision has created complications for individuals already affected by the policy. At least one woman has been left in limbo after officials moved her to Kinshasa, the capital of the DRC. Despite a judge's order for her return, officials say they will not bring her back because of the travel ban related to the Ebola outbreak.

Public health experts have questioned the effectiveness of the deportation pause in preventing disease spread. The move reflects growing scrutiny of the Trump administration's immigration enforcement operations, which have expanded significantly since the president took office.

The pause applies specifically to deportation operations to the DRC. ICE has been conducting removal flights to various countries as part of the administration's immigration enforcement priorities. The virus has prompted health restrictions in Congo, including changes to the country's World Cup soccer team's training schedule.

Ebola is a severe viral disease that spreads through contact with bodily fluids of infected people. Outbreaks have historically occurred in Central and West Africa, with the DRC experiencing multiple episodes over the past decade. The current outbreak's scope and severity have triggered operational changes by both Congolese authorities and the U.S. government.

The deportation pause represents a precautionary measure focused on protecting federal personnel involved in removal operations. ICE officials conduct these flights regularly as part of immigration enforcement, with personnel accompanying deportees to their destination countries.

The decision comes amid broader controversy over the Trump administration's deportation flight program. A group of 40 House Democrats recently sent a letter to the Federal Aviation Administration expressing grave concerns about the secretive nature of ICE charter operations. The lawmakers described allegations of mistreatment, inhumane conditions on ICE charter jets, and efforts to shield flights from public scrutiny.

According to the letter, credible reports indicate individuals have been placed on flights without notice to counsel or family members. The lawmakers cited an investigation revealing the Trump administration transported detained immigrants in ways that routinely violated constitutional rights.

The Trump administration previously described claims of hidden or weaponized transfer and deportation flights as categorically false. Officials have argued that detention centers maintain higher standards than most U.S. prisons.

The temporary nature of the deportation pause suggests the administration plans to resume flights once the outbreak is contained or adequate protective measures can be implemented. The decision balances immigration enforcement objectives against public health and safety considerations for government employees.

Jazz saxophonist Sonny Rollins dies at 95

Sonny Rollins, widely recognized as one of the greatest jazz saxophonists in history, has died at age 95. His death was announced Monday on his official website and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his...

4:00 AM ET

Sonny Rollins, widely recognized as one of the greatest jazz saxophonists in history, has died at age 95. His death was announced Monday on his official website and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was disclosed.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. Raised in Harlem, he began playing saxophone at age seven, inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. He quickly became immersed in the vibrant local jazz scene, forming a high school band that included future stars Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew.

Rollins became a defining figure of the bebop era, playing alongside Charlie Parker, Miles Davis, and Thelonious Monk. Miles Davis himself noted how Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" for his aggressive, innovative approach. Rollins described his style as driven by feeling rather than technique, a willingness to break conventions that helped reshape jazz's direction.

His early career was interrupted by heroin addiction. In 1950, he committed armed robbery to fund his habit and spent 10 months in jail on Rikers Island. He successfully overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955, which sparked remarkable creative output. His 1956 album "Saxophone Colossus" became a landmark recording, featuring the signature tune "St Thomas," which drew from calypso influences connected to his mother's Caribbean heritage.

Throughout the late 1950s and 1960s, Rollins produced significant albums including "Way Out West" and "Freedom Suite." He became known for extended practice sessions, famously practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway starting in 1959, partly to avoid disturbing neighbors. He returned to performing in 1962 with "The Bridge."

Between 1969 and 1971, Rollins took another sabbatical to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram. He continued evolving his sound through subsequent decades, incorporating funk, Latin music, and R&B influences. He also composed and performed the soundtrack to the 1966 Michael Caine film "Alfie."

Rollins maintained an active career into his later years, shifting performances away from nightclubs toward larger venues. He became an environmental advocate, organizing benefit concerts and recording the 1998 album "Global Warming."

He married twice: briefly to Dawn Finney in 1957, then to Lucille Pearson in 1965, remaining married until her death in 2004. Rollins received numerous honors including the National Medal of the Arts presented by President Barack Obama, the Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award, and the Kennedy Center honor.
