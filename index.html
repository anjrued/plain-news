A few minutes ago

A federal court has rejected Alabama's 2023 congressional redistricting map, ruling that it was intentionally drawn to discriminate against Black voters. The three-judge panel blocked the state from using the map in upcoming elections.

The decision marks the latest chapter in a years-long legal battle over Alabama's district boundaries. The case began in 2021 when Black voters challenged the state's original congressional map, arguing it diluted their voting power. A court eventually ordered Alabama to create a new map with two majority-Black districts, both of which elected Democrats. A court-appointed special master drew this revised plan, which the U.S. Supreme Court upheld in 2023.

However, after the U.S. Supreme Court weakened the Voting Rights Act in April by gutting a major provision, Alabama moved to resurrect its 2023 map for the upcoming elections. The state even moved its congressional primary earlier to try to implement this map. Alabama Attorney General Steve Marshall, a Republican, argued the map had "been in place for decades" and was "blandly unobjectionable."

The three-judge panel disagreed. The court found that when lawmakers passed the 2023 map, they "made a calculated, purposeful decision to refuse to provide the remedy for discriminatory vote dilution." The judges wrote that the legislature knew a plan without an additional Black-opportunity district would dilute Black voters' ability to participate in the political process, and "intentionally enacted that very plan."

The panel noted that the Supreme Court's recent decision on the Voting Rights Act still permits challenges to maps drawn with discriminatory intent, though proving such intent is extremely difficult. The court wrote: "We cannot see our way clear to requiring Alabamians to cast their votes in the 2026 elections under a districting plan tainted by intentional race-based discrimination."

The three judges included Clinton appointee Judge Stanley Marcus and two Trump appointees, Judges Anna Manasco and Terry Moorer. Their bipartisan decision to reject the map adds significance to the ruling, as it shows judges across the ideological spectrum agreed on the discriminatory nature of Alabama's efforts.

Alabama's attempt to implement the map was part of a broader Republican effort across the South. Following the Supreme Court's Voting Rights Act decision, Tennessee eliminated a majority-Black congressional district in Memphis, and Louisiana pursued similar changes. Civil rights groups and Black leaders condemned these efforts as an attempt to deny Black voters political representation.

Attorney General Marshall said he would appeal the decision to the U.S. Supreme Court, stating it was "not a matter of whether we win this case, only when." Alabama must now return to the redistricting process to develop a map that complies with voting rights law.

Russia has told U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio that American citizens should leave Kyiv, citing plans for systematic strikes on the Ukrainian capital. The warning extends to diplomats and nationals from other countries in the city.

The threat prompted a swift diplomatic response. The European Union summoned Russia's envoy in Brussels over the warnings, with EU foreign policy spokesperson Anitta Hipper calling the threat "an unacceptable escalation." Individual European countries also called in Russian ambassadors to protest. EU officials characterized the warnings as evidence that Russia is "absolutely not interested in any peace and has a total disregard for all efforts towards the peace."

The warning comes after Kyiv experienced one of the largest aerial assaults of the war. Early Sunday morning, Russia struck the Ukrainian capital with missiles and drones shortly after Ukraine's air force warned of a potential Oreshnik hypersonic ballistic missile launch. The attack killed one person and wounded 20, with several residential buildings damaged across multiple districts of the city, according to Kyiv Mayor Vitali Klitschko.

The Oreshnik is a particularly advanced weapon system. President Vladimir Putin has promoted it as impossible to intercept due to its reported speed of more than 10 times the sound barrier. Russia has now deployed the missile to Belarus, which borders Ukraine and three NATO member states: Lithuania, Latvia, and Poland.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy warned that the use of such weapons sets a dangerous global precedent. He stated that if Russia is permitted to destroy lives on this scale without consequences, other regimes may follow suit. He called for preventive pressure on Moscow rather than responses after attacks occur.

The coordinated European response reflected broader concern about Russian intentions. The EU delegation to Ukraine stated it would remain in Kyiv despite the threats, signaling continued support for Ukraine's government. Many countries have maintained diplomatic presences in the capital throughout the conflict as a show of solidarity.

Canada strongly condemned the attacks on civilian targets, with Prime Minister Mark Carney urging Moscow to cease strikes and the "illegal war of aggression." Carney said the attacks "prolong human suffering" and would not change the outcome of the war.

The threats against Kyiv represent an unusual diplomatic step in the conflict. By explicitly communicating planned strikes to foreign governments and warning diplomats and civilians to leave, Russia appears to be attempting to pressure allied nations to withdraw support from Ukraine or reconsider their diplomatic presence in the country.

The situation has created uncertainty for foreign embassies and international organizations operating in the Ukrainian capital. The explicit warnings, combined with the actual large-scale overnight attack, demonstrate Russia's continued offensive operations across multiple fronts.

10:14 AM ET

Nvidia has officially discontinued its GeForce Control Panel application, ending two decades of service to graphics card users. The retirement became official with the release of Nvidia Graphics Drivers 610.47 WHQL, which no longer includes the Control Panel.

The Control Panel served as the primary tool for Nvidia GPU users to manage display settings, adjust 3D configurations, and optimize graphics performance. Since its introduction, the application became a standard utility for gamers and professionals who needed to fine-tune their graphics card capabilities.

Nvidia announced its intention to replace the Control Panel more than two years ago. The company gradually migrated features from the legacy application to its newer Nvidia app, preparing users for this eventual transition. Now that the migration is complete, users must switch to the replacement application.

The Control Panel allowed users to adjust resolution, refresh rates, and color settings through an intuitive interface. It also provided access to advanced features including Nvidia's 3D settings panel, PhysX configuration, and Surround display functionality. Many users relied on these tools to optimize gaming performance and manage their GPU settings efficiently.

The application's interface remained largely unchanged in appearance throughout its two-decade lifespan, even as Nvidia introduced new features and capabilities over the years. Despite its age, the Control Panel maintained a loyal user base who appreciated its functionality and familiarity.

The new Nvidia app consolidates functions that were previously divided between the Control Panel and GeForce Experience into a single unified application. Users can now access display configuration, driver updates, game optimization features, and GPU monitoring through this replacement platform.

Users running older driver versions can continue using the Control Panel, but anyone installing driver version 610.47 WHQL or newer will need to transition to the new application. Nvidia has not made the Control Panel available as a separate installation option for users who prefer the legacy interface.

The retirement affects millions of GeForce graphics card owners worldwide who have depended on the Control Panel as their primary GPU management tool. The new application features a redesigned interface that brings previously separate functions together in a more modern design.

This transition represents a significant shift in how Nvidia manages its driver software ecosystem. While the Control Panel's retirement marks the end of a familiar tool that became synonymous with Nvidia GPU management, the company asserts that its new unified app provides users with the same functionality in a more contemporary package.

10:16 AM ET

Sonny Rollins, widely regarded as one of the greatest saxophonists in jazz history, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his website on Monday, with his publicist Terri Hinte confirming the news. He passed away at his home in Woodstock, New York, on Monday afternoon. No cause of death was provided.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he grew up in Harlem and earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began learning saxophone at age seven, inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. His early bands in high school included future stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew and Art Taylor.

Rollins became known for his genius with melody and his willingness to break musical conventions. He collaborated with many of jazz's most significant figures, including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk and John Coltrane. Davis himself noted that Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" for his aggressive, innovative approach and fresh musical ideas.

His early career was interrupted by heroin addiction. In 1950, he committed armed robbery to fund his habit. After spending ten months in jail on Rikers Island, he successfully overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955. This period of recovery sparked remarkable creative growth.

Rollins released his debut album as a bandleader in 1953. Notable recordings from the 1950s include Saxophone Colossus (1956), featuring his signature tune "St Thomas," which referenced his mother's Caribbean heritage; Way Out West (1957), which explored a piano-free "strolling" style; and Freedom Suite (1958), with its 20-minute title track becoming an elegant statement on freedom during the civil rights era.

Rollins took a three-year break from 1959, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway. He returned in 1962 with the album The Bridge. Between 1969 and 1971, he took another sabbatical to study yoga, philosophy and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout subsequent decades, Rollins continued innovating, exploring Latin American music, avant-garde styles, fusion and funk. He composed and performed the soundtrack to the 1966 film Alfie and contributed uncredited solos to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album Tattoo You. He also campaigned on climate change through benefit concerts and his 1998 album Global Warming.

Rollins married Lucille Pearson in 1965, remaining together until her death in 2004. He continued performing into his later years, maintaining a reputation for intense, exploratory live performances.

Reflecting on mortality near the end of his life, Rollins said: "I think when the creative person ends, he continues in the next existence. I'm a person who believes this life isn't the be-all and end-all of everything."

Trump administration pauses deportation flights to Congo over Ebola outbreak

The Trump administration has temporarily halted deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo due to concerns about a spreading Ebola outbreak, according to reporting by Politico. However, experts question whether the pause will actually prevent disease transmission. The decision to suspend removals represents a notable shift in the administration's aggressive deport...

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily halted deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo due to concerns about a spreading Ebola outbreak, according to reporting by Politico. However, experts question whether the pause will actually prevent disease transmission.

The decision to suspend removals represents a notable shift in the administration's aggressive deportation operations, which have expanded significantly during Trump's second term. Human rights groups tracking ICE flights documented an 84 percent increase in deportation flights from 2024 to 2025.

The pause applies specifically to new removals being sent to Congo. The administration has not specified how long the suspension will remain in effect or what conditions would need to be met for flights to resume. The decision appears to depend on the current state of the outbreak in the country.

The Ebola virus causes severe illness with a high fatality rate. Previous outbreaks in West and Central Africa have killed thousands of people. The virus spreads through direct contact with bodily fluids from infected individuals. Congo has experienced multiple Ebola outbreaks in recent years, and the country's health infrastructure has struggled to contain the virus in some regions.

Public health concerns about deportation flights center on two main issues. ICE officials would be required to enter areas where Ebola is present to carry out removals, potentially exposing them to infection. Additionally, there is concern that infected individuals could board return flights, creating a pathway for the disease to reach the United States. While airport screening measures exist, the virus can have an incubation period during which infected people show no symptoms.

The pause has created complications for some individuals. According to reporting by The Guardian, at least one woman has been moved to Kinshasa, the capital of Congo, and officials now say they will not bring her back because of the Ebola-related travel restrictions, despite a judge's order for her return.

Public health experts have noted that while Ebola does not currently pose an immediate threat to most Americans, the virus requires careful monitoring and containment efforts in affected regions. The disease has periodically raised international health concerns when outbreaks have grown in size or spread across borders.

The deportation pause is limited to Congo flights. The Trump administration continues deportation operations to other countries worldwide as part of its expanded removal efforts. The administration has not released detailed information about how long the Congo pause will last or what metrics will determine when flights can resume.

The decision reflects the administration weighing its immigration enforcement goals against public health risks. ICE officials have described detention and transfer practices as meeting established safety standards, though lawmakers and human rights groups have raised concerns about transparency and conditions on deportation flights.

Sonny Rollins, widely regarded as one of the greatest saxophonists in jazz history, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his website on Monday, with his publicist Terri Hinte confirming the news. He passed away at his home in Woodstock, New York, on Monday afternoon. No cause of death was provided.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he grew up in Harlem and earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began learning saxophone at age seven, inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. His early bands in high school included future stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew and Art Taylor.

Rollins became known for his genius with melody and his willingness to break musical conventions. He collaborated with many of jazz's most significant figures, including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk and John Coltrane. Davis himself noted that Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" for his aggressive, innovative approach and fresh musical ideas.

His early career was interrupted by heroin addiction. In 1950, he committed armed robbery to fund his habit. After spending ten months in jail on Rikers Island, he successfully overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955. This period of recovery sparked remarkable creative growth.

Rollins released his debut album as a bandleader in 1953. Notable recordings from the 1950s include Saxophone Colossus (1956), featuring his signature tune "St Thomas," which referenced his mother's Caribbean heritage; Way Out West (1957), which explored a piano-free "strolling" style; and Freedom Suite (1958), with its 20-minute title track becoming an elegant statement on freedom during the civil rights era.

Rollins took a three-year break from 1959, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway. He returned in 1962 with the album The Bridge. Between 1969 and 1971, he took another sabbatical to study yoga, philosophy and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout subsequent decades, Rollins continued innovating, exploring Latin American music, avant-garde styles, fusion and funk. He composed and performed the soundtrack to the 1966 film Alfie and contributed uncredited solos to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album Tattoo You. He also campaigned on climate change through benefit concerts and his 1998 album Global Warming.

Rollins married Lucille Pearson in 1965, remaining together until her death in 2004. He continued performing into his later years, maintaining a reputation for intense, exploratory live performances.

Reflecting on mortality near the end of his life, Rollins said: "I think when the creative person ends, he continues in the next existence. I'm a person who believes this life isn't the be-all and end-all of everything."
