8:11 PM ET

The US military carried out strikes on missile launch sites and mine-laying boats in Iran as part of what officials described as self-defense operations. US Central Command confirmed the strikes targeted facilities in southern Iran and vessels near the Strait of Hormuz.

The military action occurred as the Trump administration pursues a peace agreement with Iran. President Trump stated that a deal would include reopening the Strait of Hormuz, a critical waterway for global oil shipments. Oil prices declined following reports of progress in negotiations between Washington and Tehran.

Trump indicated that a comprehensive agreement is largely negotiated and could be finalized soon. The potential deal would involve multiple components including regional security arrangements and sanctions relief. The administration has also pushed for other countries to normalize ties with Israel as part of any Iran agreement, which could expand the existing Abraham Accords that have already established diplomatic relations between Israel and several Arab states.

US Central Command characterized the strikes as defensive measures taken in response to identified threats. Officials stated that a ceasefire remains in place even as the military operations were conducted. The timing reflects the administration's strategy of combining military pressure with active diplomatic engagement.

The strikes targeted mine-laying boats near the Strait of Hormuz, suggesting concerns about potential threats to maritime traffic through this vital shipping lane. Iran has reportedly indicated willingness to reopen the strait within 30 days of reaching a peace settlement with the United States.

The situation represents a delicate balance between maintaining military options and pursuing diplomatic solutions. While the Trump administration expressed optimism about reaching an agreement, the authorization of strikes against Iranian targets could potentially complicate ongoing negotiations.

Multiple news outlets reported on the military action, with the AP, BBC, and other organizations covering the strikes. The administration's messaging emphasized the defensive nature of the operations while also highlighting progress in peace talks.

As of the latest reports, Iranian officials had not publicly responded to the strikes. Both military and diplomatic channels remained active as the administration continued discussions with Tehran. The administration maintained that an agreement remained possible despite the military action.

The negotiations have drawn international attention given the broader implications for regional stability and global energy markets. The potential reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, which is essential for oil transport to global markets, has influenced oil prices and investor sentiment.

The Trump administration's dual approach of military strikes and diplomatic outreach reflects the complex relationship between the two nations. Officials insisted that the defensive operations would not derail ongoing peace efforts. The coming days are expected to be significant as both sides continue negotiations on a comprehensive agreement that could reshape regional dynamics.

Officials in Garden Grove, California are continuing efforts to manage a hazardous chemical leak at an aerospace facility that has prompted the evacuation of approximately 50,000 residents from the surrounding area.

The incident began Thursday afternoon when a leak was discovered at GKN Aerospace, a facility that manufactures parts for commercial and military aircraft. The affected tank contained 6,000 to 7,000 gallons of methyl methacrylate, a highly flammable chemical used in plastic and resin production.

EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin told CNN that officials have warned the overheated tank "will fail," though different scenarios exist regarding what that failure might look like. He described the most catastrophic possibility as "an explosion that results in other tanks to explode," which explains the large-scale evacuation in surrounding areas. However, Zeldin noted that officials have identified the "most likely scenario" as a "low-volume release" of chemicals.

Emergency responders have been working to cool the tank by spraying it with water and keeping its temperature below 85 degrees Fahrenheit. On Friday, officials added a neutralizing agent to a nearby tank to reduce explosion risks.

A significant development occurred late Saturday night when firefighters discovered a potential crack in the tank. According to Orange County Fire Chief TJ McGovern, this crack could relieve pressure inside the tank, making an explosion less likely. An engineering professor at Purdue University explained the positive aspect of this crack by comparing it to a pressurized soda can. "If you leave it in a hot car it can explode," he said. "But if you put a hole in the can, the product is released and the can itself doesn't explode."

However, experts caution that the danger has not completely passed. A chemical engineering department head from Texas A&M University noted that while cooling is occurring on the tank's surface, a runaway reaction may still be happening inside. He warned that "reaction is slowed compared to what it started. However, we are not out of explosive release risk."

Air quality monitoring in the area has shown no detected pollutants, with officials reporting that measurement levels remain completely normal.

The evacuation zone has been reduced from initial boundaries, allowing some residents to return home. Approximately 16,000 people remain unable to return to their homes. Garden Grove's mayor warned residents to take evacuation orders seriously, describing the situation as "very dangerous."

No injuries have been reported. At least two lawsuits have been filed against GKN Aerospace by Garden Grove residents over the chemical leak, citing evacuation orders, shelter-in-place directives, and property damage concerns.

Oil prices fell below $100 a barrel on Monday as investors responded to signs that the United States and Iran are moving closer to a peace agreement, though significant obstacles remain.

Brent crude futures, the global oil benchmark, dropped 6% to $97.43 a barrel, reaching their lowest level in two weeks. The price decline reflects optimism about ending the three-month conflict between the US, Israel, and Iran. However, an Iranian government spokesperson cautioned that an agreement was "not imminent," and the two sides continue to disagree on key issues including Iran's blockade of the Strait of Hormuz.

Stock markets climbed alongside the oil price decline. Japan's Nikkei rose nearly 3%, and the pan-European Stoxx 600 index gained 1%. The dollar also eased, dropping 0.3% against a basket of major currencies, while gold climbed 1.46% to $4,574 an ounce.

Despite the optimism, analysts urged caution. Warren Patterson, head of commodities strategy at ING, told Reuters: "We've been at this stage before, only for talks to break down. Therefore, the market will likely be more cautious about overreacting." Giovanni Staunovo, a UBS analyst, emphasized the importance of monitoring actual oil flows, noting that "flows through the strait remain restricted."

Even if negotiations succeed, reopening the strait and returning to normal oil flows will take months while energy infrastructure in Qatar and elsewhere undergoes repairs. Some tankers have recently begun moving through the waterway. Two liquefied natural gas carriers exited the strait on Monday heading to Pakistan and China, while a supertanker carrying Iraqi crude left the Gulf for China after being stranded for nearly three months.

The current oil prices, while below recent peaks, remain significantly elevated compared to pre-war levels when Brent crude traded around $70 a barrel. Barclays has maintained its average forecast for Brent crude at $100 for the year, though analysts acknowledge risks that prices could climb higher.

Stephen Innes, an independent analyst, said the market's response reflected investor expectations that the energy chokepoint "may soon reopen to something resembling normal flow." He noted that "inflation fear and hawkish rate pricing had been embedded into the curve during the recent energy shock."

Higher oil and energy prices have fueled inflation concerns globally, upending earlier expectations of interest rate cuts from central banks. Markets now anticipate rate increases instead. The Bank of England is expected to raise rates twice this year as inflation pressures persist from elevated energy and commodity costs, including fertilizer, which threatens to drive food prices sharply higher in coming months.

Google announced on Tuesday that it would substantially redesign its search interface, embedding artificial intelligence directly into the search box for the first time in the company's nearly 30-year history. The changes were revealed at Google I/O, the company's annual conference for software developers.

The updated search box will accommodate longer and more specific queries than before, allowing users to ask questions in natural language rather than using search-specific syntax. The redesign directs users to engage directly with Google's AI chatbot, powered by the newly announced Gemini 3.5 model.

Elizabeth Reid, who oversees Google Search, called the changes the biggest update the product has experienced in its history. "Google Search is AI search," she said. The updates rolled out globally to both desktop and mobile versions on Tuesday.

Search results will now display more visual elements and AI-generated summaries rather than primarily showing links to websites. Users can still access the traditional list of links by clicking a tab titled "Web". The changes build on Google's "AI Mode," which launched a year ago and allows users to chat with a Gemini-powered bot. Since that launch, queries to the AI-focused interface have doubled every quarter.

When users add photos, videos or documents to the Chrome browser's search bar, Search will automatically switch to AI mode. Google Search queries reached an all-time high last month, according to the company.

For users with AI Pro and Ultra subscriptions, the service will offer new features including "information agents" that can perform in-depth research and provide summaries or action plans. A "generative user interface" will create customized visuals and interactive dashboards based on user requests. Another new feature called Gemini Spark will access information from Gmail, Google Calendar and other Google products to help users research, shop and plan trips and appointments.

Google also announced a new line of intelligent eyewear developed with Samsung, Warby Parker and Gentle Monster. The glasses will respond to voice commands for Gemini and include embedded cameras for photos and videos. The products are set to launch in fall.

This marks Google's return to wearable technology after its 2014 Google Glass project, which sold for 1,500 dollars and was discontinued the following year. In contrast, competitor Meta has successfully marketed Ray-Ban Meta glasses with voice activation and cameras since 2021.

Josh Woodward, who oversees Gemini's development, described the goal as creating "a universal assistant that's personal, proactive, persistent." Google reported that 900 million people use the Gemini app monthly, compared to ChatGPT's 900 million weekly users.

The redesign represents a fundamental shift in how billions of people access information online, moving from traditional link-based results to AI-generated responses delivered directly within the search interface.

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest saxophonists in jazz history, has died at age 95. His death was announced Monday on his website and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He passed away at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was disclosed.

Rollins was born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930 and grew up in Harlem. He earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. Inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller, he began learning saxophone at seven years old. His early musical environment was rich with talent, and he played in high school bands with future jazz stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew, and Art Taylor.

A pioneer of bebop and hard bop, Rollins became known for his innovative approach to improvisation and his bright, catchy melodic lines. Miles Davis himself described how Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" for his aggressive, innovative playing and fresh musical ideas. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis later called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong.

Rollins's career faced obstacles in the early 1950s when heroin addiction led him to commit armed robbery in 1950. He spent ten months on Rikers Island in New York but successfully overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955. This recovery sparked an extraordinary creative period. His 1956 album "Saxophone Colossus" featured his signature tune "St Thomas," named after his mother's Caribbean birthplace. In 1959, Rollins took a three-year break from recording and performing, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the pedestrian walkway of the Williamsburg Bridge to avoid disturbing neighbors. This dedication inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge."

Over more than seven decades, Rollins recorded more than 60 albums and collaborated with jazz giants including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, and John Coltrane. His work shaped the development of modern jazz and influenced generations of musicians. Beyond music, he remained socially conscious, campaigning on climate issues and releasing the 1998 album "Global Warming."

Rollins married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004. He took another sabbatical from 1969 to 1971 to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram.

When President Barack Obama presented Rollins with the 2010 National Medal of the Arts in 2011, he said the saxophonist had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken." Rollins's death marks the end of an era, removing one of the last direct links to jazz's golden age.

However, the decision has created complications for some individuals alread...

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily halted the removal of detainees to the Democratic Republic of Congo as the country faces a worsening Ebola outbreak. According to reporting by Politico, the pause aims to protect Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials from potential exposure to the virus.

However, the decision has created complications for some individuals already in the process of being deported. At least one woman has been moved to Kinshasa, the capital of the DRC, but officials now refuse to return her despite a judge's order for her return, citing the travel restrictions related to the Ebola outbreak.

Public health experts have raised concerns about international travel to and from affected regions during disease outbreaks. The Ebola virus spreads through contact with bodily fluids from infected individuals and causes severe illness with symptoms including fever, severe headache, muscle pain, and bleeding. The DRC has experienced multiple Ebola outbreaks over the past decade, including some of the largest epidemics in history.

The pause represents an effort to balance immigration enforcement operations with public health protection. Federal officials determined that the risk of exposing ICE personnel to the virus, combined with the possibility of introducing the disease to the United States, outweighs the immediate need to complete these particular deportations.

The suspension affects only flights to the DRC. Deportation operations to other countries continue as scheduled. ICE has not announced when flights to the country might resume, stating only that the agency will coordinate with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and other health agencies to assess when conditions allow for safe resumption of deportation flights.

Public health experts have noted that while Ebola poses serious risks in outbreak zones, the disease does not spread easily through casual contact. The virus requires direct contact with infected bodily fluids to transmit, unlike airborne diseases, and has limited capacity for transmission in countries with robust healthcare infrastructure.

The temporary suspension comes as the Trump administration faces increased scrutiny over its expanded immigration detention and deportation programs. A group of 40 House Democrats recently sent a letter to the Federal Aviation Administration raising concerns about the administration's use of charter jets for deportation flights. The lawmakers demanded greater transparency regarding ICE air operations, citing reports of mistreatment and inhumane conditions on these flights, as well as allegations that individuals were placed on flights without notice to counsel or family members.

The administration has previously defended its detention centers and practices, arguing they maintain "higher standards than most US prisons" and that restraints used during flights are "long-standing, standard ICE protocol" designed to ensure safety.

Sonny Rollins, Jazz Saxophone Giant, Dies at 95

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest saxophonists in jazz history, has died at age 95. His death was announced Monday on his website and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He passed away at his home in Woodstock, New...

11:00 PM ET

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest saxophonists in jazz history, has died at age 95. His death was announced Monday on his website and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He passed away at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was disclosed.

Rollins was born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930 and grew up in Harlem. He earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. Inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller, he began learning saxophone at seven years old. His early musical environment was rich with talent, and he played in high school bands with future jazz stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew, and Art Taylor.

A pioneer of bebop and hard bop, Rollins became known for his innovative approach to improvisation and his bright, catchy melodic lines. Miles Davis himself described how Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" for his aggressive, innovative playing and fresh musical ideas. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis later called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong.

Rollins's career faced obstacles in the early 1950s when heroin addiction led him to commit armed robbery in 1950. He spent ten months on Rikers Island in New York but successfully overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955. This recovery sparked an extraordinary creative period. His 1956 album "Saxophone Colossus" featured his signature tune "St Thomas," named after his mother's Caribbean birthplace. In 1959, Rollins took a three-year break from recording and performing, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the pedestrian walkway of the Williamsburg Bridge to avoid disturbing neighbors. This dedication inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge."

Over more than seven decades, Rollins recorded more than 60 albums and collaborated with jazz giants including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, and John Coltrane. His work shaped the development of modern jazz and influenced generations of musicians. Beyond music, he remained socially conscious, campaigning on climate issues and releasing the 1998 album "Global Warming."

Rollins married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004. He took another sabbatical from 1969 to 1971 to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram.

When President Barack Obama presented Rollins with the 2010 National Medal of the Arts in 2011, he said the saxophonist had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken." Rollins's death marks the end of an era, removing one of the last direct links to jazz's golden age.

Google redesigns search box with AI integration after 25 years

Google announced on Tuesday that it would substantially redesign its search interface, embedding artificial intelligence directly into the search box for the first time in the company's nearly 30-year history. The change...

11:00 PM ET

Google announced on Tuesday that it would substantially redesign its search interface, embedding artificial intelligence directly into the search box for the first time in the company's nearly 30-year history. The changes were revealed at Google I/O, the company's annual conference for software developers.

The updated search box will accommodate longer and more specific queries than before, allowing users to ask questions in natural language rather than using search-specific syntax. The redesign directs users to engage directly with Google's AI chatbot, powered by the newly announced Gemini 3.5 model.

Elizabeth Reid, who oversees Google Search, called the changes the biggest update the product has experienced in its history. "Google Search is AI search," she said. The updates rolled out globally to both desktop and mobile versions on Tuesday.

Search results will now display more visual elements and AI-generated summaries rather than primarily showing links to websites. Users can still access the traditional list of links by clicking a tab titled "Web". The changes build on Google's "AI Mode," which launched a year ago and allows users to chat with a Gemini-powered bot. Since that launch, queries to the AI-focused interface have doubled every quarter.

When users add photos, videos or documents to the Chrome browser's search bar, Search will automatically switch to AI mode. Google Search queries reached an all-time high last month, according to the company.

For users with AI Pro and Ultra subscriptions, the service will offer new features including "information agents" that can perform in-depth research and provide summaries or action plans. A "generative user interface" will create customized visuals and interactive dashboards based on user requests. Another new feature called Gemini Spark will access information from Gmail, Google Calendar and other Google products to help users research, shop and plan trips and appointments.

Google also announced a new line of intelligent eyewear developed with Samsung, Warby Parker and Gentle Monster. The glasses will respond to voice commands for Gemini and include embedded cameras for photos and videos. The products are set to launch in fall.

This marks Google's return to wearable technology after its 2014 Google Glass project, which sold for 1,500 dollars and was discontinued the following year. In contrast, competitor Meta has successfully marketed Ray-Ban Meta glasses with voice activation and cameras since 2021.

Josh Woodward, who oversees Gemini's development, described the goal as creating "a universal assistant that's personal, proactive, persistent." Google reported that 900 million people use the Gemini app monthly, compared to ChatGPT's 900 million weekly users.

The redesign represents a fundamental shift in how billions of people access information online, moving from traditional link-based results to AI-generated responses delivered directly within the search interface.
