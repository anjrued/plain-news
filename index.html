A gunman was killed on Saturday after approaching a White House security checkpoint and firing at federal officers, according to officials. The incident resulted in the death of the suspect and injuries to a bystander who was also struck by gunfire.

The shooting occurred shortly after 6 p.m. local time near a checkpoint at the White House gate close to 17th Street and Pennsylvania Avenue NW. According to the Secret Service, a man approached the checkpoint, drew a pistol, and opened fire on officers. Secret Service personnel returned fire, striking the suspect, who was transported to a nearby hospital and later died.

During the gunfire exchange, a bystander was also shot. The Secret Service stated: "It remains unclear whether the bystander was struck by the suspect's initial gunfire or during the subsequent exchange of gunfire." No Secret Service personnel were injured in the incident.

The White House was briefly locked down as the shooting unfolded. Journalists in the area took cover as what witnesses described as dozens of gunshots rang out. ABC News White House correspondent Selina Wang shared video on social media showing her diving for cover while reporting on U.S. negotiations with Iran. She noted that staff were instructed to sprint to the press briefing room. An anchor for DC News Now estimated approximately 30 shots were fired, while a CBS News producer reported at least 20 rounds.

The suspect was already known to the Secret Service before the incident. Court records show the 21-year-old man had previously attempted to enter the White House complex last summer and was arrested at a nearby location. Following that arrest, he was issued a stay away order. Multiple U.S. media outlets reported that he had a history of mental health issues.

President Donald Trump, who was present at the White House during the incident, was not affected by the shooting. Hours later, Trump posted on social media praising the "swift and professional action" of law enforcement officials. He connected the incident to a shooting that occurred a month earlier at a White House Correspondents' Association dinner at a Washington hotel, suggesting the incident demonstrates the importance of enhanced security measures.

The shooting marks the second such incident near the White House in less than three weeks. In the previous incident, a man said to be brandishing a gun fired at a Secret Service officer near the route of Vice President JD Vance's motorcade outside the White House. A civilian witness standing behind the officer was wounded in his leg during that encounter. Officers returned fire, striking the man in the hand, left arm, and upper abdomen.

The Secret Service and other law enforcement agencies continued investigating the Saturday shooting to determine additional details about the suspect's motivations and the circumstances surrounding the incident.

Oil prices dropped to a two-week low as negotiations between the United States and Iran advanced toward a potential deal that would reopen the Strait of Hormuz to commercial shipping.

The strait, through which roughly one-fifth of global oil supplies pass, has been disrupted since the start of the war in February. President Donald Trump claimed on Saturday that a peace deal has been "largely negotiated" and said the strait would be reopened as part of the agreement, though final details remain under discussion.

The prospect of renewed access to this critical waterway has sparked optimism in energy markets. Brent crude and West Texas Intermediate both declined as investors anticipated that reopening the strait would ease supply concerns that had driven prices higher during recent trading sessions.

However, Trump's claims about progress face skepticism from Iran. Iranian officials have given no indication they are prepared to accept the proposed terms, and Tehran's Fars news agency stated the strait would remain under Iranian control. The Pakistani prime minister has indicated more talks would be needed "very soon," suggesting negotiations are not yet concluded.

The impact of the strait's closure has already rippled through global industries beyond energy markets. Toyota reported a 3 billion pound hit from costs linked to the Middle East conflict. The Japanese automaker experienced a 1.9 billion pound increase in materials costs as prices soared, while sales dropped by another 270 billion yen. Toyota's operating profits fell for what the company expects will be the third consecutive year due to the war's effects.

Asian manufacturers have been particularly affected because of their reliance on exports from the Gulf. Japan's automotive industry lobby group noted that 70 percent of the country's aluminium imports come from the Middle East. Oil price increases have also raised the cost of vehicle components including tyres and paint, while transportation expenses have climbed due to disrupted shipping routes.

The Strait of Hormuz connects the Persian Gulf with the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea. Its narrow width and strategic location have made it a recurring flashpoint in regional tensions throughout recent decades. Any disruption to shipping through this waterway has immediate global economic consequences across multiple industries.

Energy traders moved quickly in response to news of potential progress. The prospect of a 60-day ceasefire extension that would reopen the strait has already begun affecting shipping companies' planning. Several major tanker operators have indicated they are preparing to resume routes through the waterway pending formal confirmation of safe passage.

Experts cautioned that while talks show progress, no final agreement has been signed. The negotiations still face potential obstacles before implementation could begin.

The decline came amid growing optimism about diplomatic progress between the two nations. Ships have begun moving toward the Strait of Hormuz, a key shipping lane for global oil transport, as tensions in the region show signs of easing.

Oil futures edged down in early trading Monday as the maritime traffic patterns indicated reduced concern about potential supply disruptions. The Strait of Hormuz is a critical chokepoint for oil shipments, and any threat to its operation typically drives prices higher.

The potential peace deal would mark a significant shift in US-Iran relations, which have been strained for years. While specific terms of the agreement have not been disclosed, the market response suggests traders believe it could reduce risks to energy supply chains.

The oil price movement has rippled through global markets. Japan's Nikkei 225 stock index topped 65,000 for the first time, with investors reacting positively to hopes that the Hormuz strait would remain open for commercial shipping.

Lower oil prices could provide relief to consumers who have faced elevated fuel costs in recent months. Gas prices have jumped ahead of the Memorial Day weekend, though many Americans proceeded with travel plans despite higher costs for both gasoline and airfare.

The decline in crude prices represents a reversal from earlier concerns about potential supply disruptions if the Middle East crisis escalated. Energy markets had been pricing in risk premiums based on the possibility of conflict affecting oil transport routes.

The peace talks come at a time when global energy markets remain sensitive to geopolitical developments. Any agreement that reduces tensions in the Middle East would likely contribute to more stable oil pricing in the months ahead.

Market analysts will be watching closely for official confirmation of a deal and its specific provisions. The maritime traffic patterns and price movements suggest traders are positioning for a positive outcome, though uncertainties remain until a formal agreement is announced.

Star Citizen has become the highest-funded video game project in history, reaching $1 billion in crowdfunding contributions from players. The space simulation game, developed by Cloud Imperium Games, has been in development since 2012 and remains unreleased.

The funding milestone comes as the project continues to sell virtual spaceships and in-game items to backers who contribute to its ongoing development. Players have pledged money over more than a decade, supporting what developer Chris Roberts originally envisioned as a crowdfunded alternative to traditional game publishing.

Cloud Imperium Games also provided an update on Squadron 42, the single-player campaign component of the Star Citizen project. The studio announced progress on the standalone game, though no specific release date was given. Squadron 42 has been delayed multiple times since its original projected launch.

The $1 billion total represents direct contributions from the player community rather than traditional investor or publisher funding. This crowdfunding approach has allowed the developers to work without external corporate oversight, though it has also led to questions about accountability and timelines from some backers.

Star Citizen offers players access to playable alpha builds while development continues. The game aims to create a persistent online universe where players can explore space, trade, fight, and interact. Various gameplay systems remain in development, with the studio regularly releasing updates that add new features and content.

The project employs hundreds of developers across multiple studios. Cloud Imperium Games operates offices in several countries and has grown significantly since the initial crowdfunding campaign launched in 2012.

Crowdfunding for the project continues through the sale of ships and other virtual items, with some individual ships priced at hundreds or thousands of dollars. The ongoing revenue stream has sustained development far beyond the original scope and budget.

The milestone makes Star Citizen an outlier in game development, both for its funding model and its extended production timeline. Most major video games operate on budgets well below $1 billion and complete development within three to five years.

The timing raises particular uncertainty given Messi's previous statements about the 2026 tournament. Last year, he told NBC that his participation would depend on his physical condition and ability to contribute meaningfully to the team.

Governor Jared Polis faced formal disapproval from Colorado's Democratic Party after commuting the prison sentence of Tina Peters, a former Mesa County clerk convicted of allowing unauthorized access to voting equipment.

Polis reduced Peters' nearly nine-year sentence to approximately four and a half years, making her eligible for parole release on June 1. In his clemency letter, Polis wrote that the original sentence was "extremely unusual and lengthy for a first time offender who committed non-violent crimes."

Peters was found guilty of four felonies and three misdemeanors in 2024. In 2021, she permitted Conan Hayes, a former pro surfer affiliated with MyPillow CEO Mike Lindell, to access Mesa County's voting systems, copy equipment data, and attend a sensitive software upgrade. Sensitive passwords and information from the county's voting equipment later were published online by right-wing figures.

The clemency decision prompted sharp criticism from Colorado's top election official. Secretary of State Jena Griswold, a Democrat, said the commutation "validates Trump's basest impulses and emboldens this lawless president." She warned that the decision signals "it's open season on elections" to election officials nationwide and will "embolden the election denial movement across the country."

Griswold learned of Polis' decision only 45 minutes before its public announcement. She disputed Polis' comparison between Peters' case and that of former state senator Sonya Jaquez Lewis, who received probation and community service for submitting forged letters to the legislature. Griswold stated that Peters "organized the breach of the election equipment, broke the public trust and attacked the very foundations of our democratic process."

Matt Crane, executive director of the Colorado Clerks Association, said the group was "furious, disgusted, and deeply disappointed by the governor's decision."

The original sentencing judge, Matthew Barrett, had called Peters "no hero" and "a charlatan who used your prior position in office to peddle snake oil that's been proven to be junk time and time again." An appeals court overturned that sentence in April and ordered reconsideration.

Peters has become a prominent figure in election denial circles. Donald Trump, who continues to spread false claims about the 2020 election, previously issued a federal pardon to Peters, though it carried limited effect for state crimes. He has repeatedly called for her release and instructed the Justice Department to seek her freedom.

Polis, serving his second term as governor, had remained quiet about potential clemency for months before signaling his leanings in early March. The clemency decision and subsequent party censure highlight deep divisions over election security accountability within Colorado's Democratic leadership.
