US Launches Strikes on Iranian Targets as Nuclear Concerns Mount

The United States military conducted strikes targeting Iranian missile launch sites and naval vessels, with US Central Command characterizing the operations as self-defense measures. The military action occurred while senior Iranian negotiators were in Qatar for discussions aimed at ending the conflict.

The strikes have intensified concerns about nuclear safety in the region. Iranian state-linked media claimed the Iranian attacks on the Israeli city of Dimona were in response to alleged US and Israeli strikes on Iranian nuclear facilities at Bushehr and Natanz. Israel has denied conducting any strike on Natanz.

Israeli cities experienced significant damage from Iranian missile attacks. Around 200 people were wounded when Iranian missiles struck Arad and Dimona in southern Israel, with some projectiles bypassing air defense systems. Among the injured were a 12-year-old boy and a 5-year-old girl reported in serious condition. Soroka Medical Center treated 175 people from the attacks on these two cities, with 36 remaining hospitalized as of Sunday morning.

One missile reportedly carrying a conventional warhead weighing several hundred kilograms struck between residential buildings in Arad, causing structural damage and fires. The Israeli air force launched an investigation after the apparent failure to intercept the missile. A Home Front Command inquiry found that most of those injured had not reached bomb shelters when the strike occurred.

The International Atomic Energy Agency said it received no indication of damage to the nuclear facility near Dimona. No abnormal radiation levels were detected. However, UN nuclear watchdog chief Rafael Grossi called for military restraint to avoid any risk of nuclear accidents.

A separate Iranian attack on Tel Aviv involved a cluster munition warhead that dispersed bomblets across a wide area. Emergency services treated 15 people, mostly with light injuries, from this strike.

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu responded to the attacks, stating "We will continue to strike our enemies on all fronts with determination."

The escalating military operations come as diplomatic efforts continue. Secretary of State Marco Rubio indicated that reaching an agreement with Iran could take several days. President Trump stated he viewed the chances of reaching an agreement as fifty-fifty, with a decision expected by the end of the week.

Iranian officials have indicated that contradictory statements from US officials are complicating negotiations. The dual track of military operations and peace talks reflects the volatile nature of current US-Iran relations, with both countries pursuing diplomatic channels while maintaining military pressure. Oil prices have risen globally following the US strikes, adding uncertainty to peace deal prospects.

The warning came after the US military conducted strikes it described as self-defense operations. The exchange of military action represents a significant escalation in tensions between Washington and Tehran, with broad implications for the American military presence throughout the Persian Gulf region.

Khamenei's statement specifically targeted the role of Gulf powers, suggesting that countries hosting US military installations would no longer provide protection for American forces. The supreme leader's remarks signal a potential shift in Iran's approach to US military operations in the region.

These installations serve as important hubs for American military operations throughout the region. Khamenei's warning appears directed at both the United States and the Gulf countries that provide basing rights to US forces.

The statement represents some of the strongest language from Iran's leadership regarding American military presence. Iran has long opposed US military installations near its borders, viewing them as a threat to national security and regional influence.

The situation comes amid broader tensions between Washington and Tehran, complicated further by involvement from Israel. Israeli opposition leader Yair Lapid has criticized what he described as an emerging deal between the US and Iran, calling it detrimental to the region.

Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian has warned that any attack on Khamenei would constitute a declaration of war against the Iranian nation. The military strikes occurred as senior Iranian negotiators were reportedly in Qatar for discussions aimed at ending the broader conflict. This juxtaposition of military action with diplomatic talks highlights the complex and contradictory nature of current US-Iran relations.

The situation continues to develop with potential consequences for American military strategy in the Middle East and the security arrangements that have underpinned US presence in the Gulf region for decades.

Oil prices climbed following U.S. military strikes against Iranian positions, with markets reassessing geopolitical risk even as diplomatic efforts continued. Brent crude rose 1.95 percent to $71.72 per barrel, while WTI gained 2.12 percent to $66.67 per barrel in Thursday trading, after both benchmarks jumped 4 percent overnight.

The strikes came as negotiations between Washington and Tehran had been progressing. Markets had previously rallied on hopes that a U.S.-Iran peace agreement could reduce regional tensions and stabilize global oil supplies. The military action, however, introduced fresh uncertainty into energy markets that had been pricing in reduced geopolitical risk.

Analysts warn that Iran's strategic position makes any disruption particularly consequential. The country sits at the heart of the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly 20 percent of global oil supply transits. Even limited disruption or credible threats to shipping lanes could cause an immediate supply shock that ripples through global supply chains and inflation expectations.

The escalation spooked broader financial markets. Major U.S. stock indexes fell at the open, with the S&P 500 down 0.35 percent, the Nasdaq down 0.49 percent, and the Dow down 0.25 percent. European indexes extended losses, with Germany's DAX down 0.8 percent and France's CAC 40 down 0.79 percent. Investors shifted toward safe-haven assets, with gold prices climbing 0.23 percent to $4,991.58 per barrel.

Markets are currently pricing a 70 percent probability that a U.S. strike on Iran could occur, according to trading data. The timing of the military action raised questions about whether diplomatic efforts could continue and what impact renewed escalation would have on regional stability.

Some market participants appeared complacent about the risks, remaining focused on monetary policy, growth, and artificial intelligence rather than geopolitical threats. However, analysts caution that this muted response could backfire if tensions move from rhetoric to action. Oil could spike rapidly, bond yields could rise on inflation fears, and equities could experience significant volatility.

The strikes represent a significant flare-up in U.S.-Iran tensions after months of discussing potential agreements. Both nations had previously indicated willingness to pursue diplomatic solutions regarding nuclear programs and regional influence. The timing during sensitive negotiations suggests a shift in the U.S. approach, leaving energy traders reassessing forecasts for oil prices and regional stability.

Central banks monitoring inflation expectations will be watching energy markets closely, as any sustained oil price increase could influence interest rate decisions. The development underscores how quickly geopolitical events can reshape financial markets and alter the calculus for global investors.

Sonny Rollins, widely regarded as one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, died at his home in Woodstock, New York, on Monday afternoon at age 95. His death was announced on his website and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. No cause of death was provided.

Rollins was a towering figure in jazz history, helping transform the genre from primarily dance and ballad music into something far more expressively ambitious. Over more than seven decades, he released more than 60 albums and collaborated with jazz legends including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk and John Coltrane. He was among the last surviving stars of the bebop generation that revolutionized jazz in the mid-20th century.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930 and raised in Harlem, Rollins earned his nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began playing saxophone at age seven and quickly absorbed the rich jazz culture surrounding him. His early bands included future stars Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew, and he soon began performing with established musicians like Bud Powell while developing his own compositions.

Rollins became known for his melodic genius and improvisational fearlessness. He described his approach as intuitive rather than analytical, prioritizing feeling over calculation. Miles Davis himself noted that Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" because of his aggressive, innovative style and fresh musical ideas.

A heroin addiction sidetracked his early career. In 1950, he committed armed robbery to support his habit and spent ten months on Rikers Island in New York. After overcoming his addiction through rehabilitation in 1955, he entered an extraordinarily creative period. He released his debut album as bandleader in 1953 and recorded 17 more albums by 1960, including landmarks like "Saxophone Colossus" (1956) featuring his signature tune "St Thomas," and "Freedom Suite" (1958), whose title track became an eloquent statement on civil rights.

Rollins took a famous three-year sabbatical from 1959 to 1962, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway while honing his craft. He returned with "The Bridge" in 1962. Another break from 1969 to 1971 involved studying yoga, philosophy and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout subsequent decades, Rollins remained creatively restless, exploring avant-garde jazz, fusion, Latin music and R&B influences. He composed the soundtrack for the 1966 film "Alfie" and later focused on environmental activism through benefit concerts and his 1998 album "Global Warming."

Rollins was married twice, to Lucille Pearson from 1965 until her death in 2004. He received numerous accolades including a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award and the National Medal of the Arts, which President Barack Obama presented in 2011, calling Rollins an inspiration for taking creative risks.

Trump administration pauses deportation flights to Congo over Ebola outbreak

The decision reflects concern that continuing removals could expose Immigration and Customs Enforcement personnel to the virus. However, public health experts have questioned whether the pa...

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily halted deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo as an Ebola outbreak spreads through the country, according to reporting by Politico. The decision reflects concern that continuing removals could expose Immigration and Customs Enforcement personnel to the virus.

However, public health experts have questioned whether the pause will actually help prevent disease spread. The move also creates complications for individuals already deported to the region. At least one woman was moved to Kinshasa, the capital of the DRC, and officials now say they will not return her despite a judge's order for her return, citing the Ebola travel restrictions.

The temporary suspension represents a departure from the Trump administration's typically aggressive approach to deportations. Officials determined that potential exposure to ICE staff warranted pausing operations to a country experiencing an active Ebola outbreak, prioritizing health and safety considerations over enforcement priorities in this instance.

Ebola is a severe viral disease that spreads through contact with infected bodily fluids. The virus has a high fatality rate and requires strict containment measures. Outbreaks in the DRC have occurred periodically, creating significant health challenges in affected regions.

The pause affects individuals scheduled to be returned to Congo. The administration has not announced when flights might resume or what specific conditions would need to be met for operations to restart. It remains unclear whether similar pauses might be implemented for deportation flights to other countries experiencing disease outbreaks.

The decision comes amid broader scrutiny of the Trump administration's deportation operations. In a letter to the FAA administrator, 40 House Democrats expressed grave concerns about ICE's secretive deportation flight program. The lawmakers cited credible reports that individuals have been placed on flights without notice to counsel or family members, raising due process concerns.

According to the Guardian's investigation based on leaked flight data, the Trump administration has transported detained immigrants in ways that routinely violated constitutional rights. The reporting also identified allegations of abuse at a private detention center in Alexandria, Louisiana, which serves as a central hub in the deportation program.

Human rights groups tracking ICE flights documented an 84 percent increase in the number of deportation flights during 2025 compared to 2024. The lawmakers asked the FAA to provide detailed reports on all ICE air operations since Trump took office, including flight origins, destinations, and passenger numbers.

The Trump administration previously dismissed claims of hidden deportation flights as categorically false and argued its detention centers maintain higher standards than most U.S. prisons. The administration also defended its use of full body restraints on deportation flights as standard protocol designed to ensure safety.

The Congo suspension highlights tensions between enforcement operations and public health concerns during the Trump administration's expanded immigration enforcement agenda.

Rollins was a towering figure in jazz history, helping transform the genre from primarily dance and ballad music into something far more expressively ambitious. Over more than seven decades, he released more than 60 albums and collaborated with jazz legends including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk and John Coltrane. He was among the last surviving stars of the bebop generation that revolutionized jazz in the mid-20th century.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930 and raised in Harlem, Rollins earned his nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began playing saxophone at age seven and quickly absorbed the rich jazz culture surrounding him. His early bands included future stars Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew, and he soon began performing with established musicians like Bud Powell while developing his own compositions.

Rollins became known for his melodic genius and improvisational fearlessness. He described his approach as intuitive rather than analytical, prioritizing feeling over calculation. Miles Davis himself noted that Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" because of his aggressive, innovative style and fresh musical ideas.

A heroin addiction sidetracked his early career. In 1950, he committed armed robbery to support his habit and spent ten months on Rikers Island in New York. After overcoming his addiction through rehabilitation in 1955, he entered an extraordinarily creative period. He released his debut album as bandleader in 1953 and recorded 17 more albums by 1960, including landmarks like "Saxophone Colossus" (1956) featuring his signature tune "St Thomas," and "Freedom Suite" (1958), whose title track became an eloquent statement on civil rights.

Rollins took a famous three-year sabbatical from 1959 to 1962, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway while honing his craft. He returned with "The Bridge" in 1962. Another break from 1969 to 1971 involved studying yoga, philosophy and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout subsequent decades, Rollins remained creatively restless, exploring avant-garde jazz, fusion, Latin music and R&B influences. He composed the soundtrack for the 1966 film "Alfie" and later focused on environmental activism through benefit concerts and his 1998 album "Global Warming."

Rollins was married twice, to Lucille Pearson from 1965 until her death in 2004. He received numerous accolades including a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award and the National Medal of the Arts, which President Barack Obama presented in 2011, calling Rollins an inspiration for taking creative risks.
