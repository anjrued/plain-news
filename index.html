A 31-year-old man was shot and killed by federal agents near the White House after he allegedly opened fire on a Secret Service checkpoint during a high-profile event on Saturday evening.

Cole Tomas Allen, a tutor from Torrance, California, reportedly charged through a security checkpoint outside the White House Correspondents' Association dinner carrying a 12-gauge shotgun, according to court filings. The incident occurred around 8:40 p.m. when Allen approached the checkpoint where Donald Trump was attending the black-tie event with more than 2,500 members of the Washington press corps.

Federal prosecutors allege that Allen had planned the attack for weeks. Court documents include selfies showing Allen in his hotel room wearing a black suit and armed with two firearms and multiple knives. Prosecutors state the weapons were later confiscated from Allen after the incident.

A Secret Service agent engaged Allen at the checkpoint, and surveillance footage reviewed by the Washington Post shows the officer drawing his weapon within two seconds of Allen's arrival. The officer then fired four times. One officer was shot during the exchange but remained uninjured due to a bulletproof vest, according to Washington police chief Jeffery Carroll.

Allen was taken to a nearby hospital and later pronounced dead. He was not armed during a previous incident in 2025 when he attempted to access the White House complex and was arrested nearby for trespassing, according to court records filed in DC Superior Court.

Court filings reveal Allen had sent prescheduled emails minutes before the attack. In one message, he allegedly wrote that he was "no longer willing to permit a pedophile, rapist, and traitor to coat my hands with his crimes." He allegedly outlined a set of rules to follow during the attack and signed letters with aliases including "coldForce" and "Friendly Federal Assassin."

These same aliases appeared frequently on social media platforms and gaming forums where Allen was active. On sites including X, YouTube, and Twitch, he operated as "@CForce3000" and on Steam as "Ice Forged Games." Since Trump's 2024 election victory, Allen's online posts shifted from gaming discussions to political commentary, including comparisons of Trump to Adolf Hitler and encouragement for others to purchase firearms.

Allen purchased his first firearm in October 2023 and the 12-gauge shotgun used in the incident in August 2025, according to firearm records in court filings. He appeared in federal court for the first time on Monday.

Prosecutors have asked the court to keep Allen detained before trial, describing evidence of guilt as "overwhelming." If convicted, Allen faces a possible life sentence and a mandatory minimum of 10 years in prison. He has not yet entered a plea.

Oil prices fell sharply as Iran announced the Strait of Hormuz would be open to commercial shipping, signaling progress in peace negotiations between the United States and Iran. The announcement reflected a significant shift in market sentiment after weeks of regional tensions that had driven energy costs higher.

Brent crude, the international benchmark, dropped more than 10 percent to $88.80 a barrel on Friday, well below a peak of $119 reached last month but still substantially higher than the $72 level before the conflict began. The price decline came as Iran's foreign minister said vessels would be permitted to transit the strait during a 10-day ceasefire between Israel and Lebanon that was struck Thursday.

West Texas Intermediate futures also edged down as multiple vessels began moving toward the critical chokepoint, through which roughly one fifth of global oil supplies pass daily. Before the crisis, more than 130 ships per day traveled through the strait, but traffic had reduced to a trickle as approximately 800 tankers became stranded in the Gulf.

The market shift reflects growing optimism about potential diplomatic progress. Donald Trump stated the deal "should go very quickly" because "most of the points are already negotiated." He said the agreement would include reopening the Strait of Hormuz, though he added that a US naval blockade on Iran would remain in full force until a formal deal was reached.

The strait has long represented a focal point for geopolitical risk in oil markets. Any disruption to shipping through the narrow waterway can quickly send prices higher due to its critical role in global energy trade. The return of normal vessel traffic suggests shipping companies and insurers view immediate risks as diminished.

However, significant uncertainty remains. Iran's foreign minister said tankers using the designated route, nicknamed the "Tehran tollbooth," must follow specific procedures. Earlier reporting indicated tankers granted permission to pass have been required to pay approximately $2 million for safe passage, though it remains unclear whether this fee would apply to all vessels.

Energy analysts note that even preliminary progress between Washington and Tehran could provide lasting relief to oil markets. The head of the International Chamber of Shipping offered cautious optimism, stating "an orderly and sustained return to normal transit through the Strait will be essential" and calling for close coordination between international maritime authorities, regional states, and the shipping industry.

European gas markets also responded positively, with the benchmark European gas contract falling about 6.4 percent to approximately 39 euros per megawatt hour. Stock markets in Germany, France, the United Kingdom, and the United States gained on the news, reflecting broader confidence that reduced energy supply risks could benefit the wider economy.

Colorado Democrats censure governor over clemency for election conspiracy convict

The Colorado Democratic Party has formally censured Governor Jared Polis for granting clemency to Tina Peters, a former county clerk convicted in an election security breach case. Peters served as Mesa County clerk in 2020 when she allowed an unauthorized person to use a security badge to acces...

May 21, 10:12 AM ET

The Colorado Democratic Party has formally censured Governor Jared Polis for granting clemency to Tina Peters, a former county clerk convicted in an election security breach case. The censure represents a rare public rebuke of a sitting governor by his own party.

Peters served as Mesa County clerk in 2020 when she allowed an unauthorized person to use a security badge to access the county's voting equipment. Passwords and other sensitive information related to the election equipment became public and were used by election deniers to question the 2020 election results. A jury found her guilty in 2024 of attempting to influence a public servant, conspiracy to commit criminal impersonation, first-degree official misconduct, and other charges. She was sentenced to nine years in prison.

Peters has become the only person currently serving a prison sentence related to efforts to overturn the 2020 election. Donald Trump repeatedly urged Polis to pardon her and issued a federal pardon in her name, though it had no effect on her state conviction.

Polis signaled he was considering clemency in a post on social media Tuesday, comparing Peters's case to that of former state senator Sonya Jaquez Lewis. Lewis was convicted of attempting to influence a public official but received probation and community service rather than prison time.

"It is not lost on me that she was convicted of the exact same felony charge as Tina Peters and yet Tina Peters, as a non-violent first time offender got a nine year sentence," Polis wrote, calling attention to sentencing disparities.

The comparison sparked immediate pushback from Democratic officials. Colorado Secretary of State Jena Griswold said the cases were not comparable, noting that Lewis was convicted on one count of attempting to influence a public servant and three counts of forgery, whereas Peters "organized the breach of the election equipment, broke the public trust and attacked the very foundations of our democratic process."

Griswold stated that Peters's actions continue to be used to undermine elections and that "she should get no special treatment by the Governor."

When the Colorado Sun asked every Democrat in the state legislature whether Polis should reduce Peters's sentence, none said he should. State Representative Karen McCormick called it "a ridiculous idea."

U.S. Senator Michael Bennet, a Democrat running for Colorado governor, also opposed clemency. "Tina Peters knowingly broke the law, undermined our elections, and was rightfully convicted by a jury of her peers," he said.

The district attorney who prosecuted Peters called any sentence modification "a gross injustice," noting that sentencing ranges exist because the same offense can be committed in different ways with different consequences.
