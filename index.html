Multiple people died and others were injured after a chemical tank imploded at a paper and packaging mill in Longview, Washington. Authorities confirmed fatalities following what officials described as a major chemical implosion at the facility.

The incident occurred at a pulp and paper mill in Longview, located in southwestern Washington. Emergency responders arrived at the scene following reports of the implosion. Some individuals remain missing after the incident.

The tank implosion caused injuries to multiple workers at the plant. Authorities have not released the exact number of fatalities or the identities of those killed. The facility produces paper and packaging materials.

Emergency officials continue to work at the site. The cause of the implosion has not been determined. Investigators are working to account for all workers who were at the facility at the time of the incident.

The Longview mill is a significant employer in the area. Paper mills typically store chemicals used in the pulping and bleaching processes. The specific chemical involved in the implosion has not been identified by authorities.

Local emergency management agencies responded to the incident along with fire and rescue teams. The facility has been secured as the investigation continues. Officials have not provided a timeline for when operations might resume.

Family members of workers at the plant gathered near the facility seeking information about their loved ones. Authorities established a system for notifying families of those affected by the incident.

The incident marks one of the most serious industrial accidents in the region in recent years. Washington state workplace safety officials are expected to investigate the circumstances that led to the tank failure. Federal workplace safety regulators may also review the incident.

The facility's safety record and inspection history will likely be examined as part of the investigation. No evacuation orders were issued for surrounding neighborhoods following the implosion.

Ride-share drivers working for Uber and Lyft in Massachusetts have successfully organized the first statewide union for ride-share workers in the United States. State officials formally certified the union after drivers voted to support the organizing effort, establishing a significant precedent for gig economy workers seeking collective bargaining rights.

The unionization represents a major development in how gig economy workers are treated and represented. Historically, Uber and Lyft have classified their drivers as independent contractors rather than employees, which has excluded them from many workplace protections and benefits available to traditional employees. This new union structure offers a different model for organizing workers in the gig economy while maintaining their independent contractor status.

Rising concerns about job security have fueled the organizing effort. As Uber and Lyft continue investing in autonomous vehicle technology, drivers worry about their long-term prospects in the industry. The new union will negotiate directly with the ride-sharing platforms on behalf of Massachusetts drivers.

Drivers have identified several key issues they want addressed through union negotiations. These include minimum earnings guarantees, access to benefits, declining pay rates, and greater transparency in how the platforms set policies that affect their work. Many drivers have complained that their compensation has decreased while their responsibilities have remained constant.

Massachusetts has been central to debates over gig economy worker classification and regulation. State lawmakers and labor advocates have closely monitored the unionization effort as a potential model for protecting gig workers. The success in Massachusetts could inspire similar organizing campaigns in other states, where labor groups have been working to unionize workers across various gig economy sectors, from delivery services to transportation.

Union leaders have acknowledged that traditional union structures may require significant adaptation to function effectively in the ride-share industry. Drivers typically set their own schedules and often work across multiple platforms simultaneously, creating organizational challenges that differ from conventional employment relationships. The union has indicated it will focus on addressing these unique circumstances while advocating for better working conditions.

Both Uber and Lyft have faced increasing pressure in recent years to improve driver compensation and working conditions. The companies have made various efforts to address driver concerns, though these initiatives have sometimes been viewed skeptically by workers. The formation of this union provides drivers with a formal mechanism to negotiate collectively with the platforms.

The Massachusetts certification marks a significant shift in labor organizing efforts. As other states consider similar measures to provide gig workers with greater protections and bargaining power, the union's structure and approach will likely serve as a template for organizing efforts elsewhere in the country.

1:42 PM ET

Sonny Rollins, widely considered one of the greatest saxophonists in jazz history, died at age 95. His death was announced Monday on his official website and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He passed away at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided.

Rollins shaped the landscape of modern jazz across more than seven decades, releasing over 60 albums starting in the late 1940s. He collaborated with jazz legends including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane, and many others. He was among the last surviving stars of the bebop generation, helping transform jazz from primarily dance and ballad music into a form of startling emotional expression.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930 and raised in Harlem, he earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began playing saxophone at age seven, inspired by his siblings' musical talents and jazz pioneers like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. By high school, his bands featured future stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew, and Art Taylor.

Rollins became known for his melodic genius and innovative improvisational approach. Miles Davis described how Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" for his aggressive, innovative playing style. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong.

His early career was interrupted by heroin addiction. After committing armed robbery in 1950 to support his habit, he spent 10 months in jail. He overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955, which sparked an extraordinary creative period.

Rollins released his debut album as a bandleader in 1953. His 1956 album "Saxophone Colossus" featured the signature composition "St. Thomas," inspired by his mother's Caribbean heritage. Other landmark recordings included "Way Out West" in 1957 and "Freedom Suite" in 1958, which featured a 20-minute title track that became an artistic statement for civil rights.

In 1959, Rollins took a three-year break from recording and performing, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway. He returned in 1962 with the album "The Bridge." He took another extended sabbatical from 1969 to 1971 to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout his career, Rollins continued exploring new musical directions, incorporating avant-garde jazz, fusion, Latin music, funk, and calypso influences. Later in life, he campaigned for environmental awareness, releasing the 1998 album "Global Warming."

President Barack Obama presented Rollins with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, acknowledging how the saxophonist had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Rollins married twice: briefly to Dawn Finney in 1957, and then to Lucille Pearson in 1965, remaining together until her death in 2004.

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily halted deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo in response to a widening Ebola outbreak in the country. According to reporting by Politico, the decision reflects concerns about exposing Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials to the virus and potentially bringing the disease closer to the United States.

The suspension affects ICE deportation operations that had been transporting individuals back to Congo. Officials determined the health risks associated with continuing flights during the outbreak outweighed operational priorities of the deportation program.

The pause has created complications for some deportees. At least one woman has found herself in legal limbo after officials moved her to Kinshasa, the capital of the DRC. Despite a judge's order for her return, administrators say they will not bring her back because of the Ebola travel restrictions.

Experts have questioned whether the flight pause will effectively prevent disease spread. Public health officials have raised concerns about the strategy's actual impact on containing the outbreak.

The Ebola outbreak in Congo has prompted health authorities to implement restrictions in affected areas. The virus spreads through direct contact with bodily fluids of infected individuals and can cause severe illness and death. The region has experienced multiple outbreaks in recent years that have required significant international public health responses.

ICE personnel who staff deportation flights would face potential exposure during operations in areas affected by the outbreak. The decision to pause flights reflects concern about protecting federal employees from infection. Bringing exposed personnel or infected individuals back to the United States could also create domestic public health risks.

The pause applies specifically to flights bound for Congo and does not affect deportation operations to other countries. The administration has not announced a timeline for when flights might resume. The decision will likely depend on the trajectory of the Ebola outbreak and assessments from public health officials about when it would be safe to restart operations.

This temporary suspension comes as the Trump administration has generally pursued an aggressive deportation agenda. The health-based pause represents a specific operational adjustment rather than a broader policy shift. Other deportation flights to countries without active disease outbreaks continue as scheduled.

The Democratic Republic of Congo's national soccer team has also been affected by the outbreak. The team cancelled its training activities in Kinshasa due to health restrictions related to the current outbreak, though officials say the team still plans to travel to the United States for pre-World Cup preparation.

Jazz saxophonist Sonny Rollins dies at 95

Sonny Rollins, widely considered one of the greatest saxophonists in jazz history, died at age 95. His death was announced Monday on his official website and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He passed away at his...

3:00 PM ET

Sonny Rollins, widely considered one of the greatest saxophonists in jazz history, died at age 95. His death was announced Monday on his official website and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He passed away at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided.

Rollins shaped the landscape of modern jazz across more than seven decades, releasing over 60 albums starting in the late 1940s. He collaborated with jazz legends including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane, and many others. He was among the last surviving stars of the bebop generation, helping transform jazz from primarily dance and ballad music into a form of startling emotional expression.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930 and raised in Harlem, he earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began playing saxophone at age seven, inspired by his siblings' musical talents and jazz pioneers like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. By high school, his bands featured future stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew, and Art Taylor.

Rollins became known for his melodic genius and innovative improvisational approach. Miles Davis described how Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" for his aggressive, innovative playing style. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong.

His early career was interrupted by heroin addiction. After committing armed robbery in 1950 to support his habit, he spent 10 months in jail. He overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955, which sparked an extraordinary creative period.

Rollins released his debut album as a bandleader in 1953. His 1956 album "Saxophone Colossus" featured the signature composition "St. Thomas," inspired by his mother's Caribbean heritage. Other landmark recordings included "Way Out West" in 1957 and "Freedom Suite" in 1958, which featured a 20-minute title track that became an artistic statement for civil rights.

In 1959, Rollins took a three-year break from recording and performing, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway. He returned in 1962 with the album "The Bridge." He took another extended sabbatical from 1969 to 1971 to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout his career, Rollins continued exploring new musical directions, incorporating avant-garde jazz, fusion, Latin music, funk, and calypso influences. Later in life, he campaigned for environmental awareness, releasing the 1998 album "Global Warming."

President Barack Obama presented Rollins with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, acknowledging how the saxophonist had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Rollins married twice: briefly to Dawn Finney in 1957, and then to Lucille Pearson in 1965, remaining together until her death in 2004.
