Oil prices climbed following US military strikes on Iranian positions, even as diplomatic efforts to resolve the conflict between the two nations continue. Brent crude and WTI both rose approximately 4% overnight, with Brent reaching $71.72 per barrel and WTI climbing to $66.67 by Thursday afternoon.

The strikes introduced fresh uncertainty into ongoing peace talks. Iran condemned the military action as a violation of the existing ceasefire and warned of retaliation, while US officials characterized the operations as defensive measures. Despite the escalation, President Trump stated that negotiations with Iran are "proceeding nicely," and Secretary of State Marco Rubio suggested a peace deal could be finalized within days.

The timing created confusion about the direction of US-Iran relations. Trump had skipped his son's wedding to remain at the White House as military strikes were being prepared, but then announced on Saturday that an agreement would be concluded "shortly." Rubio subsequently said "good news" would come soon, though Iran's leaders quickly dismissed Trump's claims as propaganda.

Markets reacted swiftly to the military escalation. Oil traders cited concerns about potential supply disruptions, particularly given Iran's status as a major oil producer and its control of the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly 20 percent of global oil supply passes. Even limited disruption to shipping lanes could cause immediate supply shocks. Analysts noted that markets were pricing in a 70 percent probability of further US strikes on Iran.

Beyond energy markets, the strikes rippled across financial sectors. Gold prices rose 0.23 percent to $4,991.58 as investors sought safe-haven assets. Major US stock indexes opened in negative territory, with the Dow down 0.25 percent, the S&P 500 down 0.35 percent, and the Nasdaq down 0.49 percent. European markets extended losses, with the FTSE 100 down 0.6 percent, Germany's DAX down 0.8 percent, and France's CAC 40 down 0.79 percent.

According to reports, the proposed peace agreement includes provisions for Iran to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within 30 days of a deal being reached. This critical waterway has been central to concerns about global supply disruptions and oil price volatility.

Market analysts warned that investors appeared complacent about escalation risks, focusing instead on monetary policy, growth, and artificial intelligence developments. This inattention could prove costly if tensions moved from rhetoric to action, potentially triggering rapid oil spikes, rising bond yields driven by inflation fears, and equity market volatility.

The international community watched closely as both sides maintained diplomatic channels while military tensions persisted, leaving the trajectory of US-Iran relations uncertain.

Gabbard cited personal reasons for her departure. "My husband, Abraham, has recently been diagnosed with an extremely rare form of bone cancer," she wrote. "At this time, I must step away from public service to be by his side and fully support him through this battle."

Reuters reported that the White House forced Gabbard to resign, citing a source familiar with the matter. However, a spokesperson for her office denied these claims, stating that rumors of her being forcibly ousted were "100% false."

Trump confirmed the resignation on Friday through a statement on his Truth Social platform. "Unfortunately, after having done a great job, Tulsi Gabbard will be leaving the Administration on June 30th," he wrote, adding that she "has done an incredible job, and we will miss her." Aaron Lukas, principal deputy director of national intelligence, will serve as acting director.

Gabbard's tenure was marked by significant challenges. The former Democratic congresswoman from Hawaii, who later became a Republican, lacked an intelligence background and brought unconventional views to the role. Over time, she became increasingly sidelined by Trump, who excluded her from key national security discussions regarding Iran and Venezuela, according to people familiar with the office.

Last month, Trump asked cabinet members whether he should replace Gabbard, indicating growing dissatisfaction with her leadership. Despite her efforts to align with the president's agenda and her promises to root out what she viewed as politicization in US spy agencies, her position continued to weaken.

Gabbard had taken unusual steps to ingratiate herself with Trump, embracing his election denial agenda while vowing to investigate perceived internal threats within intelligence agencies. However, her resistance to foreign military interventions at times diverged from the administration's aggressive stance toward countries like Venezuela and Iran, which contributed to her marginalization.

The position of director of national intelligence oversees 18 intelligence agencies, including the CIA, NSA, and FBI. It requires Senate confirmation, and finding a replacement could prove contentious given the sensitive nature of the role and ongoing political divisions over intelligence matters.

Gabbard was a controversial choice from the start, with critics questioning her previous foreign policy statements and meetings with foreign leaders. Her departure marks a significant leadership change as the Trump administration navigates complex intelligence challenges and geopolitical tensions.

BP has removed its chairman Albert Manifold with immediate effect after the oil company's board identified "serious concerns about important governance standards, oversight and conduct." The FTSE 100 company announced the decision on Tuesday without disclosing further details about the specific issues involved.

Manifold's departure came after just eight months in the role. The announcement triggered a sharp market reaction, with BP's share price dropping 9% immediately after the news before recovering slightly to a 6% decline, making it the biggest faller on the FTSE index that day.

Amanda Blanc, senior independent director at BP and chief executive of insurance company Aviva, said the board had been "surprised and disappointed to learn of governance oversight and conduct issues it deems unacceptable and has taken decisive action."

Manifold joined BP's board in October 2025 with a mandate to oversee the company's strategic transformation, particularly refocusing the business on fossil fuel extraction and pulling back from renewable energy investments. He quickly made major leadership changes, removing chief executive Murray Auchincloss after less than two years in the role and replacing him with Meg O'Neill in December.

The removal marks the second significant leadership departure at BP for conduct-related reasons in three years. Bernard Looney, Auchincloss's predecessor as chief executive, was forced out in September 2023 after failing to disclose sexual relationships with colleagues. BP's board later determined it had been misled and denied Looney 32 million pounds in pay and shares.

Ian Tyler has been appointed as interim chairman effective immediately. Tyler is a former chief executive of infrastructure company Balfour Beatty and currently serves as chair of building supplies firm Grafton Group and as a director at mining company Anglo American, all FTSE-listed businesses.

The timing of Manifold's removal is significant given the aggressive pace of change he had implemented at BP. In just over a year, he reshaped the company's strategic direction and senior management team. However, the exact nature of the governance and conduct concerns that triggered his dismissal remains undisclosed by the company.

The removal represents a rare public acknowledgment of governance problems at the highest level of a major energy company. While BP's board chose to cite conduct concerns specifically, it has declined to elaborate on whether these issues relate to bullying behaviour or other matters, as suggested by some reporting.

Investors will likely scrutinize how quickly BP installs permanent leadership and whether the company provides any additional clarity on the circumstances surrounding Manifold's departure.

8:13 AM ET

NASA has added more astronaut transport missions to its contract with SpaceX due to Boeing's ongoing inability to certify its crew spacecraft. The space agency announced the contract expansion on Tuesday, extending SpaceX's role as the primary provider of crew rotation flights to the International Space Station.

The decision stems from delays in Boeing's Starliner program, which has faced repeated setbacks in achieving NASA certification for regular crewed missions. SpaceX has been flying astronauts to the station since 2020 under the Commercial Crew Program, while Boeing has yet to complete the certification process necessary to begin regular service.

NASA initially designed the Commercial Crew Program with two providers to ensure redundancy in accessing the space station. The agency awarded contracts to both SpaceX and Boeing in 2014, intending for both companies to provide independent transportation capabilities. Boeing received a larger initial contract value than SpaceX, but the company has struggled to meet program milestones.

The additional missions assigned to SpaceX will ensure continued American access to the orbiting laboratory while Boeing works through its certification challenges. NASA has not specified how many additional flights were added to the SpaceX contract or provided a timeline for when Boeing might achieve certification.

SpaceX uses its Crew Dragon spacecraft, launched atop Falcon 9 rockets, to transport astronauts. The company has completed numerous successful missions to the station, establishing a reliable cadence of crew rotations approximately every six months. Each mission typically carries four astronauts and remains docked at the station for about six months before returning the previous crew to Earth.

Boeing's Starliner has completed uncrewed test flights but has encountered various technical issues that have prevented final certification. The spacecraft launches on United Launch Alliance Atlas V rockets. Boeing has not publicly announced a new target date for achieving operational status.

The contract modification reflects NASA's practical need to maintain continuous crew presence aboard the International Space Station. The station requires regular crew rotations to conduct scientific research and maintain the facility. Without a second certified provider, NASA must rely solely on SpaceX for these critical transportation services.

Kyle Busch, a two-time NASCAR Cup Series champion, has died at age 41. NASCAR announced his death on Thursday, saying he had been hospitalized with a severe illness. His family later confirmed he died from severe pneumonia and sepsis.

"We are saddened and heartbroken to share the news of the passing of Kyle Busch, a two-time Cup champion and one of our sport's greatest and fiercest drivers," NASCAR said in a statement.

Busch's health issues began during a NASCAR Cup Series race on May 10 at Watkins Glen in upstate New York. He had been struggling with a sinus cold that worsened due to the intense G-forces and elevation changes at the road course. During the race, Busch radioed his crew requesting medical assistance and a shot from Dr. Bill Heisel after the race ended. Despite his condition, he continued racing and finished eighth.

Just six days before his death, Busch won a trucks race at Dover on Friday. His family announced his hospitalization on Thursday, saying he would not compete in the NASCAR race scheduled for that weekend in Charlotte, North Carolina.

Busch was a Las Vegas native who won NASCAR Cup Series championships in 2015 and 2019. In his career spanning more than two decades, he set records across NASCAR's three national series with 232 total wins, more than any driver in history. He had competed in his fourth season at Richard Childress Racing after winning titles with Joe Gibbs Racing.

The 2005 NASCAR rookie of the year was known as "Rowdy" and "Wild Thing" for his competitive nature and sometimes controversial behavior on and off the track. He was a polarizing figure who created what fans called "Rowdy Nation" through his passionate approach to racing.

In a joint statement, his family, Richard Childress Racing, and NASCAR described Busch as "a rare talent, one who comes along once in a generation. He was fierce, he was passionate, he was immensely skilled and he cared deeply about the sport and fans. Throughout a career that spanned more than two decades, Kyle set records in national series wins, won championships at NASCAR's highest level and fostered the next generation of drivers as an owner in the Truck Series."

The statement concluded: "NASCAR lost a giant of the sport today, far too soon."

Busch is survived by his wife, Samantha, and two children, Brexton and Lennix. At the time of his death, he ranked 24th in the Cup Series standings with two top-10 finishes in 12 races that season.

Sonny Rollins, widely regarded as one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, died at age 95. His death was announced on his official website on Monday and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was disclosed.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he grew up in Harlem and earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. Inspired by his musical siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller, he began playing saxophone at age seven. His early high school band included future stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew, and Art Taylor.

Rollins became a defining figure in jazz history, working alongside Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane, and other pioneers. He helped transform jazz from primarily dance and ballad music into something far more expressive. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting Rollins with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, President Barack Obama credited him with inspiring him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

His career spanned more than six decades, producing more than 60 albums. Key works included "Saxophone Colossus" from 1956, which featured his signature tune "St Thomas," and "Way Out West" from 1957, which explored a piano-free "strolling" style. His 1958 album "Freedom Suite" contained a 20-minute title track that became an elegant statement on freedom during the civil rights movement.

Rollins took two notable breaks from performing. In 1959, he stepped away for three years to refine his craft, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway. This unconventional practice space inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge." He took another sabbatical between 1969 and 1971 to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout his later career, Rollins explored diverse styles including Latin music, avant-garde jazz, and funk. He composed the soundtrack to the 1966 film "Alfie" and added uncredited saxophone to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album "Tattoo You." In the 1980s and beyond, he remained creatively restless, even campaigning on climate issues with benefit concerts and his 1998 album "Global Warming."

Rollins married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004. He continued performing into his 80s before retiring from touring. His extensive recorded legacy has influenced generations of musicians and remains widely studied by jazz professionals and students worldwide.

The decision came as health concerns mounted over the spiraling outbreak and the potential risks of transporting people...

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily halted deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo due to a widening Ebola outbreak in the country.

According to reporting by Politico, the pause applies specifically to removals of individuals to the DRC. The decision came as health concerns mounted over the spiraling outbreak and the potential risks of transporting people to the disease-stricken region.

The suspension creates complications for some individuals already caught in the system. At least one woman has been moved to Kinshasa, the capital of the DRC, but officials now say they will not bring her back because of the travel restrictions, despite a judge's order for her return. This situation raises questions about how the administration will handle cases where deportations have already been initiated.

Public health experts have questioned whether the pause will actually prevent disease spread. The move primarily appears designed to protect Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials from potential virus exposure and to limit the risk of the disease reaching the United States.

Ebola is a severe viral disease that spreads through direct contact with bodily fluids of infected people or animals. Outbreaks can be deadly, with fatality rates varying by virus strain and available medical care.

The administration has not announced when flights to Congo might resume. The decision will likely depend on how the outbreak progresses and guidance from health officials about travel safety to affected regions.

The temporary pause affects an unknown number of individuals who were scheduled for deportation. The administration has not indicated whether similar precautions will be applied to deportation flights to other countries if disease outbreaks occur there.

This decision represents a notable exception to the administration's generally forceful deportation operations, which have typically proceeded regardless of conditions in destination countries. The Trump administration has pursued an aggressive immigration agenda, with human rights groups tracking an 84 percent increase in ICE flights during 2025 compared to 2024.

However, broader questions have emerged about the administration's deportation practices. A group of 40 House Democrats recently expressed concerns about secretive deportation flights and requested that the Federal Aviation Administration address allegations of mistreatment and inhumane conditions on ICE charter jets. The lawmakers cited reports that individuals have been placed on flights without notice to counsel or family members and that flights have been shielded from public tracking systems.

The administration previously defended its detention and transfer practices, describing its detention centers as having "higher standards than most US prisons" and characterizing its use of restraints as "long-standing, standard ICE protocol" designed to ensure safety.

The DRC has faced multiple Ebola outbreaks in recent years. The country's World Cup team cancelled its training in Kinshasa due to health restrictions but still plans to travel to the United States for preparation ahead of the tournament.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he grew up in Harlem and earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. Inspired by his musical siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller, he began playing saxophone at age seven. His early high school band included future stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew, and Art Taylor.

Rollins became a defining figure in jazz history, working alongside Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane, and other pioneers. He helped transform jazz from primarily dance and ballad music into something far more expressive. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. His career spanned more than six decades, producing more than 60 albums. Key works included "Saxophone Colossus" from 1956, which featured his signature tune "St Thomas," and "Way Out West" from 1957, which explored a piano-free "strolling" style. His 1958 album "Freedom Suite" contained a 20-minute title track that became an elegant statement on freedom during the civil rights movement.

Rollins took two notable breaks from performing. In 1959, he stepped away for three years to refine his craft, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway. This unconventional practice space inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge." He took another sabbatical between 1969 and 1971 to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout his later career, Rollins explored diverse styles including Latin music, avant-garde jazz, and funk. He composed the soundtrack to the 1966 film "Alfie" and added uncredited saxophone to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album "Tattoo You." In the 1980s and beyond, he remained creatively restless, even campaigning on climate issues with benefit concerts and his 1998 album "Global Warming."

Rollins married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004. He continued performing into his 80s before retiring from touring. His extensive recorded legacy has influenced generations of musicians and remains widely studied by jazz professionals and students worldwide.
