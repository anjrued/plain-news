8:43 AM ET

BP has removed its chairman Albert Manifold effective immediately, following what the oil company's board described as "serious concerns about important governance standards, oversight and conduct." The FTSE 100 company made the announcement on Tuesday but did not provide specific details about the issues involved.

Manifold's tenure as chairman lasted only eight months. He was appointed to BP's board in October 2025 with a mandate to oversee the company's strategic shift back toward fossil fuel extraction and away from renewable energy investments. His departure triggered a sharp market reaction, with BP's share price falling 9% in the minutes after the announcement before recovering slightly to a 6% decline, making it the worst-performing stock on the FTSE 100 that day.

Amanda Blanc, BP's senior independent director and chief executive of insurance company Aviva, commented on the decision. "Albert has helped bring a welcome focus and pace to BP's transformation," she said. "However, the board has been surprised and disappointed to learn of governance oversight and conduct issues it deems unacceptable and has taken decisive action."

During his brief tenure, Manifold made significant leadership changes. He removed chief executive Murray Auchincloss after less than two years in the role and hired Meg O'Neill to replace him in December.

This marks the second time in three years that BP has removed a senior leader for conduct-related reasons. In September 2023, former chief executive Bernard Looney was forced out after failing to disclose sexual relationships with colleagues when he was appointed to the role. BP's board later determined it had been misled and denied Looney 32 million pounds in pay and shares.

The board has appointed Ian Tyler as interim chairman with immediate effect. Tyler is a former chief executive of Balfour Beatty, the FTSE 250 infrastructure company. He currently serves as chairman of Grafton Group, a FTSE 250 building supplies company, and as a director of Anglo American, a FTSE 100 mining company.

The swift removal of Manifold suggests the board viewed the governance concerns as serious enough to warrant immediate action. The lack of detail about the specific conduct issues has left questions about what triggered the decision and may prompt scrutiny from shareholders about governance practices at the oil giant. The circumstances of Manifold's departure will likely be closely examined by investors and the energy industry as BP navigates further leadership transitions.

8:13 AM ET

NASA has assigned additional crew missions to SpaceX for flights to the International Space Station, citing ongoing delays in Boeing's spacecraft certification process. The space agency announced the contract modification to ensure continued crew rotation capabilities while Boeing works to complete its certification requirements.

SpaceX has been flying astronauts to the space station since 2020 under NASA's Commercial Crew Program. Boeing was selected alongside SpaceX in 2014 to provide crew transportation services, but has faced repeated delays in certifying its Starliner spacecraft for operational missions.

The additional missions awarded to SpaceX will maintain NASA's ability to rotate crew members on the space station while Boeing continues its certification work. NASA originally contracted both companies to provide redundancy in crew transportation, ensuring the agency would not depend on a single provider for access to the orbiting laboratory.

Boeing has not yet certified its Starliner spacecraft for regular crew flights to the station. The company completed an uncrewed test flight in 2022 and flew its first crewed test mission in 2024, but has not received final certification from NASA to begin regular operational missions.

The contract modification with SpaceX ensures NASA can maintain its planned crew rotation schedule regardless of when Boeing achieves certification. The space agency requires regular crew exchanges approximately every six months to maintain continuous operations aboard the space station.

SpaceX has completed dozens of crew missions for NASA since its first operational flight in 2020. The company uses its Crew Dragon spacecraft, which launches atop a Falcon 9 rocket and can carry up to seven astronauts. Boeing's Starliner launches on United Launch Alliance's Atlas V rocket and can carry up to seven crew members.

NASA has not announced a timeline for when Boeing might complete its certification process. The agency emphasizes that safety requirements and thorough testing must be satisfied before any spacecraft receives approval for regular crew missions. The additional SpaceX missions will fill gaps in the crew rotation schedule that would otherwise depend on Boeing's availability.

Busch's family announced his hospitalization earlier that Thursday, stating he would not compete in the planned NASCAR race in Charlotte, North Carolina that weekend. No official cause of death was initially disclosed by NASCAR, though medical reports later indicated pneumonia and sepsis contributed to his death.

"We are saddened and heartbroken to share the news of the passing of Kyle Busch," NASCAR wrote on social media. "He was 41 years old."

The Las Vegas native won NASCAR Cup Series championships in 2015 and 2019. He spent his final four seasons racing for Richard Childress Racing after earlier titles with Joe Gibbs Racing. This season, Busch ranked 24th in Cup Series standings with two top-10 finishes in 12 races.

Just six days before his death, Busch won a trucks race at Dover. During a Cup Series race on May 10 at Watkins Glen, he had radioed his crew requesting medical assistance from Dr Bill Heisel. According to the broadcast, Busch was struggling with a sinus cold that was made worse by the intense G-forces and elevation changes at the upstate New York road course. Despite these difficulties, he continued racing and finished eighth.

Busch holds the record for most wins across NASCAR's three national series with 232 victories, more than any driver in history. He burst onto the Cup Series scene in 2005 by winning rookie of the year. Known by nicknames "Rowdy" and "Wild Thing," Busch became a polarizing figure in the sport, recognized for his fierce competitive spirit and sometimes controversial behavior.

In a joint statement, the Busch family, Richard Childress Racing, and NASCAR described him as "a rare talent, one who comes along once in a generation." They noted his contributions extended beyond driving to fostering the next generation as an owner in the Truck Series, creating a devoted fan base known as "Rowdy Nation."

"Throughout a career that spanned more than two decades, Kyle set records in national series wins, won championships at NASCAR's highest level and fostered the next generation of drivers," the statement said. "NASCAR lost a giant of the sport today, far too soon."

Busch is survived by his wife, Samantha, and their children Brexton and Lennix. During the Coca-Cola 600 race held at Charlotte Motor Speedway shortly after his death, winner Daniel Suárez dedicated his victory to Busch and his family, saying, "This one is for Kyle."

8:30 AM ET

Sonny Rollins, widely recognized as one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at 95. His death was announced Monday on his official website "with deep sorrow and profound love," and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He passed away at his home in Woodstock, New York.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he grew up in Harlem and earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. Inspired by his siblings who played piano and violin, as well as jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller, Rollins began playing saxophone at age seven. His early musical education was remarkable, with his high school band featuring future stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew, and Art Taylor.

Rollins became known as a genius of melody and improvisation. His bright, catchy musical lines, whether drawn from jazz standards or his own compositions, would be extended and refashioned throughout his epic solos. Miles Davis described how Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" as an innovative player with consistently fresh ideas.

His career faced a serious setback when heroin addiction led him to commit armed robbery in 1950. He spent 10 months in jail on Rikers Island but successfully overcame his addiction through rehabilitation in 1955. This recovery sparked an extraordinary creative period. His 1956 album "Saxophone Colossus" featured his signature tune "St Thomas," named after his mother's Caribbean birthplace. He followed with "Way Out West" in 1957 and "Freedom Suite" in 1958, establishing himself as a major jazz voice.

Rollins became legendary for his sabbaticals from performing and recording. Between 1959 and 1962, he took a three-year break, famously practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway to avoid disturbing neighbors. He returned with "The Bridge" album in 1962. Another sabbatical from 1969 to 1971 saw him travel to an Indian ashram to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation.

Throughout his career, Rollins collaborated with jazz giants including Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane, Dizzy Gillespie, and Max Roach. He released more than 60 albums and continuously evolved his sound, incorporating Latin American music, funk, and calypso elements. In his later years, he performed at larger venues rather than nightclubs and became an environmental advocate, releasing the 1998 album "Global Warming."

Rollins married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004. He remained active as a performer and recording artist well into his later years, representing one of the last living connections to the bebop generation and a figure who fundamentally transformed what the saxophone could achieve.

According to reporting by Politico, the pause affects removal operations targeting people being deporte...

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily halted deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo as an Ebola outbreak spreads across the country. The decision came after concerns that the flights could expose Immigration and Customs Enforcement personnel to the virus.

According to reporting by Politico, the pause affects removal operations targeting people being deported to the DRC. However, the administration has indicated it will not return detainees who have already been sent to third countries in the disease-stricken region.

The pause has created complications for at least one woman who was moved to Kinshasa, the capital of the DRC, by officials. Despite a judge's order for her return, administrators say they will not bring her back because of the Ebola travel ban.

Public health experts have questioned whether the temporary pause will actually help prevent disease spread. They argue that halting flights alone does not address the fundamental risks associated with deportations during an active outbreak.

The decision comes amid broader scrutiny of the Trump administration's deportation operations. A group of 40 House Democrats recently wrote to the Federal Aviation Administration expressing serious concerns about what they describe as a secretive and expanding program of deportation flights.

In their letter, the lawmakers cited credible reports indicating that individuals have been placed on flights without notice to their attorneys or family members. The letter states that some flights have been "inappropriately shielded from tracking systems," effectively removing people from public view while families search for their loved ones and attorneys are prevented from meaningful intervention.

According to monitoring by human rights groups, the Trump administration transported detained immigrants on 84 percent more ICE flights during 2025 compared to 2024. The lawmakers noted that information about these flights remains difficult to access, which undermines congressional oversight and prevents public understanding of the operations.

The administration has previously rejected claims that flights are hidden or weaponized, describing such allegations as "categorically false." Officials have argued that detention centers maintain "higher standards than most US prisons."

New Jersey congressman Rob Menendez, who led the letter to the FAA, emphasized the importance of transparency. "This transparency is important for the American people to understand what is happening every single day because there are so many violations of due process and legal rights happening that if people knew about them they would find it deeply problematic," he said.

The FAA letter asks for detailed reports on all ICE air operations since Trump took office, including flight origins and destinations along with passenger numbers. It also requests information about how the agency assesses humanitarian conditions aboard ICE flights, particularly regarding the controversial use of full body restraints during deportations.

Sonny Rollins, widely recognized as one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at 95. His death was announced Monday on his official website "with deep sorrow and profound love," and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He passed away at his home in Woodstock, New York.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he grew up in Harlem and earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. Inspired by his siblings who played piano and violin, as well as jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller, Rollins began playing saxophone at age seven. His early musical education was remarkable, with his high school band featuring future stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew, and Art Taylor.

Rollins became known as a genius of melody and improvisation. His bright, catchy musical lines, whether drawn from jazz standards or his own compositions, would be extended and refashioned throughout his epic solos. Miles Davis described how Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" as an innovative player with consistently fresh ideas.

His career faced a serious setback when heroin addiction led him to commit armed robbery in 1950. He spent 10 months in jail on Rikers Island but successfully overcame his addiction through rehabilitation in 1955. This recovery sparked an extraordinary creative period. His 1956 album "Saxophone Colossus" featured his signature tune "St Thomas," named after his mother's Caribbean birthplace. He followed with "Way Out West" in 1957 and "Freedom Suite" in 1958, establishing himself as a major jazz voice.

Rollins became legendary for his sabbaticals from performing and recording. Between 1959 and 1962, he took a three-year break, famously practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway to avoid disturbing neighbors. He returned with "The Bridge" album in 1962. Another sabbatical from 1969 to 1971 saw him travel to an Indian ashram to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation.

Throughout his career, Rollins collaborated with jazz giants including Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane, Dizzy Gillespie, and Max Roach. He released more than 60 albums and continuously evolved his sound, incorporating Latin American music, funk, and calypso elements. In his later years, he performed at larger venues rather than nightclubs and became an environmental advocate, releasing the 1998 album "Global Warming."

Rollins married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004. He remained active as a performer and recording artist well into his later years, representing one of the last living connections to the bebop generation and a figure who fundamentally transformed what the saxophone could achieve.
