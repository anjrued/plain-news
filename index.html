An explosion on a train in Pakistan's Balochistan province has killed at least 24 people, according to officials. The blast struck the train as it traveled through the region, which has long struggled with separatist violence and armed insurgency.

The Balochistan Liberation Army (BLA), a proscribed separatist group, has claimed responsibility for the attack. This incident represents part of a broader wave of coordinated violence that swept across the province, with armed insurgents launching simultaneous assaults on military installations, police stations, banks and other infrastructure in more than 10 cities.

Balochistan, Pakistan's largest province by area, has experienced a decades-old separatist insurgency that has intensified significantly in recent years. The region borders both Afghanistan and Iran. Separatist groups have previously targeted trains, buses and other transportation infrastructure while pressing demands for greater autonomy or independence. These groups cite grievances over resource distribution and development despite the province's significant natural gas reserves.

The broader coordinated attacks that included this train bombing resulted in at least 31 civilian deaths and 17 security personnel killed, according to initial reports. Chief Minister Sarfraz Bugti stated that 145 people described as militants had been killed in the 40 hours following the assaults.

The attacks unfolded across multiple locations. Insurgents attacked military installations and police stations, blocked main roads, and destroyed railway tracks. In the provincial capital Quetta, suicide bombers struck in the heart of the city. A vehicle rammed into the city's red zone, where government buildings including the governor and chief minister's offices are located, before detonating and killing police personnel including a deputy superintendent. In Gwadar, where China has constructed a deep-sea port, insurgents attempted to breach perimeter security but were stopped. In Mastung, insurgents attacked a prison and freed nearly 30 prisoners.

Pakistan's interior minister, Mohsin Naqvi, traveled to Quetta to offer condolences. He blamed India for backing the attackers, a claim Delhi denied.

Security analysts described the simultaneous attacks across multiple cities as unprecedented. Zahid Hussain, a security analyst and author, noted that the assaults demonstrated the insurgents had substantial local support and access to safe havens, likely in Afghanistan. Pakistan has repeatedly accused Afghanistan of providing sanctuaries to Baloch insurgents and other militant groups, though Kabul has denied these claims.

Pakistani authorities have intensified security operations in response. Mobile internet services have been suspended in several cities including Quetta, Gwadar and Noshki as investigators work to determine the exact cause of the blast and identify those responsible. The government has deployed military and paramilitary forces to counter the insurgency, though attacks continue to occur periodically throughout the province.

1:35 PM ET

Rising interest rates on government debt are putting pressure on federal finances as the Treasury Department manages increased borrowing costs. Yields on US government bonds have climbed as investors demand higher returns for holding the securities, making it more expensive for Washington to finance its operations.

The bond market reaction reflects investor concerns about fiscal sustainability and inflation expectations. When bond prices fall, yields move higher in the opposite direction. The 10-year Treasury yield, which serves as a benchmark for mortgages and corporate borrowing rates across the economy, has become a focal point for policymakers and market participants alike.

The timing presents challenges for government finances. Washington must continue issuing debt to fund ongoing operations and refinance maturing bonds. Each percentage point increase in average borrowing costs translates to billions of dollars in additional annual interest payments when spread across trillions in outstanding securities. The cumulative effect over time could significantly strain federal budgets.

According to Professor Costas Milas of the University of Liverpool, inflation remains a primary driver of higher yields. "There is no doubt that most of the latest rise in yields is due to higher, and sticky, inflation compared to other countries," Milas explained. However, he noted that political uncertainty compounds borrowing pressures by raising questions about effective governance and adding to the cost of capital.

The Federal Reserve's quantitative tightening policies, which involve selling government bonds, also contribute to upward pressure on rates. Milas cautioned against calls for the central bank to halt these efforts, noting that abandoning the policy would risk adding further inflation pressures at a time when reducing inflation remains a priority.

The Treasury yield curve, which maps interest rates across different bond maturities, provides insight into investor expectations about future economic growth and inflation. Market participants monitor these signals carefully alongside indications from policymakers about fiscal and monetary policy directions.

Higher government borrowing costs ripple through financial markets broadly. Increased rates for government debt typically flow through to consumer loans, mortgage products, and business investment decisions. The current environment tests how much upward pressure on rates officials are willing to tolerate before adjusting policy approaches.

Treasury officials face difficult choices about balancing fiscal objectives with the reality of mounting debt service obligations. The department continues conducting regular bond auctions to meet financing requirements, but must do so amid heightened market scrutiny about the government's long-term fiscal position.

The selloff highlights a fundamental challenge facing policymakers: sustaining adequate investor demand for government securities while managing the costs associated with elevated borrowing rates. How Washington navigates this situation could have far-reaching consequences for both government finances and broader economic conditions.

Russian filmmaker Andrey Zvyagintsev made his return to the international film circuit at the Cannes Film Festival this week, premiering his first film in nine years after a devastating health crisis forced him away from filmmaking.

Zvyagintsev learned of Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 while stranded in a clinic in Hanover, Germany, battling severe COVID-19 complications. The infection left him with 90 percent lung damage, unable to move or feel his limbs for several months. He spent 11 months recovering in various hospitals before eventually relearning to walk and hold a spoon.

"It was in this state that I learned of the outbreak of the war in Ukraine," he said in a recent interview. "It was a shock; I felt immense pain and deep despair."

Despite the physical and emotional toll, Zvyagintsev channeled his anguish back into filmmaking. His new film, titled Minotaur, premieres at Cannes on Tuesday. It is an adaptation of Claude Chabrol's 1969 erotic thriller The Unfaithful Wife, relocated to a medium-sized Russian provincial town. The film follows a business executive on the verge of laying off employees who discovers his wife is having an affair.

Zvyagintsev has chosen France as his place of exile and is competing for the festival's top Palme d'Or award against acclaimed directors including Pedro Almodóvar, László Nemes, and Asghar Farhadi. The 62-year-old filmmaker previously won best screenplay and jury prizes at Cannes but has never won the top award.

"Many artistic careers have been broken by the political turn that Russia has taken," said Julian Graffy, a professor of Russian literature and film at University College London. "But since Zvyagintsev was the most important of the new wave of directors that flowered in the early 21st century, the loss of his voice has been felt most keenly of them all."

Zvyagintsev first gained international recognition with his 2003 debut feature The Return, which won the Golden Lion at Venice. His subsequent films including Leviathan and Loveless were widely interpreted as searing critiques of Russian society under Vladimir Putin, examining themes of state oppression, corruption, and societal apathy.

In 2014, Russian authorities took notice of Leviathan's political dimensions. Though the Russian ministry of culture had funded 35 percent of the film's budget, then-minister Vladimir Medinsky later said he disliked the film and accused its director of being solely concerned with confronting power.

Zvyagintsev's return to Cannes marks a significant moment for Russian cinema, as the filmmaker represents one of the country's most important contemporary voices during a period when many Russian artists have faced difficult choices about their positions on the war.

Polis reduced Peters' sentence from eight and a half years to approximately four and a half years. She will be released on parole on June 1. In his clemency letter, the governor wrote: "This is an extremely unusual and lengthy sentence for a first time offender who committed non-violent crimes."

Peters was convicted of four felonies and three misdemeanors in 2024 for her role in the 2021 security breach. She allowed Conan Hayes, a former professional surfer affiliated with MyPillow CEO Mike Lindell, to access Mesa County's voting equipment, copy it, and attend a sensitive software upgrade. Sensitive passwords and other information from the county's voting systems were later published online by right-wing figures.

The clemency decision immediately triggered backlash from fellow Democrats. Jena Griswold, Colorado's secretary of state, said the decision "validates Trump's basest impulses and emboldens this lawless president." She added that the clemency "will embolden the election denial movement across the country and will leave a dark, dangerous imprint on American democracy for years to come."

Matt Crane, executive director of the Colorado Clerks Association, stated the group was "furious, disgusted, and deeply disappointed by the governor's decision." He warned that election officials across the country were interpreting the clemency as a signal that "it's open season on our elections and election officials."

Griswold learned about Polis' decision only 45 minutes before it became public. When sentencing Peters in 2024, Judge Matthew Barrett said: "You are no hero. You're a charlatan who used, and is still using, your prior position in office to peddle a snake oil that's been proven to be junk time and time again." An appeals court later overturned that sentence in April and ordered a judge to reconsider it.

In March, Polis had signaled his leanness toward clemency by comparing Peters' case to that of former state senator Sonya Jaquez Lewis, who was convicted of submitting forged letters during a legislative inquiry. Polis noted the sentencing disparity, as Lewis received probation and community service.

However, Griswold disputed the comparison, stating that beyond one overlapping felony count, the cases were fundamentally different. She emphasized that Peters "organized the breach of the election equipment, broke the public trust and attacked the very foundations of our democratic process."

The case has become a focal point for those falsely claiming the 2020 election was stolen. Former President Donald Trump has repeatedly called for Peters' release and issued her a federal pardon, though that was largely symbolic since her crimes are state-level offenses.
