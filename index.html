7:55 AM ET

The United States military conducted strikes on Iranian targets in southern Iran, including missile launch sites and boats attempting to lay mines, while diplomats from both countries engaged in peace talks in Qatar.

The US Central Command characterized the operation as defensive action taken to protect American forces. Navy Captain Tim Hawkins said Centcom "continues to defend our forces while using restraint during the ongoing ceasefire," emphasizing that the strikes did not signal an end to the seven-week ceasefire between the two nations.

Iranian news outlets reported casualties from the strikes. Tabnak, a website believed to be close to former Revolutionary Guard chief Mohsen Rezaei, identified four dead Guard troops killed in American strikes on boats. Iranian state television reported explosions around Bandar Abbas, a port city on the Strait of Hormuz, though Iranian state news agency Mehr later said the situation was "completely under control."

The timing of the strikes coincided with a delegation of Iranian negotiators traveling to Qatar for talks. Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, speaker of Iran's parliament, led the group, which also included Iran's foreign minister Abbas Araghchi and central bank governor Abdolnaser Hemmati. Hemmati's participation suggests discussions may focus on releasing frozen Iranian assets held in banks outside Iran, including in Qatar.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio said a peace deal remained possible despite the latest strikes. "There were some talks going on in Qatar today, so we'll see if we can make progress," Rubio told reporters while visiting India. He reiterated that the Strait of Hormuz would open "one way or otherwise."

A draft memorandum under negotiation would reportedly see Iran restore commercial shipping through the strait but would not address nuclear issues. Those negotiations would occur within 30 to 60 days after any initial agreement.

President Trump faces pressure from Republican loyalists over reports that billions in frozen Iranian assets could be released as part of a deal. Trump has said his primary objective is preventing Iran from developing nuclear weapons using its highly enriched uranium. He recently announced on social media that enriched uranium held in Iran could be "destroyed" inside the country under international nuclear agency oversight.

In a post on Truth Social, Trump said negotiations with Iran were progressing "nicely" but warned of fresh attacks if talks failed. "It will only be a Great Deal for all, or no Deal at all," he wrote.

Iran has insisted that a ceasefire in Lebanon be included in any memorandum of understanding. Meanwhile, Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has vowed to intensify strikes against Hezbollah, the Iran-backed militia in Lebanon.

The strikes represent the second major military action during the seven-week ceasefire, highlighting the parallel tracks of military confrontation and diplomatic engagement characterizing current US-Iran relations.

Tulsi Gabbard has resigned as US Director of National Intelligence, effective June 30. In her resignation letter to President Trump, she cited her husband's recent diagnosis with a rare form of bone cancer as the reason for stepping down.

"At this time, I must step away from public service to be by his side and fully support him through this battle," she wrote.

However, Reuters reported that the White House forced Gabbard to resign, citing a source familiar with the matter. A spokesperson for Gabbard's office denied these claims, saying allegations that she was forcibly ousted were "100% false."

Trump announced her departure on his Truth Social platform, saying Gabbard "has done an incredible job, and we will miss her." Aaron Lukas, the principal deputy director of national intelligence, will serve as acting director. Trump had been discussing with cabinet members last month whether he should replace Gabbard, according to two people briefed on those conversations.

Gabbard's tenure proved contentious from the start. As a former Democratic congresswoman from Hawaii who ran for president in 2020 before switching allegiance to Trump, she brought an unconventional background to the role. She lacked traditional intelligence experience, and her foreign policy positions sometimes diverged from the president's views, particularly regarding overseas military intervention.

During her time as director, Gabbard was largely sidelined from major foreign policy decisions. Trump excluded her from key national security conversations about Iran and Venezuela, according to people familiar with her office. She had sought to ingratiate herself with the president by vowing to root out what she called politicization in intelligence agencies and by supporting his election denial agenda.

Gabbard's appointment had drawn considerable scrutiny even before she took office. Critics questioned her qualifications and pointed to her past meetings with Syrian President Bashar al-Assad. Her record of controversial statements, including accusations that former President Barack Obama and senior national security officials had engaged in a "treasonous conspiracy" regarding Russian interference in the 2016 election, raised additional concerns about her suitability for the role.

In January, Gabbard appeared at the scene of an FBI raid in Georgia where officials were seeking ballots from the 2020 election, even though her position focused primarily on foreign intelligence matters.

The position of Director of National Intelligence was established after the September 11 attacks to improve coordination among US intelligence agencies. Gabbard's departure leaves the intelligence community without permanent leadership during a period of heightened international tensions, including escalating conflicts involving Iran and Venezuela.

US stock futures climbed higher as investors responded to reports that the United States has presented a 15-point peace framework to Iran, raising expectations for a possible ceasefire in the Middle East.

The positive market reaction was driven by multiple encouraging signals from negotiations. Iran announced it would allow "non-hostile" ships to pass safely through the Strait of Hormuz, a critical waterway that had been largely closed since the outbreak of conflict. This move could help restore one of the world's most vital shipping lanes.

Oil prices fell sharply on the news. Brent crude dropped 4 percent in early trading, sinking below $100 a barrel as traders anticipated relief from the severe supply constraints that have gripped global energy markets. The decline reflected investor optimism that a deal could ease the squeeze on oil supplies.

Global stock markets moved higher across the board. Japan's Nikkei closed up 2.9 percent, while Hong Kong's Hang Seng gained just over 1 percent. European markets also rose, with London's FTSE 100 up 1.4 percent, Germany's Dax rising 1.3 percent, and France's Cac 40 climbing about 1.3 percent. In the US, the Nasdaq gained 0.7 percent while the S&P 500 and Dow Jones both rose approximately 0.6 percent.

However, the gains came despite continued military tension. The US conducted fresh military strikes in the region, and Iran later denied that active peace talks had taken place, creating mixed signals about the status of negotiations.

The disruption to the Strait of Hormuz has created unprecedented pressure on global energy and food supplies. Before the conflict, an average of 138 vessels transited the narrow channel daily. On one recent day, only four vessels were recorded passing through, representing less than 3 percent of normal traffic levels. The waterway typically carries 20 percent of global oil and gas supplies.

The economic impact extends beyond energy. One-third of the world's fertilizers also pass through the strait. A World Trade Organization official warned that disruption to fertilizer supplies is threatening global food security by reducing harvests and raising agricultural prices.

Amelie Derambure, senior multi-asset manager at Amundi, noted the shift in investor sentiment: "The mood is on the positive side. The market is trading now the idea that peace talks or a ceasefire could be on the way."

More than 30 countries, including the United Arab Emirates, Britain, France, Germany, Canada, and Australia, have agreed to work on safeguarding the Hormuz channel. An agreement to reopen shipping lanes could provide significant relief to strained global supply chains and help stabilize energy costs that have fueled inflation concerns.

8:13 AM ET

NASA has added missions to its commercial crew contract with SpaceX because Boeing has not been able to certify its spacecraft for regular astronaut flights. The space agency announced the contract modification on Tuesday.

The decision means SpaceX will fly more astronauts to the International Space Station than originally planned under the commercial crew program. NASA launched the program to create multiple private options for transporting astronauts after the space shuttle program ended.

Boeing has faced setbacks in getting its Starliner spacecraft certified for operational crew missions. The company completed test flights but has not received approval from NASA to begin regular service carrying astronauts to the space station.

SpaceX has been flying operational crew missions since 2020 using its Crew Dragon spacecraft. The company has successfully completed multiple rotations of astronauts to and from the International Space Station.

The commercial crew program was designed to have two competing providers to ensure continued American access to space. NASA awarded contracts to both SpaceX and Boeing in 2014 to develop crew transportation systems.

Boeing received a larger initial contract award than SpaceX but has taken longer to achieve operational status. The company conducted its first crewed test flight in 2022 but has not progressed to regular missions.

The additional missions to SpaceX represent a shift from the original plan to split crew rotations more evenly between the two companies. NASA has not announced whether it will reduce future missions for Boeing or simply increase the total number of contracted flights.

SpaceX continues to be the only American company currently certified to fly astronauts. The company uses its Falcon 9 rocket to launch the Crew Dragon capsule, which can carry up to seven passengers.

NASA relies on commercial crew providers to maintain a continuous American presence aboard the International Space Station. The station requires regular crew rotations every six months to keep research operations running.

Kyle Busch, a two-time NASCAR Cup Series champion, has died at age 41 following a severe illness that required hospitalization. NASCAR confirmed his death in a statement Thursday, calling him "one of our sport's greatest and fiercest drivers."

The organization extended condolences to the Busch family, Richard Childress Racing, and the motorsports community. No specific cause of death was initially disclosed by NASCAR, though the family later stated that severe pneumonia and sepsis were responsible.

Busch's health decline appeared to begin during a NASCAR Cup Series race on May 10 at Watkins Glen in New York. During the event, he radioed his crew requesting medical assistance from Dr. Bill Heisel after experiencing symptoms. According to the broadcast, Busch had been battling a sinus cold that was intensified by the physical demands of the road course, which featured significant G-forces and elevation changes. Despite his condition, Busch finished the race in eighth place.

Just six days before his death, Busch won a trucks race at Dover on Friday. His family announced his hospitalization Thursday, saying he would not compete in the scheduled NASCAR race in Charlotte, North Carolina.

The Las Vegas native won back-to-back championships in 2015 and 2019, competing most recently in his fourth season with Richard Childress Racing after winning titles with Joe Gibbs Racing. At the time of his death, Busch ranked 24th in Cup Series standings with two top-10 finishes in 12 races this season.

His career achievements were substantial. Busch won 232 races across NASCAR's three national series, more than any driver in history. His tenure in the sport spanned more than two decades, beginning with a Rookie of the Year award in 2005.

A joint statement from the Busch family, Richard Childress Racing, and NASCAR described him as "a rare talent, one who comes along once in a generation." The statement noted his fierce competitive spirit, passion, and deep care for the sport and its fans. It highlighted his influence in developing the next generation of drivers through his ownership in the Truck Series.

Known by his nicknames "Rowdy" and "Wild Thing," Busch became a polarizing figure in racing, famous for his aggressive driving style, post-race confrontations, and emotional intensity. These characteristics created what supporters called "Rowdy Nation," a devoted fanbase spanning multiple age groups.

Busch is survived by his wife, Samantha, and their children Brexton and Lennix. The NASCAR community is expected to deliver a full tribute to his legacy at upcoming events.

4:55 AM ET

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, died at age 95. His death was announced on his website Monday with the statement "with deep sorrow and profound love," and his publicist Terri Hinte confirmed the news. Rollins died at his home in Woodstock, New York, on Monday afternoon. No cause of death was given.

Rollins was among the last surviving stars of the bebop generation, a musician who helped transform jazz from a predominantly dance and ballad form into something startlingly expressive and innovative. Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother and grew up in Harlem, where he began learning saxophone at age seven.

Over a career spanning more than seven decades, Rollins released more than 60 albums starting in the late 1940s, collaborating with Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane and numerous other jazz legends. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis has called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting Rollins with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, Barack Obama said the saxophonist had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Rollins was a genius of melody and improvisation, known for taking jazz standards and self-penned compositions and reshaping them into extended, innovative solos. Miles Davis himself wrote about how Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" and praised him as "an aggressive, innovative player who always had fresh musical ideas."

His most celebrated recordings include Saxophone Colossus (1956), featuring his signature tune St Thomas, which nodded to calypso and was named after his mother's Caribbean birthplace. Way Out West (1957) explored a piano-free "strolling" style, while Freedom Suite (1958) featured an emancipated 20-minute title track that became an argument for freedom during the civil rights movement.

Rollins took a three-year break from recording and performing in 1959, practicing for up to 15 hours a day on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway, partly to avoid disturbing neighbors. This discipline inspired his 1962 comeback album The Bridge. He took another sabbatical between 1969 and 1971 to study yoga, philosophy and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout his life, Rollins demonstrated remarkable versatility, incorporating Latin American music, R&B, funk and calypso into his work. He composed and performed the soundtrack to the 1966 Michael Caine film Alfie and even added uncredited soloing to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album Tattoo You. Later in life, he campaigned on climate issues through benefit concerts and his 1998 album Global Warming.

Rollins married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004.

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily halted deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo in response to a widening Ebola outbreak, according to reporting by Politico. However, experts say the move will not prevent the disease from spreading.

The pause creates an immediate problem for people already sent to the region. At least one woman, Adriana Zapata, 55, was moved to Kinshasa, the capital of Congo, over a month ago despite the country's inability to provide adequate medical care for her complex health needs. A U.S. judge ordered her return to America, but officials are now refusing to bring her back, citing the new travel ban.

"I'm just really worried about losing her," said Lauren O'Neal, Zapata's lawyer. "I don't want her to die before we can get her back here."

Officials told Politico that immigration agents could contact the virus during removal operations, and the disease could spread closer to the United States through deportation activities. Some of the motivation for the pause also relates to legal concerns that deporting people to a country experiencing an active Ebola outbreak could be used as a defense in immigration cases.

"By the government's own logic, if it is not safe for people to come from there to here, it is equally unsafe to send people there," said Jeremy Konyndyk, president of Refugees International and former top Ebola response official at the U.S. Agency for International Development.

At least 37 people have been moved to countries affected by or near the outbreak in recent months, according to journalist Gillian Brockell, who tracks third-country removals. Some experts suspect officials are using the Ebola ban as a convenient excuse to avoid returning detainees, since the government has previously evacuated people from Ebola regions, including patients with active cases.

"The Trump administration could absolutely return Adriana Zapata to the U.S.; telling the judge it can't be done just isn't true," Brockell said.

A Department of Homeland Security spokesperson said ICE "follows all applicable health and safety guidelines" when conducting removals. However, the agency did not respond to questions about whether Zapata will be returned or whether deportation flights to Uganda, South Sudan, and Rwanda would continue.

Sending immigrants to other countries against their will during a disease outbreak raises international law concerns, according to immigration lawyer Camille Mackler. If the outbreak expands and detainees become infected, they could potentially carry the virus to their countries of origin.

The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has implemented entry screening for all passengers from affected regions at Washington-Dulles International Airport. According to CDC officials, these measures make it very unlikely that travelers will bring Ebola into the United States.

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, died at age 95. His death was announced on his website Monday with the statement "with deep sorrow and profound love," and his publicist Terri Hinte confirmed the news. Rollins died at his home in Woodstock, New York, on Monday afternoon. No cause of death was given.

Rollins was among the last surviving stars of the bebop generation, a musician who helped transform jazz from a predominantly dance and ballad form into something startlingly expressive and innovative. Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother and grew up in Harlem, where he began learning saxophone at age seven.

Over a career spanning more than seven decades, Rollins released more than 60 albums starting in the late 1940s, collaborating with Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane and numerous other jazz legends. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis has called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting Rollins with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, Barack Obama said the saxophonist had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Rollins was a genius of melody and improvisation, known for taking jazz standards and self-penned compositions and reshaping them into extended, innovative solos. Miles Davis himself wrote about how Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" and praised him as "an aggressive, innovative player who always had fresh musical ideas."

His most celebrated recordings include Saxophone Colossus (1956), featuring his signature tune St Thomas, which nodded to calypso and was named after his mother's Caribbean birthplace. Way Out West (1957) explored a piano-free "strolling" style, while Freedom Suite (1958) featured an emancipated 20-minute title track that became an argument for freedom during the civil rights movement.

Rollins took a three-year break from recording and performing in 1959, practicing for up to 15 hours a day on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway, partly to avoid disturbing neighbors. This discipline inspired his 1962 comeback album The Bridge. He took another sabbatical between 1969 and 1971 to study yoga, philosophy and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout his life, Rollins demonstrated remarkable versatility, incorporating Latin American music, R&B, funk and calypso into his work. He composed and performed the soundtrack to the 1966 Michael Caine film Alfie and even added uncredited soloing to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album Tattoo You. Later in life, he campaigned on climate issues through benefit concerts and his 1998 album Global Warming.

Rollins married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004.
