Mining accidents in China have historically been attributed to various factors including inadequate safety measures, poor ventilation systems, and the presence of explosive gases underground. The coal mining sector employs millions of workers across the country.

State media reported the casualty figures, though additional details about the circumstances of the explosion have been provided through official channels. The scale of the disaster represents a significant loss of life in a single industrial accident.

China has previously implemented safety campaigns and regulations aimed at reducing mining deaths after particularly deadly years in the past. The government has periodically shut down smaller, less safe operations and imposed stricter oversight on the industry.

The coal mining sector remains essential to China's economy despite efforts to transition toward cleaner energy sources. The country continues to be both the world's largest producer and consumer of coal, with the industry supporting significant portions of the national energy grid and industrial production.

11:51 AM ET

Two-time NASCAR Cup Series champion Kyle Busch has died at 41 after being hospitalized with a severe illness, NASCAR announced in a Thursday statement. The racing organization confirmed his passing on social media, stating they were "saddened and heartbroken to share the news of the passing of Kyle Busch, a two-time Cup champion and one of our sport's greatest and fiercest drivers."

According to a statement released by the family on Saturday, Busch died after severe pneumonia progressed into sepsis, resulting in rapid and overwhelming associated complications. Dakota Hunter, vice-president of Kyle Busch Companies, said in a news release that the family received the medical evaluation on Saturday.

The family had announced Busch's hospitalization earlier on Thursday, saying he wouldn't compete as planned at the weekend's NASCAR race in Charlotte, North Carolina. During a NASCAR Cup Series race on May 10 at Watkins Glen, Busch had radioed to his crew requesting medical aid from Dr. Bill Heisel and a "shot" after the race. According to the TV broadcast, Busch had been struggling with a sinus cold that was exacerbated by the intense G-forces and elevation changes at the upstate New York road course. He continued to race and finished eighth.

The Las Vegas native won championships in 2015 and 2019. He was in his fourth season at Richard Childress Racing after winning titles with Joe Gibbs Racing. At the time of his death, Busch ranked 24th in the Cup Series standings, with two top-10 finishes in 12 races this season.

Busch won 232 races across NASCAR's three national series, more than any driver in history. He won the trucks race at Dover on Friday, just six days before his death.

In a joint statement, the Busch family, Richard Childress Racing and NASCAR said: "Our entire NASCAR family is heartbroken by the loss of Kyle Busch. A future Hall of Famer, Kyle was a rare talent, one who comes along once in a generation. He was fierce, he was passionate, he was immensely skilled and he cared deeply about the sport and fans."

The statement continued: "Throughout a career that spanned more than two decades, Kyle set records in national series wins, won championships at NASCAR's highest level and fostered the next generation of drivers as an owner in the Truck Series. His sharp wit and competitive spirit sparked a deep emotional connection with race fans of every age, creating the proud and loyal 'Rowdy Nation.' NASCAR lost a giant of the sport today, far too soon."

A polarizing figure known as "Rowdy" and "Wild Thing" for his post-race fights, regular feuds with other drivers and sometimes outlandish behavior, Busch stormed onto the Cup Series scene in 2005 by winning rookie of the year.

Busch is survived by his wife, Samantha, and children Brexton and Lennix.

NASA is considering ending Caltech's management of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory for the first time in the institution's history, according to reports. The move would mark a significant shift in the governance of one of America's premier space research facilities.

JPL has been managed by the California Institute of Technology since its founding. The laboratory has played a central role in numerous NASA missions, including the Mars rover programs and deep space exploration initiatives.

The potential change comes as NASA evaluates its relationships with federally funded research and development centers. JPL operates as a NASA facility but has been administered by Caltech under a contract arrangement that has existed for decades.

NASA has not publicly announced a final decision on the management structure. The space agency is reportedly reviewing multiple options for how JPL should be operated going forward.

Caltech has overseen JPL's operations since the laboratory's early days, providing academic leadership and research connections. The arrangement has been considered a model for how universities can manage government research facilities.

JPL employs thousands of scientists and engineers working on planetary science, Earth observation, and space technology development. The laboratory is located in Pasadena, California, adjacent to Caltech's main campus.

Any change in management would require NASA to establish a new administrative framework for the laboratory. This could involve direct NASA management or a contract with a different institution.

The timing of NASA's review has not been tied to any specific incident or performance issue at JPL. Federal agencies periodically reassess their management arrangements for major research facilities.

Caltech has expressed interest in continuing its management role at JPL. University officials have noted the long partnership between the two institutions and the research benefits that flow from the relationship.

NASA has not provided a timeline for when a decision might be announced. The agency is conducting its review while JPL continues its current mission work without interruption.

The laboratory's future missions include continued Mars exploration and participation in planned missions to Jupiter's moons. These programs would continue regardless of any management changes.

Acting NASA chief Sean Duffy, who also leads the US transportation department, congratulated Isaacman on Twitter/X. Duffy wished him "success as he begins his tenure and leads NASA as we go back to the Moon in 2028 and beat China."

Duffy had been lobbying to move the space agency under the permanent purview of his own bureau, a fight he lost with Isaacman's confirmation. His consolidation efforts sparked conflict with Musk around the same time that the transport secretary said SpaceX had fallen behind on its contracts with NASA. Duffy had looked to the private company's competitors, particularly Jeff Bezos's Blue Origin, for alternatives.

Isaacman's path to confirmation was unconventional. Trump initially nominated him for the NASA administrator post, then removed him in May during a public feud with Musk. Trump posted on his Truth Social platform that Isaacman's donations to Democratic candidates were the reasons behind the withdrawal.

Isaacman re-emerged as a candidate in recent months. According to the Wall Street Journal, he tapped allies within the administration to promote his cause, ultimately securing the nomination for a second time.

The new administrator made his fortune by founding Shift4, a payment processing startup that now handles billions of transactions each year. His space credentials include flying on two SpaceX missions, both of which he funded himself and which featured all-civilian crews.

The most recent mission took place in 2024 and involved the first spacewalk conducted by a private company. This hands-on experience in commercial spaceflight positions Isaacman uniquely among NASA administrators, though his close ties to SpaceX and Musk have drawn scrutiny.

The confirmation sets the stage for NASA's ambitious timeline to return astronauts to the moon by 2028, a goal framed explicitly as a competition with China's lunar ambitions. Isaacman now faces the challenge of accelerating NASA's pace while navigating the complex politics surrounding commercial space partnerships and the agency's relationship with the Department of Transportation.
