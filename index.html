A coal mine explosion in China has killed at least 90 people, making it the deadliest mining disaster in the country since 2009.

The blast occurred at a coal mine, though the exact location and timing have not been specified in available reports. The death toll of 90 represents the highest number of casualties in a Chinese mining incident in over 17 years.

China has historically struggled with mine safety, particularly in its coal industry, which provides a significant portion of the country's energy supply. The nation has worked to improve safety standards in recent years, but deadly accidents continue to occur.

The 2009 incident that previously held the record for deadliest coal mine disaster killed a similar or greater number of workers. That tragedy prompted widespread calls for reform in the industry.

Coal mining remains one of the most dangerous occupations in China, with thousands of workers employed in conditions that can involve methane gas buildup, structural instability, and inadequate ventilation. Explosions typically result from the ignition of methane or coal dust.

The scale of this incident places it among the worst industrial accidents in recent Chinese history. Mining disasters of this magnitude often lead to investigations, disciplinary actions against officials, and renewed pledges to strengthen safety protocols.

China is the world's largest producer and consumer of coal, with the industry employing millions of workers across the country. The government has periodically announced crackdowns on unsafe mining practices, particularly after major incidents.

The loss of 90 lives represents a significant human toll and raises questions about the enforcement of existing safety regulations at the affected facility. Investigations into the cause of the explosion are expected to examine whether proper safety measures were in place and followed.

A fossil site in North Dakota appears to have preserved a snapshot of the day an asteroid struck Earth and triggered the extinction of the dinosaurs. The site contains fish that died within hours of the impact, with tiny glass beads from the collision lodged in their gills.

The glass beads, known as impact spherules, formed when the asteroid vaporized rock upon impact. The molten material was thrown into the atmosphere and fell back to Earth as glassy droplets. Finding these beads inside fish gills indicates the fish were alive when the spherules rained down and died shortly after inhaling them.

The discovery provides direct physical evidence linking the moment of impact to immediate biological consequences. The asteroid struck what is now the Yucatan Peninsula in Mexico approximately 66 million years ago, creating the Chicxulub crater and setting off a chain of events that led to the mass extinction.

The North Dakota site sits thousands of miles from the impact point, yet the fossil evidence shows effects reached the location within hours. The preserved fish offer a rare window into the immediate aftermath of one of Earth's most significant extinction events.

The spherules themselves contain chemical signatures consistent with the asteroid impact. Their presence in the gills of fish that died so quickly after the event demonstrates how rapidly the impact's effects spread across the continent.

Researchers examining the site have found multiple layers of evidence from that day, including disturbed sediments and other impact debris. The fish fossils represent some of the most direct evidence yet discovered of how quickly the asteroid's effects reached organisms far from the impact zone.

The site continues to yield information about the sequence of events following the asteroid strike. Each fossil and sediment layer adds detail to the understanding of how the extinction unfolded in its first hours and days, capturing a moment that changed the course of life on Earth.

The WHO made the declaration on Sunday after the outbreak of the Bundibugyo virus prompted urgent action. Dr Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, the WHO direc...

12:19 PM ET

An Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Uganda has been declared a "public health emergency of international concern" by the World Health Organization, following 88 deaths and more than 300 suspected cases.

The WHO made the declaration on Sunday after the outbreak of the Bundibugyo virus prompted urgent action. Dr Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, the WHO director general, announced the decision before convening a formal emergency committee at the organization.

The Bundibugyo virus is one of three strains that can cause Ebola virus disease and the least common. There are no approved vaccines or treatments for the strain, or specific tests. Ebola is a highly contagious disease spread via bodily fluids such as blood and vomit.

The outbreak has hit the DRC's Ituri province in the east of the country, which borders Uganda and South Sudan. There have been 80 suspected deaths, eight laboratory-confirmed cases and 246 suspected cases in Ituri province. Two cases, including one death, have been reported in Uganda's capital, Kampala, apparently in people who had traveled from the DRC.

Dr Jean Kaseya, director general of the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention, told Sky News: "Currently, I'm on panic mode because people are dying. I don't have medicines. I don't have [a] vaccine to support countries."

The WHO said signs "all point towards a potentially much larger outbreak than what is currently being detected and reported, with significant local and regional risk of spread."

A laboratory confirmed a further Ebola case on Sunday in the city of Goma, in a separate part of the DRC also affected by conflict. "A positive case in Goma has been confirmed by tests carried out by the laboratory. It involves the wife of a man who died of Ebola in Bunia, who travelled to Goma after her husband's death whilst already infected," said Prof Jean-Jacques Muyembe, director of the Congolese National Institute for Biomedical Research.

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention said on Sunday it was supporting interagency partners coordinating the safe withdrawal of a small number of Americans directly affected by the outbreak. CDC officials said the agency had activated its emergency response center and planned to send more people to its offices in the DRC and Uganda.

Conflict is continuing in the Ituri region, the WHO said, with many people moving around and living in close quarters in urban or suburban areas, factors that can make spread more likely.

Jean Marc Asimwe, a resident of Bunia, told Associated Press: "Every day, people are dying … and this has been going on for about a week. In a single day, we bury two, three or even more people. At this point, we don't really know what kind of disease it is."
