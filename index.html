Today, 12:00 AM ET

An Ebola outbreak in central Africa has expanded beyond initial containment efforts, raising alarm among global health officials about the potential for wider spread. Health authorities are working to trace the source of the infection and prevent further transmission.

The outbreak's origins remain under investigation. Scientists are examining whether the virus jumped from animals to humans through contact with infected wildlife, a common transmission route for Ebola. The region's dense forests and wildlife populations create conditions where such spillover events can occur.

Ebola virus disease causes severe hemorrhagic fever with fatality rates that can reach 90 percent in untreated cases. The virus spreads through direct contact with bodily fluids of infected people or contaminated materials. Early symptoms include fever, fatigue, and muscle pain, progressing to vomiting, diarrhea, and in severe cases, internal and external bleeding.

Health workers are deploying rapid response teams to affected areas to isolate cases and trace contacts. Vaccination campaigns using proven Ebola vaccines are being organized for high-risk populations, including healthcare workers and close contacts of confirmed cases.

The situation is complicated by the region's limited healthcare infrastructure and ongoing security challenges that restrict access to some affected communities. Medical supplies and trained personnel are being mobilized from international organizations and neighboring countries.

Previous Ebola outbreaks in West Africa and the Democratic Republic of Congo demonstrated both the devastating potential of uncontrolled spread and the effectiveness of coordinated response efforts. The 2014-2016 West Africa epidemic killed over 11,000 people, while more recent outbreaks have been contained more quickly through improved surveillance and vaccination.

World Health Organization officials are monitoring the situation closely to determine whether it constitutes a public health emergency of international concern. That designation would trigger additional resources and coordination mechanisms.

Local communities are being educated about prevention measures, including safe burial practices and avoiding contact with potentially infected animals. Healthcare facilities are implementing strict infection control protocols to protect workers who face the highest risk of exposure.

The international response is being coordinated through established health emergency frameworks, with medical teams and equipment being positioned for rapid deployment if the outbreak continues to grow.

Tennessee called off an execution Thursday night after medical staff failed to establish an intravenous line needed to administer lethal drugs. The execution of James Barnes, 61, was abandoned after repeated unsuccessful attempts to insert the IV.

Barnes was convicted of killing Patricia Miller in 1988. Prison officials spent more than an hour trying to establish the line before Governor Bill Lee issued a temporary reprieve. The execution was scheduled for 6 PM local time but was postponed indefinitely.

This marks the latest in a series of botched execution attempts across the United States. In recent years, several states have struggled with lethal injection procedures, including difficulty finding suitable veins in older inmates or those with a history of drug use. Medical professionals have largely withdrawn from participating in executions, leaving prison staff to perform the procedures.

Tennessee uses a three-drug protocol for lethal injections. The state has faced ongoing legal challenges over its execution methods, with defense attorneys arguing the procedures can cause unnecessary suffering. The failure to establish IV access has emerged as a recurring problem in multiple states.

The execution was scheduled to proceed despite Barnes' legal team filing last-minute appeals. Those appeals were denied by courts earlier in the day. Barnes had been on death row for more than three decades.

Governor Lee's office released a brief statement confirming the reprieve but provided no additional details about what went wrong or when the execution might be rescheduled. The Tennessee Department of Correction has not commented on the specific medical issues that prevented the execution from proceeding.

Several states have temporarily suspended executions after similar failures. Alabama halted lethal injections for several months in 2022 after multiple failed attempts to establish IV lines. Other states have turned to alternative execution methods, including nitrogen gas, though those methods have also faced legal challenges.

The case highlights ongoing questions about execution protocols in states that maintain the death penalty. Anti-death penalty advocates argue these repeated failures demonstrate fundamental problems with lethal injection as an execution method. Prosecutors and victims' families have expressed frustration with the delays.

Economists note that mortgage rates typically track the yield on 10-year Treasury bonds, which have risen as investors demand higher returns amid economic volatility. The Federal Reserve has maintained its current interest rate policy, but mortgage lenders have independently adjusted their rates based on market conditions.

Industry analysts expect rates to remain elevated through the summer unless there is significant progress in resolving trade disputes or clearer economic signals emerge. Some forecasters predict rates could climb even higher if inflation pressures continue or if bond markets remain unsettled.

The rate environment particularly affects first-time homebuyers, who typically have smaller down payments and less flexibility in their budgets. Many in this group have been priced out of markets where home values have remained stubbornly high despite the rate increases.

Sea Level Rise Accelerating Since 1960, New Analysis Confirms

Researchers analyzed satellite data and coastal measurements spanning more than six decades, a...

Today, 12:00 AM ET

Sea levels have been rising at an accelerating rate since 1960, according to new research that refines decades of measurements into the clearest picture yet of ocean rise. The findings confirm what climate scientists have long suspected but can now demonstrate with greater precision.

Researchers analyzed satellite data and coastal measurements spanning more than six decades, applying improved statistical methods to account for natural variability like El Niño events and seasonal changes. The study shows that the rate of sea level rise has increased from roughly 1.5 millimeters per year in the early 1960s to about 3.7 millimeters per year today.

The acceleration is driven primarily by two factors: thermal expansion of warming ocean water and accelerated melting of ice sheets in Greenland and Antarctica. As global temperatures have risen, both processes have intensified. The research provides more accurate regional projections, showing that some coastal areas face faster rises than the global average due to ocean currents and local land subsidence.

The improved data resolution allows scientists to separate human-caused trends from natural ocean cycles with greater confidence. Previous uncertainty about whether acceleration was real or an artifact of measurement gaps has been largely eliminated. The new analysis uses advanced techniques to fill data gaps and cross-validate different measurement systems.

Coastal communities worldwide already face increased flooding during high tides and storms. The confirmation of acceleration means adaptation plans based on linear projections may underestimate future risks. Cities from Miami to Jakarta are investing billions in sea walls and drainage systems, but many projections will need updating.

The research team notes that even if greenhouse gas emissions stopped today, sea levels would continue rising for decades due to the thermal inertia of oceans and the slow response of ice sheets. However, the ultimate amount of rise depends heavily on emission paths chosen in coming years.

Scientists estimate that without significant emission reductions, seas could rise between 0.6 and 1.1 meters by 2100, with some scenarios exceeding two meters if major ice sheets destabilize. Even the lower estimates would displace hundreds of millions of people in low-lying coastal regions.

The study provides policymakers with more reliable data for long-term infrastructure planning and climate adaptation strategies. It underscores that sea level rise is not a distant future threat but an ongoing process that has already accelerated beyond historical rates.
