A few minutes ago

Tulsi Gabbard is leaving her position as US director of national intelligence after a rocky 15-month tenure marked by her exclusion from major national security decisions, according to reports from multiple news outlets.

In a letter to President Donald Trump, Gabbard announced she would resign and leave her post on June 30. "While we have made significant progress, I recognize there is still important work to be done," she wrote.

However, the White House reportedly forced Gabbard to resign, Reuters reported, citing a source familiar with the matter. Fox News first reported her departure, citing her husband's cancer diagnosis as the reason. Trump was asking cabinet members last month whether he should replace Gabbard, according to two people briefed on the discussions.

Trump acknowledged her departure on his Truth Social platform Friday. "Unfortunately, after having done a great job, Tulsi Gabbard will be leaving the Administration on June 30th," he wrote, adding that Aaron Lukas, principal deputy director of national intelligence, would serve as acting director.

Gabbard faced significant challenges during her tenure. She was largely sidelined during Trump's military operations against Venezuela and Iran. Last June, Trump publicly dismissed her Capitol Hill testimony that Iran was not building a nuclear weapon, declaring he did not care what she said and calling her assessment "wrong."

In apparent attempts to regain Trump's favor, Gabbard called for Barack Obama and several national security officials from his administration to be prosecuted, alleging they conducted a "treasonous conspiracy" to falsely depict Russia as interfering in the 2016 election. Obama denied the allegations.

This year, she appeared at an FBI raid to seize ballots from the 2020 presidential election, a matter far outside her foreign intelligence responsibilities. She was excluded from decision-making around the seizure of Venezuela's president Nicolás Maduro in January and absent from key decisions about military strikes on Iran in February.

Democratic Senator Mark Warner, vice-chair of the Senate's intelligence committee, said he hoped her successor would help ensure the office remains "grounded in facts, independence, and the rule of law."

Democratic Senator Adam Schiff was more critical, saying Gabbard's only contribution to US national security was her resignation. "She politicized intelligence. She dismantled critical agencies keeping Americans safe. She weaponized the Intelligence Community to pursue baseless election fraud claims," he wrote.

Gabbard becomes the fourth woman to depart Trump's cabinet in just over two months, following homeland security secretary Kristi Noem in March, attorney general Pam Bondi in April, and labor secretary Lori Chavez-DeRemer in April.

The former Democratic congresswoman from Hawaii served in Congress from 2013 to 2021 and ran for the Democratic presidential nomination in 2020 before leaving the party in 2022.

Eight Skiers Dead in Deadliest US Avalanche in 45 Years

Eight skiers have been confirmed dead and one remains missing after an avalanche swept through the Castle Peak area of California's Sierra Nevada mountains on Tuesday. Six other skiers who were stranded have been rescued, authorities announced at a Wednesday press conference.

The avalanche occurred about 10 miles north of Lake Tahoe at approximately 11:30 am on Tuesday, engulfing a group of backcountry skiers that included four guides and 11 clients. This marks the deadliest single avalanche in the United States since 1981, when 11 people died on Mount Rainier in Washington state.

Nevada County Sheriff Shannan Moon said the group originally had 12 people signed up for the ski tour, but "one person last minute decided to back out of that trip." The sheriff's office initially reported 16 people were involved before revising the total to 15.

About 46 emergency responders joined the search effort using a Sno-Cat vehicle that reached within 2 miles of where six survivors were sheltering in a makeshift refuge constructed partly from tarpaulin sheets. Search and rescue volunteers skied the remaining distance to reach them.

The six survivors had already recovered three bodies before rescuers arrived. First responders located five additional bodies but could not transport them due to severe weather conditions. Two of the surviving skiers were "not mobile because of the injuries they survived during the avalanche," Moon said. One was treated and released from the hospital while the other remained hospitalized with non-life-threatening injuries.

Of the six survivors, five were clients and one was a tour guide. Placer County Sheriff Wayne Woo revealed that "one of the nine missing decedents is a spouse of one of our Tahoe Nordic search and rescue team members."

The dangerous conditions resulted from rapidly accumulating snow on fragile snowpack layers combined with gale-force winds. The town of Soda Springs near the avalanche site recorded at least 30 inches of snow in a 24-hour period. The Sierra Avalanche Center had posted a "high avalanche danger" warning before dawn on Tuesday.

Russell Greene, Nevada County sheriff captain, questioned the decision to lead paying customers into the backcountry under such conditions. "I don't think it was a wise choice," Greene told the Associated Press, "but we don't know all the details yet."

The skiers were on the final day of a three-day backcountry skiing trek through rugged mountainous terrain. Chris Feutrier, forest supervisor of the Tahoe National Forest, said the avalanche was "about a football field in length." The area has since received another 3 feet of snow, keeping avalanche danger high.

Avalanches claim an average of 27 lives annually in the United States.

President Donald Trump said Friday he wants the new Federal Reserve chair to be "totally independent" as Kevin Warsh was sworn in to lead the central bank during a critical period for the American economy.

The statement marks a notable shift from Trump's earlier approach to the Fed. During his first term, Trump frequently criticized Federal Reserve policies and pressured officials to lower interest rates. His call for independence now comes as the institution faces major decisions on monetary policy amid economic uncertainty.

Warsh, a former Wall Street banker hand-picked by Trump, takes charge as the administration faces mounting pressure over Americans' financial wellbeing. The new chair assumes leadership of the powerful central bank even as Trump continues to push for interest rate cuts while prices climb.

The Federal Reserve chair serves a four-year term and traditionally operates without direct political interference. The position carries substantial influence over the American economy through control of monetary policy tools including interest rate adjustments and quantitative easing programs.

Warsh previously served on the Federal Reserve Board of Governors from 2006 to 2011, giving him experience navigating the 2008 financial crisis and its aftermath. He now takes office as the Fed balances concerns about inflation against the need to support economic growth.

Trump's emphasis on independence may provide political cover for potentially unpopular monetary policy decisions ahead. Central bank independence is widely viewed by economists as essential for maintaining credibility and preventing short-term political considerations from driving long-term economic policy.

The timing of the leadership change places Warsh at the center of current debates over monetary policy direction. The Federal Reserve's decisions in coming months will affect borrowing costs for businesses and consumers, housing markets, and overall economic activity.

The Federal Reserve operates under a dual mandate to promote maximum employment and stable prices. Warsh will lead the Federal Open Market Committee, which meets regularly to assess economic conditions and set policy accordingly.

Financial markets typically watch Federal Reserve leadership transitions closely for signals about future policy direction. Warsh's first public statements and policy decisions as chair will be scrutinized by investors, economists, and policymakers seeking clues about the central bank's approach.

The swearing-in comes as the Trump administration faces growing concern over cost of living and disapproval of its economic agenda. The new Fed chair inherits the challenge of steering monetary policy while the world's largest economy navigates continued uncertainty.

Whether Trump's stated support for Fed independence will hold if policies conflict with his preferences remains to be seen. The practical implications of his public call for independence will become clearer as Warsh begins making decisions on interest rates and other critical monetary policy matters.

The malfunction appears connected to Google's recent push to integrate artificial intelligence throughout its search platform. At its annual I/O developer conference on Tuesday, Google announced major changes to its search product, powered by its new Gemini 3.5 AI model.

Elizabeth Reid, who oversees Google Search, called the updates "the biggest Google Search had seen in its nearly 30-year history." She told conference attendees that "Google Search is AI search," signaling the company's commitment to AI-powered results.

The changes allow the search bar to handle longer, more conversational queries. Search results now feature more visual elements and suggestions to interact with Google's AI for tasks like creating calendar invites or spreadsheets. Users can still access traditional link-based results by clicking a "Web" tab.

Reid said Google Search queries reached an all-time high last month. Since launching "AI Mode" a year ago, which lets users chat with a Gemini-powered bot instead of viewing a list of links, queries to the chatbot interface have doubled every quarter.

Josh Woodward, who oversees Gemini development, described the goal as creating "a universal assistant that's personal, proactive, persistent." Google announced that 900 million people use the Gemini app monthly, though this still trails ChatGPT's 900 million weekly users.

The "disregard" search failure highlights potential problems as Google rushes to deploy AI across its core products. The word appears frequently in everyday communication, legal documents, and business contexts. Its removal from searchable results could affect users seeking dictionary definitions, news articles, or academic papers containing the term.

Google has not released a statement explaining the search restriction or whether it is intentional or temporary. The company also has not clarified if other words face similar issues.

The malfunction comes as Google faces broader challenges with its AI Overviews feature, which has previously generated inaccurate or nonsensical responses. The incident raises questions about the reliability of AI-powered search as Google makes it increasingly central to its platform, potentially affecting the billions of people who use Google Search daily.

Busch was the winningest driver across NASCAR's three series in history. He won Cup Series championships in 2015 and 2019 with Joe Gibbs Racing and was in his fourth season at Richard Childress Racing at the time of his death. He ranked 24th in the Cup Series standings with two top-10 finishes in 12 races this season. Just last week, he won a NASCAR Truck Series race at Dover International Speedway and was scheduled to compete in this weekend's Coca-Cola 600 in Charlotte.

Known by the nicknames "Rowdy" and "Wild Thing," the Las Vegas native was a polarizing figure famous for his feuds with other drivers. He won rookie of the year honors when he stormed onto the Cup Series scene in 2005. His brother Kurt was also a successful NASCAR driver.

The racing community has united in mourning. Denny Hamlin, a 22-year NASCAR veteran and Busch's former teammate, wrote on social media: "Absolutely cannot comprehend this news. We just need to think of his family during this time. We love you KB."

Dale Earnhardt Jr., once an on-track rival who later became a friend, called Busch one of the "greatest drivers in NASCAR history." He revealed that Busch had initiated a conversation years ago to repair their relationship. "Kyle and I had a really challenging existence for many years. But we luckily took the time to figure out our differences and that was something he instigated," Earnhardt said.

Even longtime rival Joey Logano, who brawled with Busch on pit road in 2017, praised his legacy. "The bottom line is he is one of the best drivers to ever sit in a race car," Logano told a Charlotte news station.

The Indianapolis Motor Speedway lit up its scoring pylon with Busch's image Thursday night. He won the Brickyard 500 at Indianapolis in both 2015 and 2016.

Fans honored Busch by donating to the Samantha and Kyle Busch Bundle of Joy Fund, a foundation the driver created with his wife to support couples seeking fertility treatments. Many donors gave $18.08, referencing the numbers 18 and 8 on cars he drove during his 26-year career.
