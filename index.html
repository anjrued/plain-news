A few minutes ago

President Trump approved a federal emergency declaration Saturday for a major sewage crisis unfolding north of Washington DC, a rupture that threatens to disrupt the nation's 250th anniversary celebrations this summer.

Millions of gallons of raw, untreated sewage have been pouring into the Potomac River following a rupture in a 72-inch pipe called the Potomac Interceptor. The break occurred last month, and local utility company DC Water estimated that 243 million gallons of wastewater had leaked from the 54-mile line before a temporary bypass was established.

"The president's action authorizes FEMA to coordinate all disaster relief efforts to alleviate the hardship and suffering caused by the emergency on the local population and to provide appropriate assistance to save lives, to protect property, public health and safety, and to lessen the threat of catastrophe," the Federal Emergency Management Agency said in a statement.

The sewage spill represents one of the largest in US history, according to reporting from the Washington Post. Environmental concerns have mounted alongside health risks. The DC Department of Energy and Environment recommends residents avoid contact with the Potomac River due to dangerous levels of E. coli bacteria.

Saturday's emergency declaration allows FEMA to mobilize cleanup and recovery efforts across Washington DC and surrounding areas in Maryland and Virginia. Washington Mayor Muriel Bowser had declared a local public emergency earlier in the week and wrote to Trump requesting he declare a major disaster.

Responsibility for the incident remains disputed among officials. Jay Apperson, a spokesperson for the Maryland Department of the Environment, said that although the broken section of pipe is located in Maryland, DC Water owns and operates the Potomac Interceptor. The pipe is regulated by the federal Environmental Protection Agency. Additionally, the break occurred within the C&O Canal National Historical Park, which is National Park Service property.

Trump has blamed local and state officials for the crisis and called it an "ecological disaster." On his Truth Social network, he wrote that Washington Mayor Bowser, Maryland Governor Wes Moore, and Virginia Governor Abigail Spanberger "must act, IMMEDIATELY."

Maryland Governor Moore, a Democrat, countered that the federal government has been responsible for the Potomac Interceptor since the last century. According to Ammar Moussa, the governor's spokesperson, "The President has his facts wrong again. Since the last century, the federal government has been responsible for the Potomac Interceptor, which is the origin of the sewage leak. For the last four weeks, the Trump Administration has failed to act, shirking its responsibility and putting people's health at risk."

Iran and the United States have made substantial progress toward a deal to end their military conflict, but significant obstacles still prevent an agreement. While negotiators have advanced on multiple fronts, Iranian officials insist that a deal is not imminent.

Esmail Baghaei, spokesperson for Iran's negotiating team, acknowledged progress but cautioned against optimism. "It is correct to say that we have reached a conclusion on a large portion of the issues under discussion. But to say that this means the signing of an agreement is imminent – no one can make such a claim," he said at a weekly foreign ministry press briefing.

The negotiations center on several complex issues. Disagreements persist over nuclear program restrictions, the timeline for sanctions relief, and access to Iranian assets frozen in foreign banks. Iran has pressed for earlier access to these frozen funds, which could amount to as much as $12 billion held in Qatar.

U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio expressed cautious optimism about reaching a deal. He emphasized that the United States preferred a diplomatic solution but remained prepared to address the situation "in another way" if talks failed.

The framework being considered would restore full access to the Strait of Hormuz within 30 days after any ceasefire. According to reports, Iran would use this period to remove mines from the strategic waterway, which has been critical to global energy markets.

Iran has set additional conditions for reopening shipping lanes. Baghaei insisted that any memorandum of understanding must include a ceasefire in Lebanon and commit to negotiations over "fees for navigational services" through the strait, which Iran describes as distinct from tolls.

On the nuclear question, disagreements remain significant. Trump has pushed for Iran to commit to disposing of its highly enriched uranium stockpile, though the method would not need to be specified initially. However, Iran has resisted transferring its uranium to the United States or Russia. Iranian officials have indicated willingness to suspend domestic enrichment for up to five years, falling short of the 20-year suspension the U.S. seeks.

Trump has linked the Iran deal to broader Middle East initiatives. He has asked countries including Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, Egypt, Jordan, and Turkey to sign the Abraham Accords to normalize relations with Israel. However, regional experts say these countries are unlikely to join the accords, and the proposal has drawn criticism from Israeli opposition figures who view the emerging Iran deal as detrimental to Israeli security.

The negotiations continue in Qatar, where Iranian negotiators have met with Qatari officials to discuss the peace framework. Both sides characterize the talks as serious and ongoing, representing the most significant diplomatic effort to resolve the conflict since fighting began.

Oil Prices Drop on Prospect of US-Iran Deal

Oil prices fell sharply on Monday as markets responded to growing optimism about a potential diplomatic agreement between the United States and Iran. Brent crude futures, the global benchmark, dropped 6 percent to $97.43 a barrel, marking the lowest level in two weeks. The decline came after nearly three months of heightened energy costs following the US-Israeli military strike...

2:54 PM ET

Oil prices fell sharply on Monday as markets responded to growing optimism about a potential diplomatic agreement between the United States and Iran. Brent crude futures, the global benchmark, dropped 6 percent to $97.43 a barrel, marking the lowest level in two weeks. The decline came after nearly three months of heightened energy costs following the US-Israeli military strikes on Iran in late February.

The price movement reflected investor expectations that an agreement could eventually restore normal oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz, a critical chokepoint that Iran had effectively closed in response to the military operations. Stock markets rose alongside the oil price decline, with Japan's Nikkei index gaining nearly 3 percent and Europe's Stoxx 600 index up 1 percent. The dollar weakened slightly while gold climbed 1.46 percent.

However, market participants are exercising caution about the deal's prospects. An Iranian government spokesperson cautioned that an agreement was "not imminent," noting that while a framework has been negotiated, the two sides remain divided over key issues including Iran's blockade of the strait. Warren Patterson, head of commodities strategy at ING, told Reuters: "We've been at this stage before, only for talks to break down. Therefore, the market will likely be more cautious about overreacting."

Even if diplomatic progress leads to a finalized agreement, analysts warn that a return to normal energy supplies will take time. Damaged energy infrastructure in Qatar and elsewhere requires repair, and analysts estimate that normalizing oil flows through the strait could take months. Giovanni Staunovo, a UBS analyst, emphasized that "physical oil flows" remain the key factor to monitor, noting that flows through the strait are currently still restricted.

Despite Monday's decline, crude prices remain significantly elevated compared to pre-war levels. Before the US and Israeli military strikes, Brent crude was trading around $70 a barrel. The recent drop to below $100 reflects hope that the Strait of Hormuz could reopen to something resembling normal operations.

The potential oil price relief comes as important economic data approaches. An inflation report scheduled for release this week could create additional market volatility, as investors assess whether declining energy prices might ease inflation pressures that have mounted due to the conflict. Stephen Innes, an independent analyst, noted that "investors started pricing the possibility that the world's most dangerous energy choke point may soon reopen to something resembling normal flow."

The higher oil prices resulting from the Iran conflict have stoked inflation fears globally and shifted market expectations toward interest rate increases rather than cuts. Markets now anticipate that central banks will raise rather than lower rates in response to elevated energy costs affecting transportation, manufacturing, and consumer prices across the economy.

The Trump administration has temporarily stopped deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo due to an Ebola outbreak in the country. The flights were suspended over concerns that they could expose Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials to the disease and potentially bring it closer to the United States.

The decision comes as public health officials monitor the outbreak in Congo. The pause affects ICE deportation operations that had been returning individuals to the central African nation.

The move reflects growing attention to disease risks as the administration manages its deportation policies. ICE officials expressed concern about the safety of personnel involved in the flights and the broader public health implications of continuing operations to an Ebola-affected region.

The suspension is temporary, though officials have not specified when flights might resume. The decision balances immigration enforcement priorities against infectious disease concerns.

Public health experts have said neither Ebola nor hantavirus poses an immediate threat to most Americans. However, they warn that eroding public trust in health systems could prove dangerous when a genuine crisis emerges. The current outbreaks are revealing weaknesses in how Americans perceive and respond to public health guidance.

The Congo situation adds to ongoing debates about how the government manages disease threats. The administration must now weigh its deportation agenda against the practical risks of operating in regions experiencing infectious disease outbreaks.

The deportation pause comes during a period when the administration has emphasized stricter immigration enforcement. Officials have not indicated whether similar suspensions might be considered for flights to other countries experiencing health emergencies.

The Congo national soccer team canceled training in Kinshasa due to health restrictions related to the outbreak. The team still plans to travel to the United States for pre-World Cup preparations despite the disease situation in their home country.
