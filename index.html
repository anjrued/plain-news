The World Health Organization and international health partners are mobilizing resources to support Congo's response. However, the eastern region's ongoing security challenges and poor infrastructure complicate efforts to track cases, treat patients, and vaccinate at-risk populations.

Previous Ebola outbreaks in Congo have killed thousands. The 2018-2020 epidemic in the same eastern region became the second-largest Ebola outbreak in history, claiming more than 2,200 lives. That crisis was marked by similar community resistance and attacks on health workers.

Health officials emphasize that early detection and isolation of cases remain critical to stopping transmission. The international community is watching closely, aware that without swift action, the outbreak could spread beyond Congo's borders into neighboring countries, potentially triggering a regional health emergency.

Google accidentally published details of an unpatched security vulnerability in Chromium, the open source browser engine that powers Chrome, Edge, and other major web browsers. The premature disclosure means millions of users may be at risk until a fix is released.

The company posted information about the flaw to its public issue tracker before a patch was ready for distribution. Security researchers consider this a serious lapse in responsible disclosure practices, as attackers can now study the vulnerability and potentially exploit it before users receive protection.

Chromium serves as the foundation for most modern browsers including Google Chrome, Microsoft Edge, Brave, Opera, and Vivaldi. When a security flaw exists in Chromium, it affects the browsing security of billions of internet users worldwide. The accidental exposure means malicious actors now have a roadmap to the vulnerability before defenses are in place.

Google has not specified the severity level of the flaw or which browser functions it affects. The company typically classifies security issues as low, medium, high, or critical based on the potential damage an attacker could cause. The premature disclosure has drawn criticism from security experts who emphasize that coordinated vulnerability disclosure exists specifically to prevent this scenario.

Browser makers typically coordinate security patches to ensure users across different platforms receive protection simultaneously. This coordinated approach prevents a window of opportunity where some users remain vulnerable while others are protected. The accidental disclosure disrupts this process.

Google has since removed the detailed technical information from public view, but security researchers note that once information is published online, it can be archived and distributed beyond the original source. The company is now racing to complete and distribute a patch across all affected browsers.

Users cannot take specific action to protect themselves until updates are released. Security experts recommend keeping browsers set to automatic updates and installing patches as soon as they become available. The incident highlights the complex coordination required to protect internet users from security threats in an ecosystem where a single code base powers multiple competing products.

Google has not provided a timeline for when the patch will be ready for public release.

Today, 7:00 PM ET

Global sea levels have been rising at an accelerating rate since 1960, according to new research that refines decades of measurement data. The findings confirm what climate scientists have long suspected but can now document with greater precision.

Researchers analyzed satellite data and coastal tide gauge measurements spanning more than six decades. By accounting for regional variations and improving error correction methods, they produced the most accurate reconstruction yet of how quickly oceans are rising worldwide.

The study shows that the rate of sea level rise is not constant but has been steadily increasing. This acceleration matches predictions based on warming temperatures, which cause seawater to expand and ice sheets to melt faster. The research provides a clearer baseline for understanding current trends and projecting future impacts.

Sea level rise directly affects hundreds of millions of people living in coastal areas. Even small increases can lead to more frequent flooding, saltwater intrusion into freshwater supplies, and erosion of shorelines. Low-lying island nations and delta regions face particularly acute risks.

The improved measurements matter for planning purposes. Cities and countries use sea level projections to design flood defenses, update building codes, and make long-term infrastructure decisions. More accurate historical data allows for better models of what to expect in coming decades.

The research also helps scientists separate natural ocean variability from human-caused changes. Ocean levels fluctuate due to weather patterns, ocean currents, and other factors. By refining the data, researchers can more clearly identify the underlying acceleration trend.

The findings align with other climate indicators showing warming continues to intensify. Arctic ice melt has accelerated, major ice sheets in Greenland and Antarctica are losing mass faster, and ocean temperatures keep setting records. Each of these factors contributes to rising seas.

While the study focuses on past measurements rather than future predictions, it strengthens the evidence base for existing projections. Most estimates suggest seas could rise between one and three feet by 2100, depending on future emissions and ice sheet behavior. The new research confirms those projections rest on solid observational ground.

Scientists emphasize that the acceleration observed since 1960 will likely continue unless greenhouse gas emissions are substantially reduced. The thermal inertia of oceans means seas will keep rising for decades even if temperatures stabilize.
