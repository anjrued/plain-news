The United States and Israel have conducted new military strikes targeting Iranian positions as diplomatic talks continue between Washington and Tehran. President Trump announced he had instructed the Department of Defense to postpone strikes against Iranian power plants and energy infrastructure for five days, citing what he described as "very good and productive conversations" with Iran over the previous two days.

Despite Trump's announcement of a pause, the Israeli Defense Forces launched a fresh wave of strikes on Tehran approximately 40 minutes after the president's statement. The IDF said it would continue operations in accordance with Israeli government directives. An Israeli military official told the Times of Israel that the military could not comment on U.S. negotiations with Iran, calling it a "political echelon matter," and stated the military would continue striking according to its plans until instructed otherwise.

According to the IDF, the strikes targeted military bases, weapons sites, and infrastructure including an Islamic Revolutionary Guards Corps air defense headquarters, a Quds Force base, intelligence sites, and a defense ministry missile production facility. An Al Jazeera Arabic correspondent in Tehran reported that explosions in the capital were "unprecedented" in size and volume.

The Israeli military said it had destroyed or rendered inoperable approximately 330 of Iran's estimated 470 ballistic missile launchers since the conflict began. The military reported that missile fire on Israel has declined to about 10 per day in recent days, down from roughly 90 at the start of the conflict.

According to the Human Rights Activists News Agency, at least 206 attacks were recorded across 15 Iranian provinces in a 24-hour period, resulting in at least four casualties. The agency reported that at least 15 percent of total human casualties in Iran have been children. Earlier reports indicated that total fatalities in Iran have surpassed 1,500, with some rights groups reporting figures as high as 3,230.

The military actions occur as Israel continues its campaign in Lebanon. Israeli strikes hit Beirut's southern suburbs, with Lebanon's health ministry reporting one person killed. Israeli forces have also positioned themselves inside the southern village of Aita al-Chaab with surveillance equipment.

Israel's far-right Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich called for the annexation of areas in southern Lebanon, stating that Israel should "apply sovereignty" over territories under its control. He indicated the military campaign should result in a change of Israel's borders, with the Litani River serving as the new boundary.

Trump said the United States and Iran are nearing a peace deal, though specific terms remain undisclosed. Oil prices rose following the military actions as markets assessed potential disruptions to Middle Eastern supply routes.

Yesterday, 11:22 AM ET

Tulsi Gabbard is stepping down as US director of national intelligence, ending a tumultuous tenure marked by her exclusion from key policy decisions. In a resignation letter addressed to President Donald Trump, she announced her departure effective June 30.

Gabbard cited personal reasons for her exit, writing that her husband Abraham "has recently been diagnosed with an extremely rare form of bone cancer" and that she needs to step away from public service to support him through his treatment.

However, Reuters reported that the White House forced Gabbard to resign, citing a source familiar with the matter. A spokesperson for Gabbard's office disputed this characterization, calling rumors of a forced ouster "100% false."

Trump confirmed the resignation on his Truth Social platform, stating that Gabbard "has done an incredible job, and we will miss her." He named Aaron Lukas, the principal deputy director of national intelligence, as acting director.

Gabbard's time leading the intelligence community proved challenging from the start. The former congresswoman and Democrat lacked a traditional intelligence background, and her foreign policy views sometimes diverged from Trump's approach to overseas military intervention. Yet she sought to align herself with the president by pledging to root out what she described as politicization within US spy agencies and by embracing his election denial agenda.

Despite these efforts, Gabbard became increasingly sidelined within the administration. Trump excluded her from significant national security discussions regarding Iran and Venezuela, according to people familiar with her office. Last month, Trump was asking cabinet members whether he should replace her, according to two people briefed on those conversations.

The position of director of national intelligence is among the most consequential in government, overseeing coordination across the nation's intelligence agencies including the CIA and NSA, and serving as the principal intelligence advisor to the president.

Gabbard's departure comes during a period of significant transition within the administration's national security team. The country faces ongoing international crises, including tensions in the Middle East and complex diplomatic relationships that the administration continues to navigate.

Before taking the intelligence director role, Gabbard served in Congress and ran for president. She also has a military background. Her unconventional appointment had drawn scrutiny from observers across the political spectrum, both for her lack of traditional intelligence credentials and for her previous positions on foreign policy matters.

Her resignation adds another significant personnel change to the Trump administration as it manages multiple national security challenges and international policy priorities.

NASA has added more missions to SpaceX's commercial crew contract because Boeing has been unable to certify its spacecraft for regular crew transport to the International Space Station. The additional missions represent an expansion of SpaceX's role in ferrying astronauts to orbit.

The space agency announced the contract modification following ongoing delays in Boeing's Starliner certification process. SpaceX has been flying astronauts to the space station since 2020 under NASA's Commercial Crew Program, which originally intended to have two providers available for crew rotation missions.

Boeing's Starliner spacecraft has faced technical setbacks that have prevented the company from achieving operational status. The delays have left NASA increasingly dependent on SpaceX's Crew Dragon spacecraft to maintain continuous American access to the space station.

The contract addition ensures NASA can maintain its crew rotation schedule while Boeing works to resolve certification requirements. SpaceX has completed multiple crewed missions to the station, establishing a track record of operational flights.

NASA designed the Commercial Crew Program with redundancy in mind, seeking at least two independent providers to reduce risk and maintain competition. The reliance on a single provider runs counter to that original strategy, though NASA has prioritized maintaining uninterrupted crew access to the orbiting laboratory.

The number of additional missions and their projected launch dates were not specified in available reports. Boeing continues working toward certification, though no timeline has been provided for when Starliner might begin regular operational flights.

SpaceX founder Elon Musk's company beat Boeing to operational status despite Boeing receiving more initial funding under the commercial crew contracts. SpaceX has transported both NASA astronauts and private crew members to orbit using its Crew Dragon capsule, which launches atop Falcon 9 rockets.

The situation highlights the challenges of developing human spaceflight systems, even for experienced aerospace contractors. NASA's investment in two separate providers was intended to protect against exactly this scenario, where technical issues with one system would not strand astronauts or halt space station operations.

NASCAR champion Kyle Busch died after severe pneumonia developed into sepsis, according to a statement from his family. The two-time NASCAR champion passed away at age 41 on Thursday, one day after losing consciousness in a racing simulator at a Charlotte, North Carolina facility.

Busch was preparing for the Coca-Cola 600 race scheduled for Sunday at Charlotte Motor Speedway when he collapsed. He was taken to a hospital in Charlotte, where he died. The family received a medical evaluation on Saturday confirming the cause of death.

"The medical evaluation provided to the Busch Family concluded that severe pneumonia progressed into sepsis, resulting in rapid and overwhelming associated complications," said Dakota Hunter, vice-president of Kyle Busch Companies, in a statement.

The day before his death, Busch experienced serious symptoms. According to a 911 call obtained by the Associated Press, an unidentified caller reported that Busch had shortness of breath, felt overheating, thought he might lose consciousness, and was coughing up blood. The caller indicated that Busch was lying on the bathroom floor inside the complex but was conscious when emergency services arrived.

Busch had been dealing with health issues before his death. He competed in a race at Watkins Glen on May 10 while experiencing what was thought to be a sinus cold. During that event, he radioed his team asking for medical attention. Despite his condition, Busch won the Trucks Series race at Dover the following weekend and finished 17th in the All-Star race on Sunday.

Brad Keselowski, another NASCAR driver, acknowledged that Busch had not been feeling well recently but declined to provide specifics.

Sepsis is a life-threatening medical emergency that develops when the body has an extreme overreaction to an infection. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the immune system's response goes into overdrive, releasing chemicals that damage the body's own tissues and organs. This can cause widespread inflammation, form microscopic blood clots, and cause blood vessels to leak.

Busch was one of NASCAR's most successful drivers. During his two-decade career across NASCAR's top three series, he won 234 races, more than any driver in history. He was a two-time NASCAR champion.

The racing community mourned his loss deeply. NASCAR's CEO Steve O'Donnell described him as "an American badass." Donald Trump called him "a true talent who loved NASCAR and its fans. Kyle was a WINNER!" JD Vance referred to him as "one of NASCAR's greatest racers."

In tribute, all 39 drivers competing in Sunday's race displayed a black number 8 decal on their cars to honor Busch.

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at age 95. His death was announced Monday on his official website and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He passed away at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided.

Rollins was among the last surviving stars of the bebop generation, a pioneering movement that transformed jazz from primarily dance and ballad music into a form of startling musical expression. Over a career spanning more than seven decades, he released more than 60 albums and collaborated with jazz legends including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, and John Coltrane.

Saxophonist Branford Marsalis has called Rollins "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting him with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, President Barack Obama said Rollins had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in Harlem, New York in 1930, he began learning saxophone at age seven. His early bands included future stars Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew. He quickly gained recognition for his melodic genius and willingness to break with convention, establishing himself as an innovative force alongside Davis and Charlie Parker.

Rollins struggled with heroin addiction in his early years and was imprisoned on Rikers Island for 10 months following an armed robbery in 1950. He overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955, after which he experienced a burst of creative activity.

He released his debut album as bandleader in 1953 and recorded 17 more albums by the end of the 1950s. His landmark recordings include "Saxophone Colossus" from 1956, featuring his signature tune "St Thomas," and "Way Out West" from 1957, which explored a piano-free improvisation style. "Freedom Suite" from 1958 featured a 20-minute title track that became an elegant argument for freedom during the civil rights movement.

In 1959, Rollins took a three-year break from recording and performing, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway. This intensive period inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge." He took another sabbatical from 1969 to 1971 to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout the 1960s and beyond, Rollins continued evolving his style, exploring Latin American music, funk, and R&B influences. He composed the soundtrack to the 1966 film "Alfie" and added uncredited soloing to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album "Tattoo You."

In his later years, Rollins campaigned on climate change issues and released the 1998 album "Global Warming." He married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004.

Trump administration pauses deportation flights to Congo over Ebola concerns

The Trump administration has temporarily halted deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo due to a widening Ebola outbreak, according to reporting by Politico. The pause aims to prevent potential exposure of Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials to the virus. However, public health experts have questioned whether the measure will actually help prevent dis...

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily halted deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo due to a widening Ebola outbreak, according to reporting by Politico. The pause aims to prevent potential exposure of Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials to the virus.

However, public health experts have questioned whether the measure will actually help prevent disease spread. The decision represents a rare instance where the administration has prioritized health concerns over its aggressive deportation agenda.

The temporary suspension affects only flights to Congo and does not impact deportation operations to other nations. The administration continues to process and deport individuals to other countries as part of federal immigration law enforcement.

The pause has created complications for at least one woman who was moved to Kinshasa, the capital of Congo. Officials now say they will not return her despite a judge's order for her return, citing the Ebola travel restrictions.

This decision comes as the Trump administration faces intensifying scrutiny over its broader deportation operations. A group of 40 House Democrats recently sent a letter to the Federal Aviation Administration expressing "grave concerns" about what they describe as secretive deportation flights and demanding greater transparency.

The lawmakers referenced credible reports indicating that individuals have been placed on flights without notice to their lawyers or family members. They stated that "credible reports indicate that individuals have been placed on flights without notice to counsel or family members, effectively disappearing from public view when flights are inappropriately shielded from tracking systems."

The letter highlights allegations of abuse and rights violations at detention centers operating as central nodes in the administration's deportation program. Human rights groups tracking ICE flights have documented an 84 percent increase in the number of flights during 2025 compared to 2024.

The lawmakers also raised concerns about unusual FAA permissions granted to charter jets used for deportation flights, allowing them to block certain data from public flight tracking sites. This has made it harder for the public and Congress to monitor ICE air operations.

New Jersey congressman Rob Menendez, who led the effort, stated that transparency is crucial. "We want people to understand what is happening on their dime," he said.

The letter calls on the FAA to provide detailed information on ICE air operations since Trump took office, including flight origins, destinations, and passenger numbers. It also requests information on how the agency assesses humanitarian conditions on ICE flights, including the use of full body restraints during deportation.

The Trump administration has previously defended its detention and deportation practices, describing restraints as "long-standing, standard ICE protocol" designed to ensure safety. The administration has argued its detention centers maintain "higher standards than most US prisons."

Jazz Saxophonist Sonny Rollins Dies at 95

9:00 AM ET

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at age 95. His death was announced Monday on his official website and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He passed away at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided.

Rollins was among the last surviving stars of the bebop generation, a pioneering movement that transformed jazz from primarily dance and ballad music into a form of startling musical expression. Over a career spanning more than seven decades, he released more than 60 albums and collaborated with jazz legends including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, and John Coltrane.

Saxophonist Branford Marsalis has called Rollins "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting him with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, President Barack Obama said Rollins had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in Harlem, New York in 1930, he began learning saxophone at age seven. His early bands included future stars Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew. He quickly gained recognition for his melodic genius and willingness to break with convention, establishing himself as an innovative force alongside Davis and Charlie Parker.

Rollins struggled with heroin addiction in his early years and was imprisoned on Rikers Island for 10 months following an armed robbery in 1950. He overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955, after which he experienced a burst of creative activity.

He released his debut album as bandleader in 1953 and recorded 17 more albums by the end of the 1950s. His landmark recordings include "Saxophone Colossus" from 1956, featuring his signature tune "St Thomas," and "Way Out West" from 1957, which explored a piano-free improvisation style. "Freedom Suite" from 1958 featured a 20-minute title track that became an elegant argument for freedom during the civil rights movement.

In 1959, Rollins took a three-year break from recording and performing, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway. This intensive period inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge." He took another sabbatical from 1969 to 1971 to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout the 1960s and beyond, Rollins continued evolving his style, exploring Latin American music, funk, and R&B influences. He composed the soundtrack to the 1966 film "Alfie" and added uncredited soloing to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album "Tattoo You."

In his later years, Rollins campaigned on climate change issues and released the 1998 album "Global Warming." He married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004.
