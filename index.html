A federal court panel ruled that Alabama cannot use a Republican-friendly congressional map in the 2026 elections because it was deliberately drawn to discriminate against Black voters. The three-judge panel said the state's 2023 redistricting plan, which lawmakers passed but never implemented, violates the Voting Rights Act.

The decision represents a significant legal victory for voting rights advocates and a setback for Republican efforts to reshape Alabama's congressional districts. The court found that Alabama's legislature intentionally refused to remedy the discriminatory vote dilution it had previously been ordered to fix.

The case stems from a long-running legal battle over Alabama's congressional maps that began in 2021. Black voters sued the state, arguing that the original map diluted their voting power. A court agreed and ordered Alabama to draw a new map that provided fair representation. When lawmakers passed the 2023 plan, the court found it still diluted Black voting power and ultimately appointed a special master to draw a map that included a second majority-Black district. The US Supreme Court upheld that court-drawn map in 2023.

However, after the US Supreme Court weakened a major provision of the Voting Rights Act in April, Alabama moved to use the 2023 map in the upcoming elections. The state even postponed its congressional primary to attempt this shift.

The three-judge panel, which included judges appointed by both Democratic and Republican presidents, rejected this effort. In their opinion, the judges wrote that they could not require Alabama voters to cast ballots under "a districting plan tainted by intentional race-based discrimination."

The court reviewed the circumstances of the legislature's decision to pass the 2023 map and concluded it was enacted with discriminatory intent. The judges noted that lawmakers "well knew that a plan without an additional Black-opportunity district would dilute Black Alabamians' opportunity to participate in the political process, and it intentionally enacted that very plan."

Alabama is expected to appeal the ruling to the US Supreme Court. The case will likely test whether the high court's recent weakening of the Voting Rights Act permits states to use maps drawn with discriminatory intent, even when that intent can be proven.

The Alabama decision comes as Republican-led states across the South attempt to redraw congressional maps following the Supreme Court's April ruling. Tennessee eliminated a majority-Black congressional district in Memphis, while Louisiana and South Carolina are pursuing similar efforts. Civil rights groups and Black leaders have condemned these attempts as an effort to deny Black voters political representation.

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his website Monday and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was disclosed.

Rollins was a towering figure in jazz history, earning the nickname "the Saxophone Colossus" for his revolutionary approach to improvisation and melodic invention. Over a career spanning more than six decades, he released more than 60 albums and collaborated with jazz icons including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane, Dizzy Gillespie and Max Roach.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in Harlem in 1930, he began playing saxophone at age seven, inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. His early high school band included future stars Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew. Rollins described himself as "primitive" and said he went "with my feelings more than my brain," an approach that helped define the bebop movement's creative freedom.

Miles Davis praised Rollins for becoming "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" and called him "an aggressive, innovative player who always had fresh musical ideas." Saxophonist Branford Marsalis later called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting Rollins with the 2010 National Medal of the Arts in 2011, Barack Obama said the saxophonist had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Rollins' career faced a serious setback when heroin addiction led him to commit armed robbery in 1950 to fund his habit. He spent 10 months on Rikers Island but successfully overcame his addiction through rehabilitation in 1955. This period of recovery sparked extraordinary creative output.

His landmark albums included Saxophone Colossus (1956), featuring the signature tune "St Thomas," named after his mother's Caribbean birthplace; Way Out West (1957), which explored a piano-free style; and Freedom Suite (1958), with its 20-minute title composition reflecting on civil rights themes. Throughout his career, Rollins continued reinventing himself, exploring avant-garde jazz, fusion, Latin music, funk and calypso.

Rollins was known for taking extended breaks to refine his craft. In 1959, he stepped away from recording and performing for three years, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway. His 1962 comeback album, The Bridge, documented this period. He took another sabbatical from 1969 to 1971 to study yoga, philosophy and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Later in life, Rollins championed environmental causes, releasing the 1998 album Global Warming and performing benefit concerts addressing climate change. He married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004.

The Trump administration has temporarily halted removal flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo following a widening Ebola outbreak in the country, according to reporting by Politico. The pause reflects concerns that continuing deportation operations could expose Immigration and Customs Enforcement personnel to the virus.

However, the move has created complications for individuals already transferred to the country. At least one woman was moved to Kinshasa, the capital of the DRC, before the travel restrictions took effect. Despite a judge's order for her return, officials say they will not bring her back because of the Ebola travel ban, leaving her in legal limbo.

Public health experts have questioned whether the pause will actually prevent disease spread. The virus spreads through direct contact with bodily fluids of infected individuals. It has caused multiple deadly outbreaks across Central Africa in recent decades, but experts have stated that Ebola does not currently pose an immediate threat to most Americans.

The suspension represents one of the first instances where disease concerns have prompted the Trump administration to temporarily halt immigration enforcement operations. ICE regularly conducts removal flights to countries worldwide as part of its enforcement mission. Federal health agencies continue to monitor the situation in Congo and coordinate with immigration officials on safety protocols.

The administration has not announced when flights might resume or what conditions would need to be met to restart operations. It also has not indicated whether similar precautions will apply to other countries if disease outbreaks occur elsewhere.

The Ebola outbreak has disrupted other activities involving Congo beyond immigration enforcement. The country's national soccer team cancelled training in Kinshasa due to health restrictions. However, the team is still planning to travel to the United States for pre-World Cup preparation, despite the health situation.

The deportation pause comes as the Trump administration faces increased scrutiny over its immigration enforcement practices. A group of 40 House Democrats recently expressed concerns about the administration's expanded use of deportation flights, citing allegations of mistreatment and inhumane conditions. The lawmakers have called for greater transparency and congressional oversight of ICE air operations.

The administration has defended its immigration detention and transfer practices, describing them as meeting established legal standards and safety protocols. Officials have previously stated that claims of rights violations are "categorically false" and argued that detention centers maintain standards comparable to or exceeding most U.S. prisons.

The Trump administration's hardline immigration agenda saw ICE flights increase by 84 percent during 2025 compared to 2024, according to monitoring by human rights groups.

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his website Monday and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was disclosed.

Rollins was a towering figure in jazz history, earning the nickname "the Saxophone Colossus" for his revolutionary approach to improvisation and melodic invention. Over a career spanning more than six decades, he released more than 60 albums and collaborated with jazz icons including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane, Dizzy Gillespie and Max Roach.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in Harlem in 1930, he began playing saxophone at age seven, inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. His early high school band included future stars Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew. Rollins described himself as "primitive" and said he went "with my feelings more than my brain," an approach that helped define the bebop movement's creative freedom.

Miles Davis praised Rollins for becoming "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" and called him "an aggressive, innovative player who always had fresh musical ideas." Saxophonist Branford Marsalis later called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting Rollins with the 2010 National Medal of the Arts in 2011, Barack Obama said the saxophonist had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Rollins' career faced a serious setback when heroin addiction led him to commit armed robbery in 1950 to fund his habit. He spent 10 months on Rikers Island but successfully overcame his addiction through rehabilitation in 1955. This period of recovery sparked extraordinary creative output.

His landmark albums included Saxophone Colossus (1956), featuring the signature tune "St Thomas," named after his mother's Caribbean birthplace; Way Out West (1957), which explored a piano-free style; and Freedom Suite (1958), with its 20-minute title composition reflecting on civil rights themes. Throughout his career, Rollins continued reinventing himself, exploring avant-garde jazz, fusion, Latin music, funk and calypso.

Rollins was known for taking extended breaks to refine his craft. In 1959, he stepped away from recording and performing for three years, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway. His 1962 comeback album, The Bridge, documented this period. He took another sabbatical from 1969 to 1971 to study yoga, philosophy and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Later in life, Rollins championed environmental causes, releasing the 1998 album Global Warming and performing benefit concerts addressing climate change. He married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004.
