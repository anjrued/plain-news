Astronomers have discovered a white dwarf star locked in an extreme 8.5-minute orbit with a binary companion, making it one of the fastest known binary systems. The white dwarf is being gradually torn apart by gravitational forces from its companion star.

The discovery reveals a binary star system where two stellar remnants orbit each other at extraordinary speed. The 8.5-minute orbital period is among the shortest ever observed, indicating the two objects are extremely close together.

White dwarfs are the dense cores left behind after stars like our Sun exhaust their nuclear fuel. In this system, the intense gravitational interaction between the two objects is stripping material from the white dwarf's outer layers.

The rapid orbit means the stars complete more than 169 revolutions around each other every day. Such tight binary systems are important for understanding stellar evolution and the eventual fate of stars.

Binary systems with ultra-short orbital periods provide astronomers with laboratories to study extreme physics. The gravitational forces in such close proximity can produce observable effects that test theories of gravity and stellar structure.

The discovery adds to the catalog of compact binary systems that may eventually merge. When white dwarfs or other dense stellar remnants spiral together and collide, they can produce detectable gravitational waves and potentially trigger supernova explosions.

Systems like this one help scientists understand how binary stars evolve over time. As the two objects lose energy through gravitational radiation, their orbit gradually shrinks, bringing them even closer together.

The finding demonstrates the capability of modern telescopes and observation techniques to detect and characterize extreme astronomical objects. Identifying systems with such rapid orbital periods requires precise timing measurements and sustained observation campaigns.

Alberta Premier Danielle Smith has announced a referendum on whether the province should pursue independence from Canada, staking her political future on the vote. The move represents one of the most significant challenges to Canadian national unity in recent history.

The referendum will ask Alberta voters to decide whether the province should begin the process of separating from Canada. Smith has framed the vote as a response to what she characterizes as federal policies that harm Alberta's economic interests, particularly in the energy sector.

Alberta is Canada's fourth most populous province and home to the country's oil and gas industry. The province has long had tensions with the federal government over energy policy and environmental regulations. However, calls for outright separation have historically remained on the political fringe.

Smith's decision to call the referendum puts her political career directly on the line. The premier has indicated she views the vote as a mandate on her leadership and her approach to federal relations. If the referendum fails, her position as premier could become untenable.

The announcement has immediate implications for Canadian politics and could reshape the country's economic and political landscape. Alberta accounts for a significant portion of Canada's GDP and natural resource production. The province's potential departure would have major consequences for federal finances and national governance.

The timing of the referendum and the specific wording of the ballot question have not yet been announced. Smith will need to navigate both provincial legislative requirements and the political realities of building support for separation among Alberta voters.

Previous separation movements in Canada have centered primarily on Quebec, which held referendums on independence in 1980 and 1995. The 1995 vote was extremely close, with federalist forces winning by less than one percentage point. Alberta has never held a formal referendum on separation.

The federal government in Ottawa has not yet issued a formal response to Smith's announcement. Constitutional experts have noted that any attempt at provincial separation would face significant legal and political hurdles, as Canada's constitution does not include a clear mechanism for a province to leave the federation.

Uganda Confirms 3 More Ebola Cases as WHO Warns Outbreak Spreading Rapidly

12:19 PM ET

An Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Uganda has been declared a "public health emergency of international concern" by the World Health Organization, with the outbreak spreading rapidly across the region.

The WHO made its declaration on Sunday after 88 deaths and more than 300 suspected cases linked to the outbreak of the Bundibugyo virus. Dr Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, the director general of the WHO, announced the decision before convening a formal emergency committee at the organization.

The outbreak has escalated quickly since it was first reported. Within a week, suspected cases tripled from 246 cases and 65 deaths to almost 750 suspected cases and 177 suspected deaths, according to WHO officials who described the situation as "deeply worrisome."

There have been 80 suspected deaths, eight laboratory-confirmed cases and 246 suspected cases in the DRC's Ituri province, which borders Uganda and South Sudan. Two cases, including one death, have been reported in Uganda's capital, Kampala, in people who had travelled from the DRC. On Sunday, a laboratory confirmed a further case in the city of Goma, in a separate part of the DRC.

"A positive case in Goma has been confirmed by tests carried out by the laboratory. It involves the wife of a man who died of Ebola in Bunia, who travelled to Goma after her husband's death whilst already infected," Prof Jean-Jacques Muyembe, director of the Congolese National Institute for Biomedical Research, told AFP.

The Bundibugyo virus is one of three strains that can cause Ebola virus disease and the least common. There are currently no approved vaccines or treatments for the strain, or specific tests.

Dr Jean Kaseya, the director general of Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention, told Sky News: "Currently, I'm on panic mode because people are dying. I don't have medicines. I don't have [a] vaccine to support countries."

He said officials were in talks with companies who had potential tests, vaccines and treatments at an early stage of development, expressing hope that some would become available in the "coming weeks."

The WHO said there were "significant uncertainties as to the true number of infected persons and geographic spread associated with this event at the present time." It added that the signs "all point towards a potentially much larger outbreak than what is currently being detected and reported, with significant local and regional risk of spread."

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention said on Sunday it was supporting interagency partners coordinating the safe withdrawal of a small number of Americans directly affected by the outbreak. The Trump administration has temporarily paused the removal of refugees to the DRC during the outbreak.
