The United States military conducted strikes targeting Iranian missile launch sites and boats along the Gulf coast, with officials characterizing the action as defensive while diplomatic talks proceed in Qatar.

According to US Central Command, the strikes focused on locations used for missile operations on Iran's coastal areas. The military emphasized that a ceasefire remains in effect despite the strikes and that the defensive action does not undermine ongoing negotiations aimed at resolving the conflict.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio indicated that an Iran peace deal could be finalized within days. He stated that the Strait of Hormuz, a vital shipping route for global oil transport, will reopen regardless of circumstances, though he provided no specifics about the method. The Strait has become a central concern in escalating regional tensions.

The timing of the military strikes coincides with senior Iranian negotiators arriving in Qatar for talks with US delegations. This simultaneous pursuit of military pressure and diplomatic engagement reflects the administration's dual approach to the situation.

Both Iranian and American officials have moderated expectations for quick progress in negotiations. A Foreign Ministry spokesperson from Iran stated that while significant portions of discussions have been resolved, claiming an imminent agreement would be premature. The spokesperson emphasized that substantial issues remain under discussion.

International markets responded with mixed signals following news of the strikes. Asian stock markets and oil prices showed volatility, reflecting uncertainty about both the military operations and the outcome of ongoing diplomatic efforts. Energy commodity prices have been particularly unstable due to concerns about regional stability and potential disruptions to global oil supplies.

The broader geopolitical context involves longstanding tensions over Iran's nuclear program and regional military activities. The conflict has drawn international attention due to implications for global energy security and regional stability.

UN officials have called for military restraint to prevent escalation. The International Atomic Energy Agency, the UN nuclear watchdog, has emphasized the importance of avoiding actions that could risk nuclear accidents during this volatile period.

The strikes represent a continuation of US military operations in the region even as diplomatic channels remain active. US Central Command maintained that all actions were proportional responses to threats facing American forces and strictly defensive in nature.

Regional analysts note the delicate balance between demonstrating military capability and maintaining credibility in negotiations. The administration's approach suggests it aims to maintain pressure while keeping diplomatic options open for a potential resolution to the conflict.

The situation remains fluid, with both military and diplomatic developments unfolding simultaneously. Yesterday, 11:24 PM ET

Authorities in Orange County have ordered the evacuation of 40,000 people after a chemical storage tank at a Garden Grove facility began leaking methyl methacrylate, a highly flammable substance used in manufacturing resins and plastics.

The crisis started Thursday at a GKN Aerospace facility when a storage tank began off-gassing and threatened to fail. Local authorities initially responded with a hazmat team and ordered residents to evacuate, but lifted the order later that day. The situation worsened after damage to a valve on the tank created additional operational challenges that prevented complete mitigation.

By Friday, new evacuation orders had expanded to cover residents across six cities including Cypress, Stanton, Anaheim, Buena Park and Westminster. Orange County Fire Authority Division Chief Craig Covey told reporters the danger remained acute.

"We have a tank that is actively in crisis," Covey said at a news conference. "There are literally two options left remaining. One: the tank fails and spills a total of about 6-7,000 gallons of very bad chemicals into the parking lot in that area. Or two: the tank goes into a thermal runaway and blows up, affecting the tanks that are around them that have fuel or the chemicals in them as well."

Covey warned that the tank would eventually fail but authorities did not know when. Emergency crews established containment barriers using sandbags to prevent any chemical spill from entering storm drains, creeks or nearby ocean waters.

County Health Officer Dr Regina Chinsio-Kwong explained the health risks posed by the chemical. If methyl methacrylate heats up, it releases vapors that can cause respiratory issues, itching, burning eyes, nausea and headaches.

Garden Grove police reported that approximately 15 percent of people facing evacuation orders were refusing to leave. The city established at least two shelters for displaced residents.

However, by Monday, firefighters said they had eliminated the threat of an explosion through overnight cooling and monitoring operations. The tank had cracked over the weekend, which officials hoped would relieve internal pressure and reduce explosion risk.

Following the stabilization of the immediate danger, authorities began allowing evacuees to return home. Approximately 65 percent of those evacuated received clearance to return to their residences as emergency teams continued monitoring the facility.

Despite the improved situation, officials emphasized the emergency was not entirely over. Remaining evacuees have not yet received clearance to return as crews work to fully stabilize the chemical storage facility.

The incident disrupted normal life across Orange County, with schools and businesses in the evacuation zone closing during the peak of the crisis. Emergency response teams from multiple agencies coordinated the evacuation and ongoing stabilization efforts.

Garden Grove is located about 30 miles southeast of downtown Los Angeles, less than a mile from Disneyland's theme parks, which were not placed under evacuation orders.

Yesterday, 7:57 AM ET

Google has redesigned its search box for the first time in nearly 30 years, embedding artificial intelligence directly into the core search experience. The changes were announced at Google I/O, the company's annual conference for software developers, and began rolling out globally to desktop and mobile users on Tuesday.

The redesign allows users to enter longer and more conversational queries than the search syntax previously required. Rather than directing users to a list of links, the new interface guides them to engage directly with Google's chatbot powered by Gemini, the company's latest artificial intelligence model. Elizabeth Reid, who oversees the search product, described the changes as "the biggest Google Search had seen in its nearly 30-year history," calling the platform "AI search."

The visual layout now closely resembles expanded versions of AI Overviews, the bulleted summaries Google Search currently provides, rather than a traditional list of links. Users can still access the classic web results by clicking a tab labeled "Web." When users add photos, videos, or documents to the Chrome browser's search bar, Google Search automatically switches to AI mode.

Google introduced Gemini 3.5, the artificial intelligence model underlying these changes, which can make longer text predictions based on what users have already typed. Search results will increasingly include visual elements and suggestions to interact with Google's AI to complete actions like creating calendar invites or building spreadsheets within Google's suite of products.

A new feature called Gemini Spark operates as an autonomous AI agent within search. For subscribers to Google's AI Pro and Ultra plans, it can access information from Gmail, Google Calendar, and other Google products to conduct research, manage shopping, and plan trips and appointments. Another feature, called "generative user interface," can create customized visuals and interactive dashboards based on user requests.

The expansion reflects growing demand for AI-powered search. Since Google introduced AI Mode a year ago, queries to the chatbot-specific interface have doubled every quarter. Last month, Google Search reached an all-time high in query volume. The company reports that 900 million people use the Gemini app monthly.

The redesign represents Google's response to competition in the consumer AI space. However, the changes have raised concerns among publishers and content creators about reduced website traffic. The new design de-emphasizes traditional blue links to external sites, potentially decreasing visitors to sources that provide the underlying information for Google's AI systems. Google has not detailed how it plans to balance AI-generated content with links to original sources.

Yesterday, 9:41 PM ET

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, died at his home in Woodstock, New York, on Monday afternoon at age 95. His death was announced on his website with the statement that it came "with deep sorrow and profound love." No cause was provided.

Rollins emerged as a leading figure in the bebop generation, helping transform jazz from primarily a dance and ballad form into something far more expressive. Over more than seven decades, he released more than 60 albums and collaborated with jazz legends including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk and John Coltrane.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in Harlem in 1930, he earned his nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He picked up the saxophone at seven years old, inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz artists like Fats Waller. His high school band included future stars Jackie McLean and Art Taylor. Early in his career, he worked with established musicians and began composing original material.

Rollins struggled with heroin addiction in his younger years. In 1950, he committed armed robbery to fund his habit, later describing himself as "a despicable character." He served 10 months on Rikers Island but successfully overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955.

Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting him with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, Barack Obama said Rollins had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

The 1950s and 1960s marked Rollins' most prolific period. His 1956 album "Saxophone Colossus" featured his signature tune "St Thomas," named after his mother's Caribbean birthplace. In 1959, he took a three-year break from performing and recording, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway to avoid disturbing neighbors. This period led to his acclaimed 1962 comeback album "The Bridge."

Throughout his career, Rollins took periodic sabbaticals to develop his craft. Between 1969 and 1971, he traveled to an Indian ashram to study yoga, philosophy and meditation. He continued performing and recording into his later years, exploring fusion, funk and calypso styles. He also composed the soundtrack to the 1966 film "Alfie" and contributed uncredited saxophone work to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album "Tattoo You."

Beyond music, Rollins advocated for environmental causes, performing benefit concerts and releasing the 1998 album "Global Warming." He married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004.

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has stopped deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo due to an Ebola outbreak in the country. The pause aims to protect Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials and prevent potential spread of the disease to the United States.

The decision represents a shift in deportation policy driven by public health concerns. ICE officials could be exposed to the virus during deportation operations in areas affected by the outbreak.

The Ebola virus causes severe hemorrhagic fever and has historically resulted in high fatality rates during outbreaks in central Africa. The current outbreak in Congo has prompted health restrictions severe enough that the country's World Cup team cancelled training in the capital city of Kinshasa.

Public health experts have said that neither Ebola nor hantavirus, another disease that has recently drawn attention, poses an immediate threat to most Americans. However, the response to these outbreaks has exposed what experts describe as a trust deficit in the American public health system.

The concern among health officials extends beyond the immediate disease threat. They warn that eroded public confidence in health institutions could prove catastrophic when a more severe crisis arrives. The pause in deportation flights reflects the administration's calculation that the risk of disease transmission outweighs enforcement priorities.

The temporary halt applies specifically to flights bound for Congo. The administration has not indicated whether similar pauses might be implemented for other countries experiencing disease outbreaks. ICE has not announced when deportation flights to Congo might resume or what conditions would need to be met for operations to restart.

The decision comes as the administration has otherwise pursued aggressive deportation policies. The Ebola outbreak has created an exception to that approach based on health and safety considerations for federal personnel and domestic disease control.

Google has redesigned its search box for the first time in nearly 30 years, embedding artificial intelligence directly into the core search experience. The changes were announced at Google I/O, the company's annual conference for software developers, and began rolling out globally to desktop and mobile users on Tuesday.

The redesign allows users to enter longer and more conversational queries than the search syntax previously required. Rather than directing users to a list of links, the new interface guides them to engage directly with Google's chatbot powered by Gemini, the company's latest artificial intelligence model. Elizabeth Reid, who oversees the search product, described the changes as "the biggest Google Search had seen in its nearly 30-year history," calling the platform "AI search."

The visual layout now closely resembles expanded versions of AI Overviews, the bulleted summaries Google Search currently provides, rather than a traditional list of links. Users can still access the classic web results by clicking a tab labeled "Web." When users add photos, videos, or documents to the Chrome browser's search bar, Google Search automatically switches to AI mode.

Google introduced Gemini 3.5, the artificial intelligence model underlying these changes, which can make longer text predictions based on what users have already typed. Search results will increasingly include visual elements and suggestions to interact with Google's AI to complete actions like creating calendar invites or building spreadsheets within Google's suite of products.

A new feature called Gemini Spark operates as an autonomous AI agent within search. For subscribers to Google's AI Pro and Ultra plans, it can access information from Gmail, Google Calendar, and other Google products to conduct research, manage shopping, and plan trips and appointments. Another feature, called "generative user interface," can create customized visuals and interactive dashboards based on user requests.

The expansion reflects growing demand for AI-powered search. Since Google introduced AI Mode a year ago, queries to the chatbot-specific interface have doubled every quarter. Last month, Google Search reached an all-time high in query volume. The company reports that 900 million people use the Gemini app monthly.

The redesign represents Google's response to competition in the consumer AI space. However, the changes have raised concerns among publishers and content creators about reduced website traffic. The new design de-emphasizes traditional blue links to external sites, potentially decreasing visitors to sources that provide the underlying information for Google's AI systems. Google has not detailed how it plans to balance AI-generated content with links to original sources.

Jazz saxophonist Sonny Rollins dies at 95

1:00 AM ET

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, died at his home in Woodstock, New York, on Monday afternoon at age 95. His death was announced on his website with the statement that it came "with deep sorrow and profound love." No cause was provided.

Rollins emerged as a leading figure in the bebop generation, helping transform jazz from primarily a dance and ballad form into something far more expressive. Over more than seven decades, he released more than 60 albums and collaborated with jazz legends including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk and John Coltrane.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in Harlem in 1930, he earned his nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He picked up the saxophone at seven years old, inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz artists like Fats Waller. His high school band included future stars Jackie McLean and Art Taylor. Early in his career, he worked with established musicians and began composing original material.

Rollins struggled with heroin addiction in his younger years. In 1950, he committed armed robbery to fund his habit, later describing himself as "a despicable character." He served 10 months on Rikers Island but successfully overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955.

Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting him with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, Barack Obama said Rollins had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

The 1950s and 1960s marked Rollins' most prolific period. His 1956 album "Saxophone Colossus" featured his signature tune "St Thomas," named after his mother's Caribbean birthplace. In 1959, he took a three-year break from performing and recording, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway to avoid disturbing neighbors. This period led to his acclaimed 1962 comeback album "The Bridge."

Throughout his career, Rollins took periodic sabbaticals to develop his craft. Between 1969 and 1971, he traveled to an Indian ashram to study yoga, philosophy and meditation. He continued performing and recording into his later years, exploring fusion, funk and calypso styles. He also composed the soundtrack to the 1966 film "Alfie" and contributed uncredited saxophone work to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album "Tattoo You."

Beyond music, Rollins advocated for environmental causes, performing benefit concerts and releasing the 1998 album "Global Warming." He married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004.
