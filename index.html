Russia carried out a massive assault on Kyiv and its surrounding region using its powerful hypersonic Oreshnik ballistic missile for the third time in the conflict, killing at least four people and injuring approximately 100 others.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said Russia struck the city of Bila Tserkva in the Kyiv region with the Oreshnik missile. He described a heavy Russian attack that also damaged a water supply facility, burned down a market, and hit dozens of residential buildings and several schools. "They are genuinely deranged," Zelenskyy said on Telegram.

Russia's defense ministry confirmed using the Oreshnik, which can carry nuclear or conventional warheads. The ministry said it had successfully attacked Ukrainian military command facilities, airbases and other military enterprises using Oreshnik, Iskander, Kinzhal and Zircon missiles. Russia claimed the attack was retaliation for Ukrainian strikes on "civilian facilities on Russian territory."

Zelenskyy said the "heavy attack" on Kyiv involved 600 drones and 90 missiles of various kinds, including 36 ballistic missiles. "Unfortunately, not all of the ballistic missiles were intercepted. The largest number of hits was in Kyiv. Kyiv was the primary target of this Russian attack," he wrote. "It is important that this does not pass without consequences for Russia."

Kyiv Mayor Vitali Klitschko reported two people killed in the capital and 56 wounded, while the head of the surrounding Kyiv region said two additional people died there with nine wounded. Klitschko said damage was recorded in every district of Kyiv, noting that an attack on a school started a fire and another on a business center left people trapped in a shelter.

Ukraine's National Art Museum, housing one of the country's largest collections, was damaged in the blast. The culture ministry posted images showing damaged ceilings, broken windows, shattered glass and debris scattered throughout the building. Ukraine's foreign ministry was also damaged for the first time since World War II, according to Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha.

The intense barrage came after Russian President Vladimir Putin vowed revenge on Ukraine, accusing its forces of a deadly drone attack on a student dormitory in Luhansk, a Russian-controlled region in eastern Ukraine. Ukraine denied the accusations and said it had struck an elite drone command unit in the area.

French President Emmanuel Macron condemned the attacks, including the use of the Oreshnik missile, which he said signaled "the dead end of Russia's war of aggression." German Chancellor Friedrich Merz called the use of that weapon a "reckless escalation." EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas described the use of the Oreshnik as "political scare tactics and reckless nuclear brinkmanship."

Authorities in Orange County, California, have ordered tens of thousands of residents to evacuate after a storage tank containing highly flammable chemicals began threatening to explode or spill its contents.

The crisis began Thursday at a GKN Aerospace facility in Garden Grove, where a tank holding approximately 6,000 to 7,000 gallons of methyl methacrylate started off-gassing and showed signs of failure. The chemical is used in manufacturing resins and plastics.

Local hazmat teams initially responded Thursday, prompting evacuation orders for nearby residents. Officials lifted those orders later that day, but the situation deteriorated overnight. According to Garden Grove authorities, damage to a valve on the tank created additional operational challenges that prevented crews from fully resolving the problem.

By Friday, new evacuation orders had expanded to cover residents in six cities: Garden Grove, Cypress, Stanton, Anaheim, Buena Park, and Westminster. The orders affect approximately 40,000 people.

"We have a tank that is actively in crisis," Orange County Fire Authority Division Chief Craig Covey told reporters. "There are literally two options left remaining. One: the tank fails and spills a total of about 6,000 to 7,000 gallons of very bad chemicals into the parking lot in that area. Or two: the tank goes into a thermal runaway and blows up, affecting the tanks that are around them that have fuel or the chemicals in them as well."

Covey emphasized the urgency of the situation, stating, "This thing is going to fail, and we don't know when. We're doing our best to figure out when or how we can prevent it."

Emergency crews have constructed containment barriers using sandbags to prevent any chemical spill from entering storm drains or reaching nearby creeks and the ocean.

Dr. Regina Chinsio-Kwong, the county health officer, warned that if the chemical heats up, it can release vapors harmful to human health. Exposure can cause respiratory problems, burning and itching eyes, nausea, and headaches.

Garden Grove Police Chief Amir El-Farra reported that approximately 15 percent of residents under evacuation orders were refusing to leave their homes. The city has established at least two shelters for displaced residents.

Garden Grove is located about 30 miles southeast of downtown Los Angeles and less than a mile from Disneyland's two theme parks. The popular tourist destination was not under evacuation orders Friday.

Division Chief Covey urged residents to take the evacuation orders seriously. "We are setting up these evacuations in preparation for these two options: it fails or it blows up. Please follow our requests and orders for evacuations."

GKN Aerospace is a division of a British corporation that manufactures airplane engines and other aircraft components.

9:50 AM ET

Kevin Warsh officially took over as chair of the Federal Reserve on May 14, succeeding Jerome Powell in one of the most powerful economic positions in the US government. The transition comes at a challenging time for the central bank, which faces intense pressure from the Trump administration to lower interest rates even as inflation continues to rise.

The Senate confirmed Warsh in a historically divisive 54-45 vote in mid-May, with votes splitting almost entirely along party lines. Only one Democrat, Senator John Fetterman of Pennsylvania, crossed the aisle to support the nomination. The confirmation marked the most contentious Fed chair vote in history, surpassing even the 70-30 vote for Ben Bernanke in 2010.

Warsh brings extensive experience to the role, having previously served as a Fed governor from 2006 to 2011. During that period, he earned a reputation as an inflation hawk who favored higher interest rates to combat rising prices. He ultimately left the Fed board due to disagreements over the central bank's post-financial crisis stimulus policies. Before his government service, Warsh worked as a Wall Street banker and holds credentials as an Ivy League economist.

The new chair assumes control of monetary policy as inflation has climbed to 3.8 percent, complicating any potential rate cuts. The Fed's interest rate decisions directly affect the cost of borrowing money throughout the economy. Higher rates typically slow spending and cool prices but can increase unemployment, while lower rates can stimulate economic growth at the risk of accelerating inflation.

Democrats have expressed strong concerns about Warsh's independence from White House influence. Senator Elizabeth Warren, the top Democrat on the Senate banking committee, accused Trump of nominating Warsh to serve as his "sock puppet" on interest rates. Warren argued the appointment benefits Wall Street rather than working families. These concerns intensified when Warsh refused to answer questions about the 2020 election during his confirmation hearing.

Warsh defended his independence during his Senate banking committee appearance, pledging to "take politics out of monetary policy and monetary policy out of politics." Senator Fetterman cited this promise as his reason for supporting the nomination, saying he believes Warsh will maintain transparency and Fed independence.

The Trump administration celebrated the confirmation, with White House spokesperson Kush Desai calling it "a welcome step towards finally restoring accountability, competence and confidence in Fed decision-making."

In an unusual move, outgoing chair Jerome Powell has chosen to remain on the Fed's rate-setting board as a voting governor. Powell announced he would stay on until the White House ends its scrutiny of over-budget renovations at Fed headquarters, which he called a "pretext" for pressuring the central bank to lower rates.

The World Health Organization has declared the Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Uganda a "public health emergency of international concern" following 88 deaths and more than 300 suspected cases linked to the Bundibugyo virus strain.

Dr. Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, WHO director general, announced the decision on Sunday before convening a formal emergency committee. The speed of the declaration likely reflects the gravity of the situation, according to experts.

The outbreak involves the Bundibugyo virus, one of three strains that can cause Ebola virus disease and the least common. There are currently no approved vaccines, treatments, or specific tests for this strain. Ebola spreads through bodily fluids such as blood and vomit and has a high fatality rate, particularly in low-resource settings.

Dr. Jean Kaseya, director general of the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention, told Sky News he is in "panic mode because people are dying. I don't have medicines. I don't have [a] vaccine to support countries." Africa CDC announced the outbreak publicly on Friday but said it remains unclear when it began. The organization is convening experts and likely to announce a continental public health emergency in the coming days.

There have been 80 suspected deaths, eight laboratory-confirmed cases, and 246 suspected cases in the DRC's Ituri province, which borders Uganda and South Sudan. Uganda has reported two cases in its capital, Kampala, including one death, both in people who traveled from the DRC. An additional case was confirmed in Goma, another DRC city, involving the wife of a man who died of Ebola in Bunia.

The WHO warned that signs "all point towards a potentially much larger outbreak than what is currently being detected and reported, with significant local and regional risk of spread." Conflict continues in the Ituri region, with many people moving around and living in close quarters, factors that can increase transmission.

Dr. Kaseya said officials are in talks with companies that have potential tests, vaccines, and treatments at early development stages to see whether any could be safely used or trialed during the outbreak. He expressed hope that some would become available in the coming weeks.

The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention activated its emergency response center and is supporting the safe withdrawal of a small number of Americans directly affected by the outbreak. The agency plans to send more personnel to its offices in the DRC and Uganda.

In Bunia, residents described unusually high numbers of burials. "Every day, people are dying, and this has been going on for about a week. In a single day, we bury two, three or even more people," said Jean Marc Asimwe, a local resident. "At this point, we don't really know what kind of disease it is."
