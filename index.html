Tulsi Gabbard is leaving her post as US director of national intelligence following a tumultuous stint in which she was largely sidelined as Donald Trump launched attacks on Venezuela and Iran.

In a letter to the US president, she said she would resign and leave her post on 30 June. "While we have made significant progress … I recognize there is still important work to be done," she wrote.

The White House forced Gabbard to resign, the Reuters news agency reported, citing a source familiar with the issue. However, a spokesperson for Gabbard's office said rumors that Gabbard had been forcibly ousted from office were "100% false".

In her resignation letter, Gabbard said her husband, Abraham, "has recently been diagnosed with an extremely rare form of bone cancer". She added: "At this time, I must step away from public service to be by his side and fully support him through this battle."

Gabbard was an unconventional choice for the role. The former Democrat lacked an intelligence background and her foreign policy views at times diverged with Trump's, particularly on questions of overseas military intervention. She also took unusual measures to ingratiate herself to the president by vowing to root out politicization across US spy agencies and embracing his agenda of election denial.

Over time, Gabbard became increasingly sidelined by the president, who excluded her from key national security conversations on Iran and Venezuela, according to people familiar with the office of the director of national intelligence.

Trump was asking cabinet members last month whether he should replace Gabbard, according to two people briefed on the discussions.

"Unfortunately, after having done a great job, Tulsi Gabbard will be leaving the Administration on June 30th," he wrote in a statement on his Truth Social platform on Friday. Gabbard "has done an incredible job, and we will miss her", the president said, adding that Aaron Lukas, principal deputy director of national intelligence, would serve as acting director of national intelligence.

Gabbard's tumultuous 15-month tenure as the nation's top intelligence official ended Friday when she submitted her resignation. Over 15 months, the former Democrat without an intelligence background took actions seemingly meant to flatter Trump.

Her departure makes her the fourth woman to depart Trump's cabinet during this period. The resignation comes as Trump faces mounting pressure over Americans' financial wellbeing and continues military operations in the Middle East. Yesterday, 7:55 PM ET

The California Institute of Technology could lose management control of NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory for the first time in the institution's history. Caltech has operated JPL under contract with NASA since the laboratory's founding in the 1930s.

The potential change comes as NASA evaluates its relationship with the research institution. JPL, located in Pasadena, California, has been responsible for many of NASA's robotic space missions, including Mars rovers and deep space probes. The laboratory employs thousands of scientists and engineers.

Caltech's management of JPL has defined the relationship between the university and the space agency for generations. The laboratory was established as a rocket research facility before World War II and became part of NASA when the space agency was created in 1958. Throughout this period, Caltech has maintained operational control through a federally funded research and development center agreement.

The possible transition represents a significant shift in how one of America's premier space research facilities operates. JPL has produced landmark achievements in planetary science and space exploration under Caltech's stewardship. The laboratory has managed missions to every planet in the solar system and continues to operate active spacecraft exploring Mars and the outer reaches of the solar system.

NASA has not announced a final decision on the management structure. Any change would need to address how JPL's research programs, workforce, and ongoing missions would be affected. The laboratory's deep integration with Caltech's academic programs and research initiatives has been a defining feature of its operations.

The situation follows broader discussions within NASA about how it manages its network of research centers and contractor relationships. JPL's unique status as a university-managed facility has distinguished it from NASA's other field centers, which are operated directly by the space agency.

Caltech and NASA have not provided a timeline for any potential management changes. The outcome will determine whether the decades-long partnership continues or whether JPL transitions to a different operational model.
