A hazardous chemical leak at a facility in Garden Grove, California has forced the evacuation of thousands of residents, though officials said the immediate risk of a major explosion has diminished.

The incident began Thursday at a GKN Aerospace facility, where a storage tank containing methyl methacrylate began releasing vapors and showed signs of potential failure. Methyl methacrylate is a highly flammable chemical used in the production of resins and plastics. Local authorities initially ordered residents to evacuate but lifted the order the same day. However, the situation worsened when damage to a valve on the tank created additional operational challenges, forcing officials to issue new evacuation orders by Friday that expanded to residents in six cities.

At the peak of the crisis, approximately 40,000 people were ordered to evacuate from Garden Grove and nearby communities including Cypress, Stanton, Anaheim, Buena Park and Westminster. Orange County fire authority division chief Craig Covey described the gravity of the situation at a news conference, saying the tank faced two possible outcomes: it could fail and spill 6,000 to 7,000 gallons of toxic chemicals into the surrounding area, or it could experience thermal runaway and explode, potentially affecting nearby tanks containing fuel or other chemicals.

"This thing is going to fail, and we don't know when," Covey said. "We're doing our best to figure out when or how we can prevent it."

County health officer Dr Regina Chinsio-Kwong warned that if the chemical heats up sufficiently, it can release harmful vapors. Exposure can cause respiratory issues, eye irritation, nausea and headaches. Emergency crews established containment barriers using sandbags to prevent any chemical spill from reaching storm drains, creeks or the nearby ocean.

Despite the evacuation orders, some residents refused to leave. Garden Grove police chief Amir El-Farra reported that approximately 15 percent of people facing evacuation orders would not comply. The city established at least two emergency shelters for those who evacuated.

The situation stabilized following an overnight cooling and monitoring operation. Authorities announced that the explosion threat had been eliminated after the tank cracked over the weekend, which helped relieve internal pressure. A new evacuation zone was established, reducing the number of displaced residents to approximately 16,000 people who remained unable to return home.

Garden Grove is located about 30 miles southeast of downtown Los Angeles and less than a mile from Disneyland's theme parks, which were not placed under evacuation orders. GKN Aerospace is a division of a British corporation that manufactures airplane engines and aircraft components.

9:41 PM ET

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at 95. His death was announced on his website on Monday afternoon at his home in Woodstock, New York, with his publicist confirming the news. No cause of death was disclosed.

Rollins was a defining figure in jazz history, helping transform the genre from primarily dance and ballad music into something startlingly expressive. With more than 60 albums released from the late 1940s onward, he collaborated with Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane and numerous other jazz legends. He was among the last surviving stars of the bebop generation.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in Harlem in 1930, he earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began learning saxophone at seven years old, inspired by his siblings who played piano and violin, as well as jazz greats like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. His high school band included future stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew and Art Taylor.

Rollins became known as a genius of melody and improvisation. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting him with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, President Barack Obama said Rollins had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Early in his career, Rollins struggled with heroin addiction. In 1950, he committed armed robbery to fund his habit, later describing himself as "really a despicable character." He spent 10 months in jail on Rikers Island but successfully overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955. This period of recovery sparked remarkable creative output.

Rollins released landmark albums including Saxophone Colossus in 1956, featuring his signature tune St Thomas, which referenced his mother's Caribbean birthplace. Way Out West in 1957 explored a piano-free style, while Freedom Suite in 1958 featured a 20-minute title track that became an argument for freedom during the civil rights era.

Throughout his career, Rollins took sabbaticals to develop his craft. During a three-year break starting in 1959, he practiced up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge walkway, later inspiring his 1962 comeback album The Bridge. Another break between 1969 and 1971 saw him study yoga, philosophy and meditation at an Indian ashram.

In later decades, Rollins continued evolving his sound, incorporating funk, calypso and R&B influences. He also composed the soundtrack to the 1966 film Alfie and contributed uncredited saxophone to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album Tattoo You. He campaigned on climate issues through benefit concerts and his 1998 album Global Warming.

Rollins married twice, first to Dawn Finney in 1957 and then to Lucille Pearson in 1965, remaining together until her death in 2004.

The Trump administration has stopped deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo due to an ongoing Ebola outbreak in the country. Officials suspended the flights because of concerns that they could expose Immigration and Customs Enforcement personnel to the virus and potentially help transport the disease closer to United States territory.

The decision reflects the administration's assessment of health risks associated with deportation operations to regions experiencing infectious disease outbreaks. ICE officials handle deportees during flights and transfers, creating potential exposure points for the deadly virus.

The Democratic Republic of Congo has faced recurring Ebola outbreaks in recent years. The current outbreak prompted health restrictions severe enough that the country's national soccer team cancelled training in the capital city Kinshasa ahead of World Cup preparations. The team still plans to travel to the United States for other training activities despite the domestic health situation.

Public health experts have stated that neither Ebola nor other recent viral threats pose an immediate danger to most Americans. However, they have raised concerns about a growing trust deficit in the public health system that could become serious if a major health crisis arrives.

The suspension marks a departure from the administration's typically aggressive deportation posture. The pause appears to be temporary rather than a permanent policy change, tied specifically to the current disease outbreak. The administration has not announced when flights might resume or what conditions would need to be met for operations to restart.

Ebola is transmitted through direct contact with bodily fluids from infected individuals and has a high fatality rate. Previous outbreaks in West Africa killed thousands of people before being brought under control through international health interventions. The virus requires careful handling and isolation protocols to prevent spread.

The deportation pause comes as the administration faces other foreign policy challenges, including ongoing military operations in Iran and diplomatic efforts in the Middle East. Administration officials have not provided a timeline for reviewing the Congo flight suspension.

Google announced a redesign of its search interface, integrating artificial intelligence directly into the search box for the first time since the company's founding. The changes represent a shift in how users will interact with the search engine.

The updates were revealed at the company's I/O 2026 conference. Google is positioning the changes around what it calls the "agentic Gemini era," referring to its AI technology. Users will now encounter AI-generated responses as part of their search experience.

CEO Sundar Pichai addressed concerns about the technology during the announcement. He acknowledged public anxiety about artificial intelligence while defending the company's direction. The changes affect how information appears when users enter queries into the search box.

The redesign marks a departure from Google's traditional approach of displaying links to websites. The company is now emphasizing AI-generated summaries and responses within the search interface itself. This represents one of the most significant changes to the platform since its creation.

Industry observers have described the shift as a major turning point for internet usage. Time Magazine characterized it as heralding a fundamental change in how people use the internet. The Register published analysis suggesting Google is using web content to train and feed its AI systems.

The modifications come as artificial intelligence becomes increasingly integrated into consumer technology. Google's search engine processes billions of queries daily, making changes to its interface particularly significant for internet users worldwide.

CNN reported that AI is permanently altering the structure of the internet. Axios described Google's overhaul as marking the end of what it termed the internet's golden age, when traditional search results dominated the user experience.

The company has not specified whether the AI-integrated search will fully replace the previous interface or exist alongside it. The announcement follows growing competition in the AI space from other technology companies developing similar tools.

Google's decision to fundamentally alter its core product after 25 years signals the company's view that AI integration is necessary for its future. The search box redesign affects the primary tool that established Google as the dominant search engine.

Jazz Saxophone Legend Sonny Rollins Dies at 95

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at 95. His death was announced on his website on Monday afternoon at his home in Woodstock, New York, with his publicist confirming the news. No...

11:00 PM ET

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at 95. His death was announced on his website on Monday afternoon at his home in Woodstock, New York, with his publicist confirming the news. No cause of death was disclosed.

Rollins was a defining figure in jazz history, helping transform the genre from primarily dance and ballad music into something startlingly expressive. With more than 60 albums released from the late 1940s onward, he collaborated with Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane and numerous other jazz legends. He was among the last surviving stars of the bebop generation.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in Harlem in 1930, he earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began learning saxophone at seven years old, inspired by his siblings who played piano and violin, as well as jazz greats like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. His high school band included future stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew and Art Taylor.

Rollins became known as a genius of melody and improvisation. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting him with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, President Barack Obama said Rollins had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Early in his career, Rollins struggled with heroin addiction. In 1950, he committed armed robbery to fund his habit, later describing himself as "really a despicable character." He spent 10 months in jail on Rikers Island but successfully overcame his addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955. This period of recovery sparked remarkable creative output.

Rollins released landmark albums including Saxophone Colossus in 1956, featuring his signature tune St Thomas, which referenced his mother's Caribbean birthplace. Way Out West in 1957 explored a piano-free style, while Freedom Suite in 1958 featured a 20-minute title track that became an argument for freedom during the civil rights era.

Throughout his career, Rollins took sabbaticals to develop his craft. During a three-year break starting in 1959, he practiced up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge walkway, later inspiring his 1962 comeback album The Bridge. Another break between 1969 and 1971 saw him study yoga, philosophy and meditation at an Indian ashram.

In later decades, Rollins continued evolving his sound, incorporating funk, calypso and R&B influences. He also composed the soundtrack to the 1966 film Alfie and contributed uncredited saxophone to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album Tattoo You. He campaigned on climate issues through benefit concerts and his 1998 album Global Warming.

Rollins married twice, first to Dawn Finney in 1957 and then to Lucille Pearson in 1965, remaining together until her death in 2004.
