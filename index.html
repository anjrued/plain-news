Today, 11:00 PM ET

An Ebola outbreak in central Africa has spiraled beyond containment, with health authorities struggling to track cases and prevent further spread. The World Health Organization has confirmed more than 400 cases across multiple provinces, with the death toll rising daily as medical teams work to identify the source of the outbreak.

The current outbreak marks one of the most severe Ebola crises since the 2014-2016 West African epidemic that killed more than 11,000 people. Unlike previous outbreaks that were contained to rural areas, this one has reached urban centers where population density makes transmission control significantly more difficult.

Health officials are investigating how the outbreak began, with early evidence suggesting possible spillover from wildlife in forested regions. However, gaps in surveillance and delayed reporting allowed the virus to spread undetected for weeks before confirmation. The delay has complicated contact tracing efforts, a critical tool for stopping Ebola transmission.

Treatment centers are overwhelmed, and medical supply shortages have hampered response efforts. While vaccines exist and have proven effective in previous outbreaks, distribution challenges and community resistance in some areas have slowed immunization campaigns. Healthcare workers, who face the highest risk of infection, report inadequate protective equipment.

The outbreak has broader consequences beyond immediate health impacts. Trade restrictions and movement controls are disrupting local economies. Agricultural production has declined as workers flee affected areas. Schools have closed in several provinces, affecting hundreds of thousands of children.

International aid organizations have deployed emergency response teams, but funding remains insufficient for the scale of the crisis. The outbreak also strains health systems already weakened by previous disease outbreaks and chronic underfunding. Neighboring countries have increased border surveillance and implemented screening measures to prevent cross-border transmission.

Experts warn that without rapid intervention, the outbreak could match or exceed the scale of the 2014 crisis. The virus has a case fatality rate between 25% and 90% depending on the strain and quality of care. Current projections suggest cases could double within weeks if transmission continues at the present rate.

Today, 11:00 PM ET

Tennessee officials called off the execution of a death row inmate Thursday night after the execution team was unable to establish intravenous access needed to administer the lethal injection. The failure marks the latest in a series of botched execution attempts that have raised questions about execution protocols nationwide.

The inmate was returned to his cell after prison staff spent more than an hour attempting to place IV lines, according to officials at Riverbend Maximum Security Institution in Nashville. The state has not announced when it will attempt the execution again.

Tennessee uses a three-drug protocol for lethal injections. The procedure requires successful placement of two separate IV lines to ensure the drugs can be delivered. When execution teams cannot establish venous access, as happened Thursday, the execution cannot proceed.

The incident adds to a growing record of execution complications across the United States. Multiple states have experienced similar technical failures in recent years, including prolonged attempts to set IV lines and instances where executioners struggled to find viable veins. These incidents have intensified debate over lethal injection as a method of capital punishment.

Critics of the death penalty point to such failures as evidence that lethal injection, long promoted as a more humane alternative to other execution methods, remains unreliable. Medical professionals are generally barred from participating in executions due to ethical guidelines, which means the procedures are often carried out by personnel with limited medical training.

Tennessee has not publicly identified the inmate or disclosed whether health conditions may have contributed to the difficulty establishing IV access. State law shields most details about execution procedures and personnel from public disclosure.

The state resumed executions in 2018 after a nearly decade-long pause and has carried out several executions since then. Tennessee offers inmates a choice between lethal injection and the electric chair, though lethal injection remains the default method.

Governor and corrections officials have not commented on whether the failed attempt will prompt any review of execution protocols. The state is expected to announce a new execution date after internal assessment of Thursday's events.

Today, 11:00 PM ET

Google accidentally published details of an unpatched security vulnerability in Chromium, the open-source browser that powers Chrome, Edge, and other web browsers used by billions of people. The premature disclosure means attackers now have information about the flaw before users can protect themselves with a fix.

The vulnerability, tracked as CVE-2025-0983, affects the V8 JavaScript engine that runs code on web pages. Google's Threat Analysis Group discovered the flaw was being actively exploited in the wild before the company inadvertently made technical details public through its issue tracking system.

Security researchers noted that exploit code could be developed within days now that the vulnerability's specifics are public. The flaw allows attackers to escape Chrome's security sandbox, which normally isolates malicious code from the rest of a user's computer. This type of vulnerability is particularly valuable to attackers because it can be triggered simply by convincing someone to visit a malicious website.

Google has since restricted access to the technical details, but the information circulated widely enough that security experts consider it effectively public. The company is rushing to complete a patch and push it to users through Chrome's automatic update system.

Browser security flaws present massive risk because of the sheer number of people affected. Chrome alone has roughly 3 billion users worldwide. Microsoft Edge and other Chromium-based browsers share the same underlying code, multiplying the scope of vulnerable systems.

The incident highlights tensions in coordinated vulnerability disclosure. Security teams typically keep flaw details private until patches are ready and distributed, a process called responsible disclosure. Premature publication hands attackers a roadmap while defenders scramble.

Google has not publicly explained how the disclosure error occurred. The company's security team normally follows strict protocols for handling vulnerability information, especially for flaws already being exploited.

Users cannot manually fix the vulnerability. The only protection is installing the upcoming security update as soon as Google releases it. Chrome typically updates automatically, but users can manually check for updates through the browser's settings menu. Security experts recommend restarting the browser after any update to ensure the patch takes effect.

The situation underscores the complex challenge of securing software used by billions while maintaining transparency about security issues.

New Research Confirms Sea Level Rise Has Accelerated Since 1960

Scientists have refined measurements of global sea level rise and confirmed that the rate of increase has been accelerating for more than six decades. Today, 11:00 PM ET

Scientists have refined measurements of global sea level rise and confirmed that the rate of increase has been accelerating for more than six decades. The research provides the clearest picture yet of how quickly oceans are rising and affects coastal planning for hundreds of millions of people worldwide.

The study improves upon previous satellite and tide gauge data to create a more accurate timeline of sea level changes. Researchers found that while sea levels rose relatively slowly in the early 20th century, the rate began increasing around 1960 and has continued to accelerate since then.

The acceleration is driven primarily by two factors: thermal expansion of warming ocean water and increased melting of ice sheets in Greenland and Antarctica. As global temperatures have risen, both processes have intensified, adding more water to the oceans at a faster pace each year.

Current measurements show sea levels are rising by about 3.4 millimeters per year on average, but this rate is not constant. The acceleration means that future decades will see even faster increases unless greenhouse gas emissions are significantly reduced. Coastal regions already face more frequent flooding during high tides and storms.

The improved data will help governments and cities make more accurate projections for infrastructure planning. Sea walls, drainage systems, and building codes all depend on reliable forecasts of future water levels. Previous uncertainty in the measurements made long term planning more difficult.

Researchers combined multiple data sources to reduce errors and fill gaps in the historical record. Satellite altimetry, which began in the early 1990s, provides precise global measurements but only covers recent decades. Tide gauges offer longer records but are limited to coastal locations and can be affected by local land movements.

The study confirms that sea level rise is not a future threat but an ongoing process that has been building for generations. The acceleration pattern matches predictions from climate models, strengthening the link between human caused warming and rising seas. Scientists emphasize that the trend will continue for centuries even if emissions stop immediately, though the rate of increase can still be influenced by current policy decisions.

Today, 11:00 PM ET

Kyle Busch, one of NASCAR's most successful drivers, has died unexpectedly, Motorsport.com reports. The racing community is mourning the loss of the two-time Cup Series champion.

Busch won 63 Cup Series races over his career, ranking him ninth on the all-time wins list. He secured championships in 2015 and 2019 driving for Joe Gibbs Racing before moving to Richard Childress Racing in 2023.

Known for his aggressive driving style and fierce competitiveness, Busch also accumulated 102 wins in the Xfinity Series and 66 in the Truck Series, making him the winningest driver across NASCAR's three national series combined with 231 total victories.

The 38-year-old Las Vegas native began his Cup Series career in 2004 and quickly established himself as one of the sport's dominant forces. He won at least one race in 19 consecutive seasons from 2005 to 2023, a streak matched only by Richard Petty in NASCAR history.

Busch leaves behind his wife Samantha and their two children. The family has not released details about the cause of death. NASCAR has not yet issued an official statement.

Tributes have begun pouring in from across the motorsports world as drivers, team owners, and fans process the sudden loss. Busch's impact extended beyond his win total, as he was known for mentoring younger drivers and maintaining one of the sport's most active schedules, often competing in multiple series on the same weekend.

The death represents a significant loss for NASCAR at a time when the sport has been working to maintain its prominence in American motorsports. Busch remained an active competitor and was seen as a bridge between NASCAR's older generation of stars and its emerging talent.

Funeral arrangements have not been announced. NASCAR is expected to hold a moment of silence at its next scheduled race.
