Iranian state-linked media claimed the strikes on Dimona were carried out in response to alleged attacks by the US and Israel on nuclear facilities in Bushehr and Natanz. Yesterday, 11:24 PM ET

Approximately 16,000 residents near Garden Grove in Orange County have been allowed to return to their homes after firefighters eliminated the threat of a catastrophic explosion at an overheating chemical tank at GKN Aerospace, an aerospace parts manufacturer located about 5 miles from Disneyland.

The crisis began Thursday afternoon when a leak was discovered at the facility. The tank contained between 6,000 and 7,000 gallons of methyl methacrylate, a highly flammable chemical used to produce plastic and resin. EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin warned that the tank "will fail," with the most catastrophic scenario involving an explosion that could trigger additional tank explosions in the surrounding area. This possibility prompted officials to evacuate approximately 50,000 people initially.

Firefighters worked to prevent an explosion by spraying water on the tank to keep the chemical temperature below 85 degrees Fahrenheit. On Friday, officials added a neutralizing agent to a nearby tank to further reduce explosion risk.

A significant breakthrough came late Saturday night when firefighters discovered a crack in the tank. According to Orange County Fire Chief TJ McGovern, this crack could relieve pressure inside the tank, making an explosion less likely. Andrew Whelton, an engineering professor at Purdue University, explained the principle: "Think of a soda can. If you leave it in a hot car it can explode. But if you put a hole in the can, the product is released and the can itself doesn't explode."

However, some experts urged caution. Faisal Khan, head of the chemical engineering department at Texas A&M University, told news agencies that while cooling was occurring on the tank's surface, a runaway reaction might continue deep inside. "Yes, reaction is slowed compared to what it started. However, we are not out of explosive release risk," he said.

By Monday, firefighters declared the explosion threat eliminated through overnight cooling and monitoring operations. A reduced evacuation zone was established with approximately 16,000 residents still unable to return home initially.

Officials monitoring air quality detected no pollutants in the surrounding area. "We are happy to report that levels are completely normal in our measurements," said Jason Low from the South Coast Air Quality Management District.

Exposure to methyl methacrylate can cause serious respiratory problems, render someone unconscious, and cause neurological damage and skin and eye irritation. However, Orange County health officials noted the chemical is easy to smell and residents may notice it over a wide area without being harmed.

At least two Garden Grove law firms filed lawsuits against GKN Aerospace on behalf of residents, citing evacuation orders, exposure concerns, chemical odors, and property damage.

Disneyland officials, whose parks were not under evacuation orders, said they were monitoring the incident and supporting affected employees.

Yesterday, 7:57 AM ET

Google announced sweeping changes to its search interface, marking the most significant redesign in the platform's nearly 30-year history. The company revealed the overhaul at its I/O developer conference, introducing artificial intelligence as the centerpiece of how billions of users will search the web.

The traditional search box, long synonymous with internet search itself, is being replaced with an AI-powered system that handles longer, more conversational queries. Rather than requiring the specific syntax that search engines have demanded for decades, users can now ask questions the way they would speak to another person.

Elizabeth Reid, who oversees Google Search, called the changes fundamental. "Google Search is AI search," she said, noting that the redesign represents the biggest shift the product has experienced since its inception. The updates rolled out globally to both desktop and mobile versions on Tuesday.

The new search results will look markedly different from the traditional list of links. Instead, users will see visually enhanced responses that resemble expanded versions of AI Overviews, the summarized responses Google Search currently provides. Users can still access the original link-based search format by clicking a "Web" tab.

The changes are powered by Gemini 3.5, Google's latest artificial intelligence model, which can make longer text predictions based on what users type. Google's search queries recently hit an all-time high, and the company reports that queries to its AI Mode chatbot interface have doubled every quarter since its debut a year ago.

For paying subscribers, Google is introducing "information agents" that can conduct in-depth research and deliver summaries or action plans. Gemini Spark, a new agent feature, will integrate information from Gmail, Google Calendar and other Google products to help users research topics, shop and plan trips and appointments.

Google is also rolling out a generative user interface that creates customized visuals and interactive elements like dashboards based on user requests. Josh Woodward, who oversees Gemini's development, described the goal as creating "a universal assistant that's personal, proactive, persistent."

The company disclosed that 900 million people use the Gemini app monthly, a figure that remains below ChatGPT's 900 million weekly users.

Alongside the search redesign, Google unveiled new smart eyeglasses developed with Samsung, Warby Parker and Gentle Monster. The voice-activated glasses, with embedded cameras, will respond to Gemini commands and launch this fall. The move represents Google's second attempt at optical computing after Google Glass, which launched in 2014 for $1,500 and was discontinued the following year after widespread criticism.

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his website Monday and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided.

Rollins was a towering figure in jazz history who helped transform the genre from primarily dance and ballad music into something startlingly expressive. Known as "the Saxophone Colossus," he was among the last surviving stars of the bebop generation and left behind more than 60 albums recorded from the late 1940s onward.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. Raised in Harlem, he was inspired by his siblings' musical talents and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. He began learning saxophone at age seven and quickly became immersed in his neighborhood's vibrant jazz scene. Early bands included future stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew and Art Taylor.

Rollins became celebrated for his melodic genius and improvisational brilliance. His bright, catchy lines would be unpicked, extended and refashioned in sometimes epic solos. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When President Barack Obama presented him the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, Obama said Rollins had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Rollins's career included landmark albums like "Saxophone Colossus" in 1956, featuring his signature tune "St Thomas," and "Freedom Suite" in 1958, where an extended title composition became an argument for freedom during the civil rights movement. He collaborated with Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane, Dizzy Gillespie and many others.

His personal life included struggles with heroin addiction that led to his imprisonment on Rikers Island in 1950. He overcame the addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955, after which he experienced a burst of creative productivity.

Rollins took periodic breaks from performing to advance his craft. Most famously, he practiced for up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway from 1959 to 1962, partly to avoid disturbing neighbors. This period inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge." He took another sabbatical from 1969 to 1971 to study yoga, philosophy and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout his later decades, Rollins continued pushing musical boundaries, incorporating funk, calypso and R&B influences. He also composed music for film and campaigned on climate issues, releasing the album "Global Warming" in 1998.

Rollins married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004.

The Trump administration has temporarily halted deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo as the country battles a widening Ebola outbreak, according to reporting by Politico. Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials suspended the flights due to concerns that personnel could be exposed to the virus during operations.

However, the pause will not affect detainees already sent to the DRC. At least one woman remains in limbo after officials transferred her to Kinshasa, the capital, and now refuse to bring her back despite a judge's order for her return. The administration's decision to maintain the deportation ban while keeping already-deported individuals in place raises questions about the consistency of its health precautions.

Public health experts say the pause won't help prevent the spread of disease. The Ebola virus spreads through contact with bodily fluids from infected individuals and can cause severe illness with high mortality rates without prompt treatment. Previous outbreaks in the Democratic Republic of Congo have been contained through coordinated international public health responses, including vaccination campaigns and contact tracing.

The move comes as the Trump administration faces increased scrutiny over its expanded deportation operations. A group of 40 House Democrats recently described "grave concerns" about the administration's secretive deportation flight program and demanded the Federal Aviation Administration address allegations of mistreatment and inhumane conditions on ICE charter jets.

In a letter to FAA administrator Bryan Bedford, the lawmakers expressed urgent concerns about transparency. "Credible reports indicate that individuals have been placed on flights without notice to counsel or family members, effectively disappearing from public view when flights are inappropriately shielded from tracking systems," the letter states.

The Democrats noted that the Trump administration transported detained immigrants in ways that routinely violated their constitutional rights, according to an investigation by the Guardian based on leaked flight data. The reporting identified allegations of abuse and rights violations at a private detention center in Alexandria, Louisiana, which operates as a central hub in the deportation program.

Monitoring by human rights groups tracked an 84 percent increase in ICE flights from 2024 to 2025. "Information regarding these flights is nearly impossible to find, which undermines congressional oversight and prevents the public from understanding the scope and conditions of these flights," the lawmakers wrote.

The Trump administration has previously dismissed claims of hidden flights as "categorically false" and argued its detention centers have "higher standards than most US prisons."

The DRC's World Cup team announced plans to continue its trip to the United States for pre-tournament training despite the health situation in their home country. The team cancelled training scheduled in Kinshasa due to health restrictions but maintained its scheduled preparation in the US.

ICE has not announced when flights might resume or what conditions would need to be met for operations to restart.

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at age 95. His death was announced on his website Monday and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided.

Rollins was a towering figure in jazz history who helped transform the genre from primarily dance and ballad music into something startlingly expressive. Known as "the Saxophone Colossus," he was among the last surviving stars of the bebop generation and left behind more than 60 albums recorded from the late 1940s onward.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. Raised in Harlem, he was inspired by his siblings' musical talents and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. He began learning saxophone at age seven and quickly became immersed in his neighborhood's vibrant jazz scene. Early bands included future stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew and Art Taylor.

Rollins became celebrated for his melodic genius and improvisational brilliance. His bright, catchy lines would be unpicked, extended and refashioned in sometimes epic solos. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When President Barack Obama presented him the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, Obama said Rollins had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Rollins's career included landmark albums like "Saxophone Colossus" in 1956, featuring his signature tune "St Thomas," and "Freedom Suite" in 1958, where an extended title composition became an argument for freedom during the civil rights movement. He collaborated with Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, John Coltrane, Dizzy Gillespie and many others.

His personal life included struggles with heroin addiction that led to his imprisonment on Rikers Island in 1950. He overcame the addiction through a rehabilitation program in 1955, after which he experienced a burst of creative productivity.

Rollins took periodic breaks from performing to advance his craft. Most famously, he practiced for up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway from 1959 to 1962, partly to avoid disturbing neighbors. This period inspired his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge." He took another sabbatical from 1969 to 1971 to study yoga, philosophy and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout his later decades, Rollins continued pushing musical boundaries, incorporating funk, calypso and R&B influences. He also composed music for film and campaigned on climate issues, releasing the album "Global Warming" in 1998.

Rollins married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004.
