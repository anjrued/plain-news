A few minutes ago

A federal court panel ruled on Tuesday that Alabama cannot use a congressional redistricting map in the 2026 midterm elections because it was intentionally drawn to discriminate against Black voters. The decision marks a significant setback for Republican efforts to reshape district boundaries in the state.

The three-judge panel blocked Alabama from implementing a map that state lawmakers passed in 2023. The court had previously found that the original map was created with the explicit intent to discriminate based on race. Alabama was then ordered to adopt a map containing two majority-Black districts that both elected Democratic candidates.

The timing of the ruling is notable because it comes after the U.S. Supreme Court weakened a major provision of the Voting Rights Act in April. In response to that decision, Alabama took the unusual step of moving up its congressional primary elections and attempted to use the 2023 map for the upcoming midterms.

"We cannot see our way clear to requiring Alabamians to cast their votes in the 2026 elections under a districting plan tainted by intentional race-based discrimination," the court wrote in its opinion.

The court's decision is particularly significant because judges determined that even after the Supreme Court's action on voting rights protections, Alabama was not permitted to use the discriminatory map. This ruling suggests that federal courts maintain authority to block redistricting plans with evidence of intentional racial discrimination, despite recent changes to voting rights law.

Alabama is expected to appeal the decision to the U.S. Supreme Court, setting up another potential conflict over voting rights and redistricting at the highest level of the judiciary. The state now faces pressure to develop a compliant redistricting plan before the elections.

The case reflects broader national tensions over how electoral districts should be drawn. Similar legal challenges have emerged in multiple states where courts have questioned whether redistricting plans unfairly minimize the voting power of minority communities. The outcome in Alabama may have implications for how other states approach redistricting following recent Supreme Court decisions.

The blocked map would have benefited Republican candidates by organizing districts in ways favorable to GOP representation. The court's intervention ensures that Alabama's congressional landscape will be reshaped to provide fairer representation for Black voters.

The ruling means that candidates and political organizations must await the redrawn district boundaries before finalizing campaign strategies for the 2026 midterms. The decision underscores continued judicial scrutiny of redistricting efforts that courts determine are motivated by racial considerations or designed to dilute minority voting power.

Iran and the United States exchanged military fire late Thursday in the most serious test of their month-long ceasefire. The incident involved strikes on targets near the Strait of Hormuz and raised immediate questions about the viability of the fragile agreement.

The U.S. military said it targeted Iranian military facilities after what it called unprovoked attacks on three American destroyers: the USS Truxtun, USS Rafael Peralta, and USS Mason. According to U.S. Central Command, Iranian forces launched multiple missiles, drones, and small boats at the vessels, though no American assets were struck.

Iran's military rejected the U.S. account. A military spokesperson accused the U.S. of breaking the ceasefire by targeting an Iranian oil tanker and another ship in the strait. The spokesperson also claimed the U.S. conducted airstrikes on civilian areas along the Iranian coast, including the populated island of Qeshm, which is home to about 150,000 people and a water desalination plant. Iran said its armed forces responded by attacking U.S. military vessels.

President Trump downplayed the confrontation, describing the American strikes as a "love tap" in an interview with ABC News. When asked about the situation during an evening visit to the Reflecting Pool in Washington, Trump said, "They trifled with us today. We blew them away." He praised the destroyer crews for transiting out of the waterway while under fire and insisted the ceasefire remained intact.

Despite the military escalation, Trump expressed optimism about ongoing negotiations. He said a deal "might not happen, but it could happen any day" and claimed that Iran wanted the agreement more than he did.

The timing of the strikes complicated diplomatic efforts. Before the exchanges of fire, reports indicated the U.S. and Iran may have been close to a temporary agreement, with a one-page memorandum being shared between Washington and Tehran through Pakistan. Senior Iranian officials have recently rejected concessions, with some favoring extended negotiations closer to the U.S. midterm elections.

Regional diplomats suggested Iran could overplay its hand by delaying talks. The current moment presents an opportunity to end the conflict and claim victory, they noted, while resuming all-out fighting could make future negotiations more difficult.

The incident also affected oil markets. Brent crude oil rose to trade around 101 dollars per barrel following news of the attacks.

U.S. Central Command stated it does not seek escalation but remains positioned to protect American forces. The fresh skirmishes highlighted the fragility of the ceasefire even as both sides continue diplomatic discussions to resolve the underlying conflict.

BP has removed its chairman Albert Manifold with immediate effect, citing serious concerns about governance standards, oversight, and conduct. The FTSE 100 company announced the decision on Tuesday without disclosing specific details about the issues involved.

Manifold held the role for only eight months after joining BP's board in October 2025. His removal triggered a sharp market reaction, with BP's share price falling 6 percent in the hours following the announcement, making it the FTSE's biggest faller on the day.

Amanda Blanc, BP's senior independent director and chief executive of insurance company Aviva, said in a statement: "Albert has helped bring a welcome focus and pace to BP's transformation. However, the board has been surprised and disappointed to learn of governance oversight and conduct issues it deems unacceptable and has taken decisive action."

Reports suggest that bullying and overbearing behavior may have contributed to the decision, though BP declined to confirm these specifics.

Manifold was brought in to oversee a significant shift in BP's strategy, directing the company away from renewable energy investments and back toward fossil fuel extraction. During his brief tenure, he moved quickly to remove chief executive Murray Auchincloss after less than two years in the role and appointed Meg O'Neill as his replacement in December.

This marks the second high-profile departure at BP for conduct reasons in three years. In September 2023, former chief executive Bernard Looney was forced out after failing to disclose sexual relationships with colleagues when appointed to the role. The board subsequently denied Looney 32 million pounds in pay and shares, determining it had been misled about the circumstances of his appointment.

BP has appointed Ian Tyler as interim chairman with immediate effect. Tyler previously served as chief executive of infrastructure company Balfour Beatty and currently holds positions as chair of building supplies company Grafton Group and director at mining company Anglo American.

The chairman position, distinct from the chief executive role, carries responsibility for board oversight and corporate governance matters. Manifold's sudden departure raises questions about what specifically prompted the board's action, particularly given his mandate to reshape the company's strategic direction.

The removal comes as BP navigates broader challenges in the energy sector, including market volatility and global supply pressures. The company operates across multiple jurisdictions and must comply with various corporate governance codes and regulations in each market where it operates.

The lack of detailed disclosure about the specific conduct issues has left investors and industry observers seeking additional information about what led to the board's swift decision to remove its chairman after such a short tenure.

Yesterday, 3:00 AM ET

Google has fundamentally redesigned its search box for the first time in nearly 30 years, integrating artificial intelligence directly into the core search experience. The changes, announced at Google I/O, represent the company's most significant update to the search product since its inception in 1998.

The new interface allows users to ask longer, more conversational questions rather than the keyword-focused syntax that has traditionally defined Google Search. According to Elizabeth Reid, who oversees the product, these represent "the biggest Google Search had seen in its nearly 30-year history."

The redesigned search now returns results that incorporate AI-generated summaries and visual elements alongside traditional web links. Users engage directly with Google's Gemini chatbot as part of the standard search experience, rather than accessing it as a separate tool. The visual presentation more closely resembles augmented versions of Google's existing AI Overviews feature, which provides bulleted summary responses, than a simple list of links.

The underlying technology powering these changes is Gemini 3.5, Google's latest artificial intelligence model announced simultaneously with the search redesign. Gemini can generate longer text predictions based on user input, enabling more sophisticated interactions within Google's ecosystem. When users add photos, videos, or documents to Chrome's search bar, the system automatically enters AI mode.

Google maintains that users can still access the original search experience by clicking a "Web" tab, allowing them to view traditional link-based results. However, the AI-powered interface now serves as the default search experience across desktop and mobile versions globally.

The expansion reflects impressive user adoption of AI search features. Since Google introduced "AI Mode" one year ago, queries to its chatbot-powered interface have doubled every quarter. Google Search reached an all-time high in queries last month, according to Reid.

For subscribers to Google's AI Pro and Ultra plans, the company is introducing additional features including "information agents" that autonomously conduct research and return summaries or action plans. The "generative user interface" can create customized visuals and interactive dashboards based on user requests. A new agent feature called Gemini Spark can access information from Gmail, Google Calendar, and other Google products to handle research, shopping, and trip planning.

Google also announced that 900 million people use the Gemini app monthly, though this remains below ChatGPT's 900 million weekly users.

The redesign positions Google as a direct competitor to OpenAI in the consumer AI market. Industry observers have characterized the shift as a major change in how people access information online, marking a transition from link-based search to AI-powered information synthesis.

Sonny Rollins, widely considered one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at age 95. His death was announced Monday on his official website and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided.

Rollins was born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930 and grew up in Harlem. He earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. Inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller, he began playing saxophone at age seven. His early high school band included future stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew and Art Taylor.

Over more than seven decades, Rollins released more than 60 albums and collaborated with jazz's most celebrated figures, including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk and John Coltrane. He became known for his melodic genius and innovative approach to improvisation. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting Rollins with the 2010 National Medal of the Arts in 2011, President Barack Obama said the musician had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Rollins' career produced several landmark recordings. His 1956 album Saxophone Colossus featured his signature tune St Thomas, a nod to calypso and his mother's Caribbean roots. Way Out West in 1957 explored a piano-free "strolling" style, while Freedom Suite in 1958 showcased his emancipated compositions during the civil rights movement.

In 1959, Rollins stepped away from recording and performing for three years, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway in New York. This intensive period inspired his 1962 comeback album The Bridge. He took another sabbatical between 1969 and 1971 to study yoga, philosophy and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout the 1960s and beyond, Rollins continued evolving his style, incorporating elements of avant-garde jazz, Latin American music, funk and calypso. He composed music for the 1966 film Alfie and later contributed uncredited soloing to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album Tattoo You.

Beyond music, Rollins was an environmental advocate, campaigning around climate change through benefit concerts and his 1998 album Global Warming.

Rollins was married twice. His first marriage to Dawn Finney in 1957 was brief. He met his second wife, Lucille Pearson, that year and married her in 1965. They remained together until her death in 2004.

Officials halted the flights due to concerns that they could expose Immigration and...

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily suspended deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo amid an expanding Ebola outbreak, according to reporting by Politico. The pause affects immigration enforcement operations that had been removing individuals to the central African nation.

Officials halted the flights due to concerns that they could expose Immigration and Customs Enforcement personnel to the virus. The decision also reflected worries that deportation operations could potentially contribute to spreading the disease closer to the United States.

However, public health experts have stated that the move will not actually help prevent the disease from spreading, according to Guardian reporting on the pause.

The temporary suspension has created complications for at least one woman who was moved to Kinshasa, the capital of the DRC. Officials now say they will not bring her back because of the Ebola travel restrictions, despite a judge's order for her return, the Guardian reported.

The decision represents an intervention in standard immigration enforcement procedures. ICE officials involved in deportation flights would face potential exposure risks when delivering individuals to a country experiencing an active Ebola outbreak. The administration has not announced similar pauses for removal operations to other countries, and officials have not specified what conditions would need to be met for deportation flights to resume.

The Ebola outbreak in Congo has disrupted other activities in the country. The DRC's World Cup team cancelled the Kinshasa leg of its pre-World Cup training due to health restrictions, though the team is still planning its US trip.

The pause comes as the Trump administration faces increased scrutiny over its expanded deportation operations. A group of 40 House Democrats recently sent a letter to the Federal Aviation Administration expressing "grave concerns" about secretive deportation flights and demanding transparency. The lawmakers cited credible reports indicating that individuals have been placed on flights without notice to counsel or family members, raising serious due process concerns.

The Democrats' letter referenced an investigation by the Guardian based on leaked flight data, which revealed the Trump administration transported detained immigrants in ways that routinely violated their constitutional rights. The reporting also identified allegations of abuse and rights violations at a private detention center in Alexandria, Louisiana, a central node in the administration's deportation program.

Human rights groups tracking ICE flights identified an 84 percent increase in the number of flights during 2025 compared to 2024. Information regarding these flights is difficult to find, the lawmakers stated, which undermines congressional oversight and prevents the public from understanding the scope and conditions of these operations.

The Trump administration has previously defended its detention centers as having "higher standards than most US prisons" and described claims of "hidden" or "weaponized" transfer flights as "categorically false."

Sonny Rollins, widely considered one of the greatest jazz saxophonists of all time, has died at age 95. His death was announced Monday on his official website and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided.

Rollins was born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930 and grew up in Harlem. He earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. Inspired by his musically talented siblings and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller, he began playing saxophone at age seven. His early high school band included future stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew and Art Taylor.

Over more than seven decades, Rollins released more than 60 albums and collaborated with jazz's most celebrated figures, including Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk and John Coltrane. He became known for his melodic genius and innovative approach to improvisation. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting Rollins with the 2010 National Medal of the Arts in 2011, President Barack Obama said the musician had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Rollins' career produced several landmark recordings. His 1956 album Saxophone Colossus featured his signature tune St Thomas, a nod to calypso and his mother's Caribbean roots. Way Out West in 1957 explored a piano-free "strolling" style, while Freedom Suite in 1958 showcased his emancipated compositions during the civil rights movement.

In 1959, Rollins stepped away from recording and performing for three years, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway in New York. This intensive period inspired his 1962 comeback album The Bridge. He took another sabbatical between 1969 and 1971 to study yoga, philosophy and meditation at an Indian ashram.

Throughout the 1960s and beyond, Rollins continued evolving his style, incorporating elements of avant-garde jazz, Latin American music, funk and calypso. He composed music for the 1966 film Alfie and later contributed uncredited soloing to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album Tattoo You.

Beyond music, Rollins was an environmental advocate, campaigning around climate change through benefit concerts and his 1998 album Global Warming.

Rollins was married twice. His first marriage to Dawn Finney in 1957 was brief. He met his second wife, Lucille Pearson, that year and married her in 1965. They remained together until her death in 2004.
