7:19 AM ET

At least 14 people died and dozens were injured when a bomb exploded on railway tracks in southwestern Pakistan, derailing a passenger train carrying military personnel home for the Eid holiday. The attack occurred in Balochistan province, where armed separatist groups have targeted infrastructure for decades.

The explosion struck the Jaffar Express as it traveled through the region, causing several carriages to leave the tracks. Emergency responders transported the wounded to nearby hospitals, though the full extent of casualties remained under assessment as rescue operations continued.

Balochistan has experienced recurring attacks on railways, roads, and energy installations. Separatist groups in the province have conducted a long-running campaign against what they describe as exploitation of the region's natural resources. The groups have previously targeted Chinese workers and projects connected to the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor.

Armed separatists are suspected of carrying out the attack, according to officials. Pakistani security forces have increased patrols along railway lines following previous attacks, but the remote terrain and length of the rail network make comprehensive protection difficult.

The Jaffar Express operates between Rawalpindi in Punjab province and Quetta, the capital of Balochistan. The route passes through areas where militant activity has posed persistent security challenges. Previous attacks on the same line have killed passengers and disrupted service for extended periods.

Authorities suspended train service on the affected section of track while investigators examined the blast site and crews worked to clear debris. Railway officials said repairs to the damaged infrastructure would take several days. Passengers planning to use the route were advised to seek alternative transportation.

The attack represents the latest in a series of violent incidents in Balochistan, where both separatist insurgents and sectarian groups operate. The province, despite its vast mineral wealth and strategic location along the Arabian Sea, remains Pakistan's poorest and least developed region.

The targeting of the train carrying military personnel heading home for Eid highlights the vulnerability of transport networks in the region. Security analysts have long warned that Pakistan's railway infrastructure remains exposed to militant threats despite increased military presence in areas known for separatist activity.

Officials have not yet provided detailed statements about security measures or plans to prevent similar attacks in the future. The incident adds to growing concerns about the safety of transportation networks in volatile parts of Pakistan, particularly those passing through provinces where organized militant groups maintain a presence.

Authorities in Orange County, California, have ordered the evacuation of around 40,000 people due to concerns about a chemical tank that threatens to either leak or explode. The crisis began on Thursday at a GKN Aerospace facility in Garden Grove, where a storage tank containing methyl methacrylate started releasing vapors and showed signs of failure.

Methyl methacrylate is a highly flammable chemical used in the production of resins and plastics. A hazmat team initially responded to the incident on Thursday, and evacuation orders were lifted later that day. However, the situation deteriorated when damage to a valve on the tank created additional problems, preventing workers from fully addressing the crisis.

By Friday, authorities expanded evacuation orders to residents across six cities in the region, including Cypress, Stanton, Anaheim, Buena Park, and Westminster, in addition to Garden Grove.

"We have a tank that is actively in crisis," said Craig Covey, division chief of the Orange County Fire Authority. "There are literally two options left remaining. One: the tank fails and spills a total of about 6,7,000 gallons of very bad chemicals into the parking lot in that area. Or two: the tank goes into a thermal runaway and blows up, affecting the tanks that are around them that have fuel or the chemicals in them as well."

Covey emphasized the unpredictability of the situation, stating "This thing is going to fail, and we don't know when. We're doing our best to figure out when or how we can prevent it."

To prepare for a potential chemical spill, crews constructed containment barriers using sandbags to prevent the toxic substance from entering storm drains or reaching nearby waterways and the ocean.

Dr. Regina Chinsio-Kwong, the county health officer, warned that if the chemical heats up, it releases a vapor that poses health risks. Exposure can cause respiratory problems, eye irritation, nausea, and headaches.

Garden Grove, located approximately 30 miles southeast of downtown Los Angeles, sits less than a mile from Disneyland's two theme parks, which were not placed under evacuation orders. The city established at least two shelters to accommodate evacuees.

Not all residents complied with the evacuation orders. According to the Orange County Register, about 15 percent of people facing evacuation orders were refusing to leave their homes.

Covey urged residents to take the situation seriously. "We are setting up these evacuations in preparation for these two options: it fails or it blows up. Please follow our requests and orders for evacuations."

GKN Aerospace, a division of a British corporation that manufactures airplane engines and aircraft components, has not yet issued a public statement regarding the incident.

US Treasury bonds have experienced a sharp selloff, testing the government's ability to manage rising borrowing costs as investors demand greater returns on government debt. The decline in bond prices has pushed yields on benchmark bonds to levels not seen in over a decade, according to market data.

The selloff directly affects the interest rates the federal government pays to borrow money. Higher yields mean the Treasury must pay more to finance government operations and service the roughly $28 trillion national debt. Each percentage point increase in yields translates to billions of dollars in additional annual interest costs. The movement in bond markets comes as the government faces ongoing debates about spending levels and debt ceiling negotiations.

The bond market turbulence extends beyond Washington's fiscal challenges. Mortgage rates typically track Treasury yields, meaning homebuyers face higher borrowing costs. Corporate bonds and other debt instruments also price off Treasury rates, potentially making it more expensive for companies to borrow and invest. Consumer spending may suffer as well, with higher petrol prices already prompting shoppers to reduce spending according to retail data.

Some economists view the selloff as a market reassessment of inflation risks and future Federal Reserve policy. Others point to technical factors, including large institutional investors adjusting portfolio allocations. The Treasury market is considered one of the world's most liquid and important financial markets, with daily trading volumes in the hundreds of billions of dollars.

International observers have weighed in on the implications of higher borrowing costs. The International Monetary Fund has urged policymakers to continue reducing budget deficits given current market pressures. Sustained higher yields could force policy discussions about spending priorities and revenue measures.

Washington policymakers from both parties have expressed concern about the national debt in recent years, though they disagree on solutions. Some economists suggest difficult choices lie ahead. Long-term fiscal pressures from aging populations, defense spending, and climate transition investments may require spending reforms or revenue adjustments beyond recent tax increases.

The bond market dynamics underscore the interconnected nature of financial markets. When oil prices dropped recently, for example, government bond yields fell slightly as investors reassessed inflation risks. This volatility demonstrates how multiple factors influence borrowing costs across the economy.

Treasury officials have not commented publicly on the recent market movements. The department continues its regular schedule of bond auctions to fund government operations. Market analysts note that the current environment adds urgency to fiscal debates in Washington, as higher debt service costs will compete with other spending priorities in future budgets.

Turkish police stormed the main opposition Republican People's Party (CHP) headquarters and forced out party members in the latest escalation of a political crisis gripping the country. The operation came days after a court ruling removed the party's leader and reinstalled a predecessor more amenable to the government.

An appeals court in Ankara had ordered the removal of CHP leader Özgür Özel on Thursday, annulling a 2023 leadership contest. The 51-year-old had become the public face of Turkey's opposition and was credited with revitalizing the party. He was replaced by his predecessor, Kemal Kılıçdaroğlu, who lost a general election to President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan in 2023.

The court ruling was widely criticized as an attempt to weaken the CHP and install a more compliant leader. The decision sent shockwaves through Turkey's economy, with stock market trading briefly suspended in Istanbul following a 6 percent drop in share prices.

The party's subsequent use of its headquarters to defy the ruling prompted the police action. Officers forced their way into the building and physically removed occupants, marking a significant escalation in tensions between the government and opposition forces.

The crisis reflects a broader crackdown on Erdoğan's political opponents since the 2024 local elections, where the CHP won control of municipalities and mayoralties across the country. More than 20 CHP mayors have been detained on corruption, bribery, and terrorism-related charges in the months that followed.

Among those arrested was Istanbul Mayor Ekrem İmamoğlu, seen as a potential CHP presidential candidate. He has spent the past year in a maximum-security facility and is currently on trial as one of 400 defendants accused of involvement in a sprawling corruption scheme. Human Rights Watch called the trial part of a systematic effort to weaponize the criminal justice system against the CHP.

In recent weeks, tensions escalated between Özel and Erdoğan. A court ordered Özel to pay the president 300,000 lira in damages for calling him an "oppressor" and telling him to "leash your dogs" in criticism of the crackdown on opposition figures. Erdoğan responded by calling the opposition leader "delusional" and accused the CHP of acting as a "puppet of terrorists seeking to undermine this state."

CHP officials have indicated they plan to contest a presidential election expected next year. There has been speculation that they could attempt to run the jailed İmamoğlu as a candidate even while he remains in detention. Özel previously stated that the upcoming election would represent a referendum on whether Turkey would have "autocracy or democracy."

The forced removal from party headquarters disrupts the opposition's ability to organize political activities from its main administrative base in the capital.

Turkish police stormed the main opposition Republican People's Party (CHP) headquarters and forced out party members in the latest escalation of a political crisis gripping the country. The operation came days after a court ruling removed the party's leader and reinstalled a predecessor more amenable to the government.

An appeals court in Ankara had ordered the removal of CHP leader Özgür Özel on Thursday, annulling a 2023 leadership contest. The 51-year-old had become the public face of Turkey's opposition and was credited with revitalizing the party. He was replaced by his predecessor, Kemal Kılıçdaroğlu, who lost a general election to President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan in 2023.

The court ruling was widely criticized as an attempt to weaken the CHP and install a more compliant leader. The decision sent shockwaves through Turkey's economy, with stock market trading briefly suspended in Istanbul following a 6 percent drop in share prices.

The party's subsequent use of its headquarters to defy the ruling prompted the police action. Officers forced their way into the building and physically removed occupants, marking a significant escalation in tensions between the government and opposition forces.

The crisis reflects a broader crackdown on Erdoğan's political opponents since the 2024 local elections, where the CHP won control of municipalities and mayoralties across the country. More than 20 CHP mayors have been detained on corruption, bribery, and terrorism-related charges in the months that followed.

Among those arrested was Istanbul Mayor Ekrem İmamoğlu, seen as a potential CHP presidential candidate. He has spent the past year in a maximum-security facility and is currently on trial as one of 400 defendants accused of involvement in a sprawling corruption scheme. Human Rights Watch called the trial part of a systematic effort to weaponize the criminal justice system against the CHP.

In recent weeks, tensions escalated between Özel and Erdoğan. A court ordered Özel to pay the president 300,000 lira in damages for calling him an "oppressor" and telling him to "leash your dogs" in criticism of the crackdown on opposition figures. Erdoğan responded by calling the opposition leader "delusional" and accused the CHP of acting as a "puppet of terrorists seeking to undermine this state."

CHP officials have indicated they plan to contest a presidential election expected next year. There has been speculation that they could attempt to run the jailed İmamoğlu as a candidate even while he remains in detention. Özel previously stated that the upcoming election would represent a referendum on whether Turkey would have "autocracy or democracy."

The forced removal from party headquarters disrupts the opposition's ability to organize political activities from its main administrative base in the capital.
