A coal mine explosion in China has killed 90 workers, marking the deadliest mining disaster in the country since 2009. The blast occurred on Saturday morning at a coal mining facility, according to reports.

The explosion represents a significant loss of life in what has been one of China's most dangerous industries. Mining accidents have declined in China over the past two decades as safety regulations have been strengthened, but the sector continues to experience periodic disasters.

The death toll of 90 makes this the deadliest single mining incident in China in over 15 years. In 2009, a coal mine explosion killed more than 100 workers in what was one of the worst mining disasters in recent Chinese history.

China is the world's largest producer and consumer of coal, which remains a critical energy source for the country despite efforts to transition to cleaner energy. The coal mining industry employs millions of workers across the country, particularly in northern and western provinces where major coal deposits are located.

Mining has historically been among the most dangerous occupations in China. The government has implemented stricter safety standards and increased inspections at mining operations in recent years, leading to a reduction in mining fatalities overall. However, enforcement of safety regulations can vary across different regions and facilities.

The cause of Saturday's explosion has not been reported. Coal mine explosions are typically caused by buildups of methane gas, coal dust, or other combustible materials that can be ignited by sparks or heat sources in underground mining operations.

Details about the specific location of the mine, the circumstances of the explosion, and any rescue or recovery operations have not been provided in available reports.

12:31 AM ET

The United States and Iran exchanged fire late Thursday in the most serious test yet of their month-long ceasefire, throwing into question the viability of the fragile truce that had largely held for the previous month.

The U.S. military said it targeted sites responsible for attacking three U.S. destroyers transiting the Strait of Hormuz in what it called "unprovoked" hostilities by Tehran. The USS Truxtun, USS Rafael Peralta and USS Mason came under fire as they transited the strait.

According to U.S. Central Command (Centcom), Iranian forces "launched multiple missiles, drones and small boats" at the three destroyers, but "no U.S. assets were struck." Centcom said its forces eliminated "inbound threats and targeted Iranian military facilities" responsible for attacking U.S. forces, including missile and drone launch sites, command and control locations, and intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance nodes.

Iran's military accused the U.S. of breaking the ceasefire agreement by targeting an Iranian oil tanker and another ship entering the Strait of Hormuz. A military spokesperson said the U.S. carried out airstrikes on "civilian areas" along the coasts of Bandar Khamir, Sirik and the island of Qeshm, which is home to about 150,000 people and a water desalination plant. The spokesperson said Iran's armed forces responded by attacking U.S. military vessels, "reportedly inflicting significant damage on them."

President Donald Trump insisted the ceasefire remained intact despite the strikes, which he described in an interview with ABC News as a "love tap." "They trifled with us today," Trump told reporters during an evening visit to the Reflecting Pool in Washington DC. "We blew them away. They trifled. I call that a trifle."

Asked about hopes of a negotiated end to the conflict, Trump said a deal "might not happen, but it could happen any day." He claimed, "I believe they want the deal more than I do."

The United Arab Emirates said it had intercepted Iranian missile and drone attacks hours after the U.S. said it thwarted attacks on its destroyers. Iran's Press TV reported that after several hours of fire, "the situation on Iranian islands and coastal cities by the strait of Hormuz is back to normal now."

Brent crude oil rose to trade about $101 a barrel on news of the attacks.

Before the strikes, there were reports the U.S. and Iran may be close to a temporary agreement to halt the war, with a one-page memorandum being shared between Washington and Tehran through Pakistan. Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif had struck an optimistic tone prior to the exchanges of fire, saying in televised remarks, "I firmly believe that this ceasefire will turn into a long-term ceasefire."

The U.S. has been pressuring Iran to reopen the strait, enforcing a naval blockade of Iranian ports.

Yesterday, 7:55 PM ET

NASA is considering removing the California Institute of Technology from management of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory for the first time in the facility's history. The decision would end a partnership that has lasted decades and reshape the future of one of the nation's premier space research centers.

JPL, located in Pasadena, California, has been operated by Caltech under contract with NASA since the laboratory's establishment. The facility has managed numerous high-profile missions including Mars rovers, deep space probes, and planetary exploration programs.

The potential change in management comes as NASA evaluates how its research centers are operated and funded. JPL employs thousands of scientists, engineers, and support staff who work on missions exploring the solar system and beyond.

Caltech has held the contract to manage JPL through various agreements with NASA over the years. The relationship has made JPL one of the most productive NASA centers, contributing to advances in planetary science, robotics, and space technology.

If NASA proceeds with removing Caltech from JPL management, the space agency would need to find a new operator for the facility or bring it under direct NASA control. Such a transition would represent a significant shift in how the laboratory operates and potentially affect ongoing and planned missions.

The decision affects not only the institutional relationship between Caltech and NASA but also the thousands of people employed at JPL. The laboratory has been central to many of NASA's most successful missions, including the recent Mars exploration programs and missions to Jupiter's moons.

Neither NASA nor Caltech has announced a final decision on the matter. The consideration of this change reflects broader discussions within NASA about how to structure its research and development operations going forward. JPL's role in future space missions and planetary exploration could be affected by any change in management structure.

"It Takes Two" became one of hip-hop's most recognizable tracks, featuring a sample from Lyn Collins' "Think (About It)" and Base's partner DJ E-Z Rock. The song reached number 36 on the Billboard Hot 100 and became a cultural touchstone, frequently featured in films, commercials, and sporting events over the decades.

Base, born Robert Ginyard in Harlem, New York, formed the duo Rob Base and DJ E-Z Rock in the mid-1980s. Their debut album "It Takes Two" was released in 1988 and went platinum. The title track's infectious beat and call-and-response vocals made it a staple at parties and sporting events.

The duo released their second album "The Incredible Base" in 1989, but never matched the success of their breakthrough hit. DJ E-Z Rock, whose real name was Rodney Bryce, died in 2014 at age 46.

Base continued to perform over the years, often appearing at nostalgia tours and hip-hop festivals. "It Takes Two" remained his signature song and a definitive track of the golden age of hip-hop.

The song's influence extended beyond its initial chart success. Its upbeat energy and memorable hook made it a frequent choice for movie soundtracks and television shows. The track has been sampled and referenced by numerous artists across multiple genres.

Base's contribution to hip-hop came during a period when the genre was transitioning from underground phenomenon to mainstream cultural force. "It Takes Two" demonstrated that hip-hop could produce crossover hits that appealed to audiences beyond the genre's core fanbase.

The rapper's death adds to the list of hip-hop pioneers from the 1980s and 1990s who have died in recent years, as the genre's first generation of mainstream stars ages.
