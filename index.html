A train struck a school minivan at a railway crossing in Belgium, killing four people including pupils. The collision occurred in the town of Halle, south of Brussels.

Emergency services responded to the scene. The victims included children traveling in the minivan and the driver. The train was carrying passengers at the time of the accident.

Authorities have launched an investigation into the cause of the collision. Railway crossings in Belgium are equipped with warning signals and barriers designed to prevent such accidents.

The school minivan was transporting students when it was hit by the train. Details about the ages of the children killed have not been released. The train driver and passengers on the train were not reported injured.

Local officials expressed condolences to the families of the victims. The collision prompted temporary disruptions to rail service in the area while investigators examined the scene.

Belgian transport safety authorities will review the circumstances of the accident, including whether warning systems at the crossing were functioning properly. Railway crossing accidents in Belgium have prompted safety reviews in the past.

The town of Halle is located approximately 15 kilometers southwest of Brussels. The railway line serves commuter and regional traffic between Brussels and other Belgian cities.

Families of the victims have been notified. The investigation will determine whether mechanical failure, human error, or other factors contributed to the collision. Belgian federal police are assisting local authorities with the inquiry.

The accident marks one of the deadliest railway crossing incidents in Belgium in recent years. Transport officials have not announced changes to crossing safety protocols in response to the collision.

BP's board has unanimously removed chairman Albert Manifold with immediate effect after serious governance and conduct concerns came to light. Manifold served in the role for only eight months before the swift departure.

The oil company announced the decision on Tuesday without disclosing specific details about the nature of the issues. Senior independent director Amanda Blanc said the board had been "surprised and disappointed" to learn of governance oversight and conduct matters deemed unacceptable.

"Albert has helped bring a welcome focus and pace to BP's transformation," Blanc said. "However, the board has been surprised and disappointed to learn of governance oversight and conduct issues it deems unacceptable and has taken decisive action."

The announcement sent BP shares tumbling, with the stock falling 9 percent immediately after the news before recovering slightly to a 6 percent decline, making it the FTSE 100's biggest faller on the day.

Manifold was appointed to BP's board in October 2025 with a mandate to oversee significant strategic changes at the oil company, including refocusing on fossil fuel extraction and moving away from renewable energy investments. During his brief tenure, he oversaw major leadership changes, including removing chief executive Murray Auchincloss after less than two years and hiring Meg O'Neill to replace him in December.

The removal marks the second instance in three years where a senior BP leader has departed over conduct concerns. Bernard Looney, Auchincloss's predecessor as chief executive, was forced out in September 2023 for failing to disclose sexual relationships with colleagues. BP's board later determined it had been misled and denied Looney 32 million pounds in pay and shares.

Ian Tyler, an existing board member, has been appointed as interim chairman. Tyler brings extensive experience in leadership roles across major corporations. He previously served as chief executive of Balfour Beatty, a FTSE 250 infrastructure company, and currently chairs Grafton Group, a FTSE 250 building supplies company. He also serves as a director at Anglo American, the FTSE 100 mining company.

BP said it would provide further updates as appropriate but did not commit to revealing additional details about the specific governance and conduct concerns that prompted Manifold's removal. The board now faces the task of identifying permanent replacement leadership for the chairman position while navigating a transition period amid broader energy sector scrutiny.

The departure underscores ongoing governance challenges at the major oil company as it manages its operations and public commitments around energy transition issues.

NASA has assigned more astronaut transport missions to SpaceX while Boeing continues to work toward certifying its Starliner spacecraft for regular crew flights. The space agency announced the contract modification, adding missions to SpaceX's existing commercial crew agreement.

The decision reflects ongoing delays in Boeing's efforts to complete the certification process for its crew capsule. SpaceX has been flying astronauts to the International Space Station since 2020 under NASA's Commercial Crew Program, which originally selected both companies to provide transportation services.

Boeing's Starliner has faced technical setbacks that have prevented the spacecraft from achieving operational status. The company has completed test flights but has not yet received NASA approval to begin regular crewed missions to the space station.

SpaceX operates its Crew Dragon capsule on Falcon 9 rockets, launching from Kennedy Space Center in Florida. The company has completed multiple crewed missions for NASA, including routine crew rotations and private astronaut flights.

The Commercial Crew Program was designed to restore American capability to launch astronauts from U.S. soil and create competition in the spacecraft market. NASA awarded contracts to both SpaceX and Boeing in 2014, with the goal of ending reliance on Russian Soyuz vehicles for station access.

SpaceX's Crew Dragon became operational in 2020 after successful demonstration missions. Boeing conducted an uncrewed test flight in 2019 that encountered software problems, requiring a second attempt in 2022. The company has since been working to address technical issues and complete certification requirements.

The additional missions give NASA continued assured access to the space station while Boeing works through its certification timeline. NASA has not announced specific launch dates for the newly assigned SpaceX missions.

The contract modification ensures the space agency can maintain its planned crew rotation schedule aboard the International Space Station. Each mission typically carries four astronauts who spend approximately six months conducting research and maintaining the orbiting laboratory.

Boeing continues development work on Starliner with the goal of eventually providing a second domestic crew transportation option for NASA.

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest saxophonists in jazz history, has died at age 95. The musician's death was announced on his website Monday "with deep sorrow and profound love," and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York.

Rollins emerged as a major figure in jazz during the 1950s, working alongside legends like Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, and John Coltrane. His playing combined technical mastery with spontaneous creativity, earning him recognition as one of the greatest improvisers in the genre. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis has called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he grew up in Harlem and earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. Inspired by family members who played piano and violin, as well as jazz heroes like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller, he started learning saxophone at age seven. His high school band featured future stars Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew.

His most celebrated albums include "Saxophone Colossus" from 1956, which featured the signature composition "St. Thomas," named after his mother's Caribbean birthplace. Other landmarks included "Way Out West" in 1957 and "Freedom Suite" in 1958, whose 20-minute title track became an elegant statement on freedom during the civil rights movement. His comeback album "The Bridge" came in 1962 after a three-year break during which he practiced up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge.

Rollins took another sabbatical between 1969 and 1971 to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram. Throughout the following decades, he continued evolving his sound, incorporating funk, calypso, and Latin American influences. He also composed and performed the soundtrack to the 1966 Michael Caine film "Alfie."

Unlike many of his contemporaries, Rollins maintained an active performing schedule well into his eighties, continuing to tour internationally and release new recordings. He shifted his performances away from nightclubs to larger venues and campaigned on climate change with benefit concerts and his 1998 album "Global Warming."

Throughout his career, Rollins received numerous honors including recognition as a NEA Jazz Master and the National Medal of the Arts. When President Barack Obama presented him with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, Obama said Rollins had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Rollins approached each performance as an opportunity for fresh invention, rarely repeating himself and treating standards and original compositions as frameworks for in-the-moment creation. He lived in New York for most of his life, remaining a fixture in the jazz community until his death. His passing marks the loss of one of the last direct links to the bebop era.

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has stopped deportation flights to the Democratic Republic of Congo due to an Ebola outbreak in the country. Officials expressed concerns that the flights could expose Immigration and Customs Enforcement personnel to the virus and potentially help spread the disease closer to United States borders.

The pause represents a shift in the administration's deportation operations, which have been central to its immigration enforcement strategy. ICE had been conducting flights to return Congolese nationals to their home country as part of broader deportation efforts.

The Ebola outbreak in Congo has raised alarms among public health officials monitoring the situation. The virus, which causes severe hemorrhagic fever, has historically had high fatality rates during outbreaks in central Africa. The disease spreads through direct contact with bodily fluids of infected individuals.

The decision to halt flights reflects a calculation that the health risks to enforcement personnel and the possibility of importing the disease outweigh the priority of continuing deportations to that destination. ICE officials involved in deportation operations would have direct contact with deportees during flights and the transfer process in Congo.

Public health experts have noted that while neither Ebola nor other recent viral outbreaks pose an immediate threat to most Americans, the responses to these situations reveal challenges in the country's public health infrastructure. The trust deficit these viruses expose could prove significant if a major health crisis reaches the United States.

The Ebola situation has also affected international events. The Democratic Republic of Congo's World Cup team canceled training in Kinshasa due to health restrictions, though the team still plans to travel to the United States for preparations ahead of the tournament.

The administration has not announced when deportation flights to Congo might resume or what conditions would need to be met for operations to restart. The pause appears to be temporary, pending assessment of the outbreak's trajectory and associated risks to personnel.

Sonny Rollins, Jazz Saxophone Legend, Dies at 95

9:00 AM ET

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest saxophonists in jazz history, has died at age 95. The musician's death was announced on his website Monday "with deep sorrow and profound love," and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York.

Rollins emerged as a major figure in jazz during the 1950s, working alongside legends like Miles Davis, Thelonious Monk, and John Coltrane. His playing combined technical mastery with spontaneous creativity, earning him recognition as one of the greatest improvisers in the genre. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis has called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong.

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in New York City in 1930, he grew up in Harlem and earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. Inspired by family members who played piano and violin, as well as jazz heroes like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller, he started learning saxophone at age seven. His high school band featured future stars Jackie McLean and Kenny Drew.

His most celebrated albums include "Saxophone Colossus" from 1956, which featured the signature composition "St. Thomas," named after his mother's Caribbean birthplace. Other landmarks included "Way Out West" in 1957 and "Freedom Suite" in 1958, whose 20-minute title track became an elegant statement on freedom during the civil rights movement. His comeback album "The Bridge" came in 1962 after a three-year break during which he practiced up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge.

Rollins took another sabbatical between 1969 and 1971 to study yoga, philosophy, and meditation at an Indian ashram. Throughout the following decades, he continued evolving his sound, incorporating funk, calypso, and Latin American influences. He also composed and performed the soundtrack to the 1966 Michael Caine film "Alfie."

Unlike many of his contemporaries, Rollins maintained an active performing schedule well into his eighties, continuing to tour internationally and release new recordings. He shifted his performances away from nightclubs to larger venues and campaigned on climate change with benefit concerts and his 1998 album "Global Warming."

Throughout his career, Rollins received numerous honors including recognition as a NEA Jazz Master and the National Medal of the Arts. When President Barack Obama presented him with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, Obama said Rollins had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Rollins approached each performance as an opportunity for fresh invention, rarely repeating himself and treating standards and original compositions as frameworks for in-the-moment creation. He lived in New York for most of his life, remaining a fixture in the jazz community until his death. His passing marks the loss of one of the last direct links to the bebop era.
