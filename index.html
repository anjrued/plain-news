The U.S. military said it struck Iranian missile and drone launch sites, command centers, and surveillance facilities after Iranian forces launched multiple missiles, drones, and small boats at three American destroyers: the USS Truxtun, USS Rafael Peralta, and USS Mason. According to U.S. Central Command, no American vessels were damaged and all inbound threats were eliminated.

Iran's military disputed this account, claiming the U.S. violated the ceasefire by targeting two ships at the strait and attacking civilian areas near Bandar Khamir, Sirik, and the island of Qeshm, which has a population of about 150,000 people and a water desalination plant. Iranian officials accused the U.S. of launching strikes with help from regional countries. The United Arab Emirates said it intercepted Iranian missile and drone attacks hours after the U.S. reported thwarting attacks on its destroyers.

Despite the violence, President Trump characterized the strikes as minimal. "We blew them away. They trifled. I call that a trifle," Trump told reporters. He described the action as a "love tap" in an ABC News interview and insisted the ceasefire remained intact.

When asked about prospects for a negotiated settlement, Trump said a deal "might not happen, but it could happen any day." He added: "I believe they want the deal more than I do."

The U.S. has been pressuring Iran to reopen the strait as part of a naval blockade of Iranian ports. Earlier this week, Trump announced "Project Freedom," a campaign to guide stranded tankers through the waterway after the military destroyed six Iranian boats and cruise missiles.

Before Thursday's strikes, reports suggested the U.S. and Iran were close to a temporary agreement, with a one-page memorandum circulating between Washington and Tehran through Pakistan. However, senior Iranian officials have recently rejected concessions. Some prefer extending negotiations closer to the U.S. midterm elections, when the Trump administration may face pressure to settle.

Regional diplomats warned Iran could overplay its hand by dragging out talks, missing an opportunity to claim victory and end the conflict. They noted that if no agreement emerges, Washington could unilaterally end the war and leave Iran under severe economic sanctions.

Elon Musk said Tuesday that US military suicide drones used Starlink satellite internet service in violation of SpaceX policies, blaming a military contractor for the unauthorized use.

Musk stated the drones should have used Starshield, a separate satellite service SpaceX designed specifically for military and government applications, rather than Starlink, the company's commercial internet service.

The disclosure raises questions about how military contractors access and deploy satellite communications technology. SpaceX has maintained distinct product lines for civilian and military use, with Starshield developed to meet defense and intelligence requirements while Starlink serves commercial customers.

Musk did not identify which military contractor was involved or provide details about when or where the drones were deployed. He also did not specify what consequences, if any, SpaceX plans to pursue against the contractor.

The revelation comes as SpaceX prepares for an initial public offering and as Starlink expands its customer base. The company announced a contract with American Airlines on the same day to install Starlink on more than 500 Airbus aircraft.

Starshield was created to provide secure satellite communications for government operations, including military applications. The service uses modified Starlink satellites with additional security features and operates under different terms of service than the commercial Starlink network.

SpaceX has previously emphasized that Starlink's acceptable use policy prohibits certain military applications. The company has faced scrutiny over how its satellite services are used in conflict zones and by defense forces.

The unauthorized use of Starlink on weapons systems could create legal and regulatory complications for SpaceX, particularly as the company seeks to maintain contracts with both civilian and military customers. Federal regulations govern the export and use of satellite communications technology, especially in weapons applications.

Neither SpaceX nor the Department of Defense immediately provided additional comment on the matter. The incident highlights ongoing challenges in controlling dual-use technologies that serve both commercial and military markets.

A few minutes ago

Tensions between Washington and Tehran intensified after the U.S. conducted military strikes against southern Iran on Monday, the first such action since a ceasefire began on April 8. The strikes targeted missile launchers and Iranian efforts to lay mines in the Strait of Hormuz, killing four Iranian soldiers and jeopardizing delicate peace negotiations that have been progressing under mediation from Pakistan and Qatar.

Iran's foreign ministry condemned the military action as "an act of bad faith" and "a definitive violation of the ceasefire," warning that the country would not leave the aggression unanswered. However, Iran chose not to withdraw from ongoing talks, suggesting both sides want to preserve the possibility of reaching an agreement despite the escalation.

The Strait of Hormuz remains central to negotiations. The proposed peace framework would require the U.S. to lift its blockade of Iranian oil ports within 30 days, while Iran would allow commercial shipping through the strait to resume at pre-war levels. This reopening would provide significant relief to global oil markets, which have faced severe disruption since the conflict began on February 28.

Oil prices climbed 4 percent immediately following news of the renewed military action, reflecting market anxiety. Brent crude futures have fluctuated around 100 dollars per barrel since Iran closed the strait in response to the initial U.S. and Israeli offensive. Economic analysts warn that oil markets are approaching a dangerous threshold that could trigger inflation, shortages, and recession if prices remain elevated.

The negotiations have centered on several key issues. Iran's parliamentary speaker and chief negotiator, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, has been working in Doha to unlock over 12 billion dollars in frozen Iranian assets. The agreement would also include sanctions relief for Iran's oil and petrochemical exports during a 60-day period designated for negotiating constraints on Iran's nuclear program.

Ghalibaf, who was recently reelected as speaker, has maintained focus on accessing frozen funds, which his allies describe as the final serious dispute between Tehran and Washington. The accumulated distrust between the nations means further negotiations cannot proceed without transferring these frozen assets first.

The agreement carries significant political weight for all parties involved. Hardliners in Washington, Tehran, and Jerusalem are pressuring their respective negotiators against making additional concessions. Each side must present the final deal to its constituency as evidence that the conflict's sacrifices produced meaningful results.

Whether the peace process can survive the latest military action remains uncertain. President Trump had planned a rare cabinet meeting at Camp David but postponed it, citing bad weather. Both governments face mounting pressure to resolve the oil crisis while protecting their security interests in the region.
