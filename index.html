8:11 PM ET

The United States military conducted strikes on Iranian missile launch sites and naval vessels in what it described as self-defense operations. US Central Command confirmed the strikes targeted locations in southern Iran.

The military action comes amid ongoing negotiations between Washington and Tehran over a broader diplomatic agreement. Multiple news organizations reported the strikes occurred while discussions continue about reopening the Strait of Hormuz and other regional security issues.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio indicated that a deal with Iran remains possible within days despite the military strikes. The Nikkei reported, citing a source, that under a potential peace agreement Iran would reopen the Strait of Hormuz 30 days after signing.

The strikes mark a significant escalation in direct US military action against Iranian territory. Central Command characterized the operations as defensive in nature, though specific details about what prompted the immediate military response were not provided in available reports.

The timing of the strikes appears to complicate ongoing diplomatic efforts. The Economist had earlier analyzed potential terms of an agreement between Washington and Tehran, suggesting negotiations had advanced to substantive discussions about regional security arrangements.

CBS News reported that US officials insisted a ceasefire remains in place amid the negotiations, despite the military action. The apparent contradiction between striking Iranian territory while maintaining active diplomatic talks reflects the complex nature of the current US-Iran relationship.

The strikes targeted both land-based missile facilities and maritime assets. The BBC confirmed US forces launched operations in southern Iran, while CNN and ABC News provided live coverage of the developing situation.

No casualties from the strikes have been reported. Iranian government response to the military action has not been detailed in available sources. The impact of the strikes on the timeline for a potential diplomatic agreement remains uncertain, though Rubio's comments suggest US officials believe negotiations can continue.

The strikes represent the most direct US military engagement with Iran in recent years, coming at a moment when both countries appeared to be moving toward a diplomatic resolution of longstanding tensions.

Officials in Orange County, California have ruled out the possibility of a massive explosion from a chemical leak at a GKN Aerospace facility in Garden Grove, though they cautioned that the overall crisis remains unresolved.

The incident began Thursday when a storage tank containing methyl methacrylate, a highly flammable chemical used in resin and plastic manufacturing, began off-gassing and threatened to fail. Damage to a valve on the tank created additional operational challenges and prevented complete mitigation of the problem.

Initial evacuation orders were lifted Thursday but reimposed Friday as conditions worsened. The expanded evacuation ultimately affected approximately 40,000 people across six cities: Garden Grove, Cypress, Stanton, Anaheim, Buena Park and Westminster. Garden Grove is located about 30 miles southeast of downtown Los Angeles and less than a mile from Disneyland's theme parks, which were not placed under evacuation orders.

Orange County fire authority division chief Craig Covey described the severity of the situation before the latest developments. "This thing is going to fail, and we don't know when," he said. He outlined two possible scenarios: the tank could spill 6,000 to 7,000 gallons of toxic chemicals into the parking lot, or it could undergo thermal runaway and explode, potentially affecting nearby tanks containing fuel or other chemicals.

County health officer Dr Regina Chinsio-Kwong warned that if the chemical heats up, it releases vapor harmful to human health, potentially causing respiratory issues, itching, burning eyes, nausea and headaches.

Authorities responded by creating containment barriers with sandbags to prevent any chemical spill from entering storm drains or reaching creeks and the ocean. The city established at least two shelters for evacuees. Garden Grove police reported that about 15 percent of people facing evacuation orders refused to leave.

In an overnight operation, firefighters stabilized the overheating tank through cooling and monitoring efforts. The tank cracked over the weekend, which authorities hoped would relieve pressure and reduce explosion risk. Following these developments, officials established a new, reduced evacuation zone.

While the explosion threat has been eliminated according to county fire officials, approximately 16,000 people remain displaced and unable to return home. Officials emphasized that although immediate danger has diminished, ongoing precautions continue as crews work to resolve the chemical leak at its source.

Orange County emergency management coordinated with state resources throughout the response. Officials plan to provide additional updates as verification work concludes and more residents receive clearance to return to their homes.

5:36 PM ET

Oil prices dropped below $100 a barrel on Monday as investors responded to signs that the United States and Iran may be moving toward a peace deal. Brent crude futures, the global oil benchmark, fell 6% to $97.43 a barrel, marking the lowest level in two weeks. The price decline reflects market optimism that an agreement could end the three-month conflict and potentially reopen the Strait of Hormuz, a critical chokepoint for global energy supplies.

Stock markets gained alongside the oil price decline, with Japan's Nikkei rising nearly 3% and Europe's Stoxx 600 index up 1%. The dollar weakened slightly, falling 0.3% against a basket of major currencies, while gold climbed 1.46% to $4,574 an ounce. These movements suggest investors are pricing in reduced geopolitical risk from a potential Iran agreement.

However, cautious signals temper the optimism. An Iranian government spokesperson said an agreement was "not imminent," despite reported progress on a framework. The U.S. and Iran remain at odds over key issues including Iran's blockade of the Strait of Hormuz. Warren Patterson, head of commodities strategy at ING, told Reuters: "We've been at this stage before, only for talks to break down. Therefore, the market will likely be more cautious about overreacting."

Recent U.S. military strikes against Iranian positions have also created uncertainty about peace prospects. Stephen Innes, an independent analyst, noted that the market response reflected investor hopes "that the world's most dangerous energy choke point may soon reopen to something resembling normal flow."

Even if negotiations succeed and the strait reopens, analysts warn that returning to normal oil flows will take months. Damaged energy infrastructure in Qatar and elsewhere requires repair. Two tankers carrying liquefied natural gas exited the strait on Monday heading to Pakistan and China, while a supertanker with Iraqi crude left the Gulf bound for China after being stranded for nearly three months. UBS analyst Giovanni Staunovo emphasized that physical oil flows remain restricted despite the positive sentiment.

The price movements are significant given that oil remains substantially higher than before the Iran war, when Brent crude traded around $70 a barrel. Barclays maintained its average Brent crude forecast of $100 for the year but acknowledged upside risks.

Inflation concerns have shifted considerably due to the energy situation. Rising oil and gas prices, combined with increased costs for materials like fertilizer, have driven expectations of higher food prices globally. Central banks that anticipated rate cuts before the conflict now face pressure to consider increases instead. Markets expect the Bank of England to raise rates twice this year.

The combination of potential diplomatic progress and ongoing supply concerns has left investors carefully monitoring developments on both the Iran deal and physical oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz.

Google has redesigned its search box for the first time in a quarter century, integrating artificial intelligence directly into its core search interface. The change was announced at the company's I/O conference and represents a fundamental shift in how users interact with the search engine.

The new interface allows users to choose between traditional Google search results and AI-generated responses. This marks the first major visual and functional change to the search box since Google's founding in 1998, when the simple text input field became one of the most recognizable elements of the internet.

Google CEO Sundar Pichai addressed public concerns about artificial intelligence during the announcement. The company is positioning the change as part of what it calls the "agentic Gemini era," referring to its Gemini AI system that now powers search responses.

The redesign affects how billions of people access information online. Users will now see the option to receive AI-generated summaries and answers alongside traditional search results. The change has prompted discussions about the future of web browsing and how AI will reshape internet usage patterns.

Time Magazine described the shift as a major change in how people use the internet. The AI-powered search can provide direct answers and summaries rather than simply listing links to websites, which has raised questions about how this will affect web traffic to news sites, blogs, and other online publishers.

The Register reported that Google is using web content to train and feed its AI systems, describing the process as the company cannibalizing the web. This has sparked debate about the relationship between AI companies and content creators whose work is used to generate AI responses.

CNN noted that AI is changing the internet forever, while Axios characterized Google's overhaul as marking the end of the internet's golden age. The change represents Google's most significant response to competition from AI chatbots like ChatGPT, which have attracted users looking for conversational answers rather than lists of links.

The new search interface is rolling out to users, though the timeline for full deployment was not specified in the announcements.

This marks the Knicks' first Finals appearance since 1999, when they lost to the San Antonio Spurs. The franchise has not won an NBA championship since 1973.

The Cavaliers never mounted a serious challenge in the series after the opening game. In Game 1, Cleveland held a 22-point lead in the fourth quarter before Jalen Brunson led the Knicks to a thrilling comeback victory in overtime. New York won the remaining three games by margins of 16, 13, and 37 points.

The Knicks' sweep of Cleveland follows an equally dominant run through the Eastern Conference. They previously swept the Philadelphia 76ers in the conference semifinals and defeated the Atlanta Hawks 4-2 in the first round. This latest victory extends their winning streak to 11 consecutive playoff games, the third longest in a single postseason in NBA history.

Brunson was named the Eastern Conference Finals MVP and emphasized the team effort in a postgame interview. "It means a lot to get to the finals, but I wouldn't be here without my teammates," he said. "My coach, this staff, the fanbase, without them none of this is possible."

Teammate Josh Hart highlighted Brunson's leadership throughout the series. "His even keel is vital for us," Hart said. "He doesn't let us get too high or too low. Even games like this when we're up 15 or 20, he wants to make sure we're doubling down on our habits."

The Knicks' return to the Finals has generated significant excitement in New York. Celebrity fans including filmmaker Spike Lee, comedian Tracy Morgan, and actor Timothée Chalamet traveled to Cleveland to witness the clinching victory. Guard Landry Shamet praised the passionate fan base, noting that Knicks supporters had traveled to the road game in large numbers.

"Knicks fans are a specific species of human that should be studied, they're crazy," Shamet said. "They fly out to Cleveland on a Monday, they're everywhere. The buzz is unbelievable."

The Knicks will now face either the defending champion Oklahoma City Thunder or the San Antonio Spurs in the NBA Finals. The Thunder and Spurs are tied 2-2 in their Western Conference Finals series, with Game 5 scheduled for Tuesday. The Spurs are led by 7-foot-4-inch superstar Victor Wembanyama.

New York enters the Finals in peak form, having swept two consecutive opponents and established themselves as a formidable title contender.

9:41 PM ET

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest saxophonists in jazz history, has died at age 95. His death was announced Monday on his website and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided.

Rollins was widely regarded as a colossus of the jazz saxophone, known for his technical mastery and improvisational genius. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting Rollins with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, President Barack Obama said the musician had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in Harlem in 1930, he earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began learning saxophone at age seven, inspired by musical family members and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. His early bands included future stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew and Art Taylor.

Rollins described himself as "primitive," saying he went "with my feelings more than my brain." This willingness to break with convention helped chart a new course for jazz alongside Miles Davis and others in the bebop movement. Davis wrote about how Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" who was always generating "fresh musical ideas."

Rollins struggled with heroin addiction in his early career and served ten months on Rikers Island in New York after committing armed robbery in 1950. He overcame his addiction through rehabilitation in 1955, which sparked remarkable creative output. He released his debut album as bandleader in 1953 and recorded 17 more by the end of the decade, including landmarks like "Saxophone Colossus" (1956), featuring his signature tune "St Thomas," and "Freedom Suite" (1958).

In 1959, Rollins took a three-year break from performing and recording, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway to avoid disturbing neighbors. This intensive preparation led to his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge." Another sabbatical between 1969 and 1971 saw him travel to an Indian ashram to study yoga, philosophy and meditation.

Throughout his career, Rollins evolved with jazz's changing landscape, exploring avant-garde, fusion, Latin American, and R&B-influenced directions. He composed the soundtrack to the 1966 film "Alfie" and added uncredited soloing to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album "Tattoo You."

Rollins married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004. His recorded legacy includes more than 60 albums and remains essential listening for jazz students and music lovers worldwide.

The pause affects deportation operations that would normally send individuals back to the DRC as part of...

May 21, 5:06 PM ET

The Trump administration has temporarily halted the removal of detainees to the Democratic Republic of Congo due to a widening Ebola outbreak, according to reporting by Politico. However, the move raises questions about its actual effectiveness in preventing disease spread.

The pause affects deportation operations that would normally send individuals back to the DRC as part of standard immigration enforcement procedures. The decision reflects concerns about exposing Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials to the virus and the potential risks associated with transporting people from an outbreak region.

The temporary nature of the suspension suggests the administration may resume flights once conditions change, though officials have not specified what circumstances would trigger a restart or provided a timeline for when operations might resume.

The halt creates complications for some individuals. At least one woman who was moved to Kinshasa, the capital of the DRC, now faces uncertainty after officials said they would not return her despite a judge's order for her return, citing the Ebola travel restrictions.

This pause comes amid broader scrutiny of the Trump administration's deportation flight operations. A group of 40 House Democrats recently sent a letter to the Federal Aviation Administration expressing "grave concerns" about what they describe as a secretive deportation flight program. The lawmakers demanded transparency regarding ICE's expanded use of charter aircraft to transfer detained immigrants.

The Democratic letter references an investigation revealing that the Trump administration transported detained immigrants in ways that "routinely violated their constitutional rights." The correspondence also highlighted allegations of abuse at a private detention center in Alexandria, Louisiana, which serves as a central hub in the deportation program.

The lawmakers expressed particular concern about efforts to shield these flights from public scrutiny. According to reporting by the Associated Press, dozens of charter jets used for deportation flights were granted unusual FAA permission to block certain data, including tail numbers, from public flight tracking sites.

"Credible reports indicate that individuals have been placed on flights without notice to counsel or family members, effectively disappearing from public view when flights are inappropriately shielded from tracking systems," the letter states. "Families are left searching for their loved ones, and attorneys are denied meaningful opportunities to intervene, raising serious due process concerns."

New Jersey congressman Rob Menendez, who authored the letter, emphasized the importance of transparency. "We want people to understand what is happening on their dime," he said.

The letter also requests detailed information from the FAA about humanitarian conditions on ICE flights, including the controversial use of full body restraints during deportation. The Trump administration has previously defended such restraints as "long-standing, standard ICE protocol" designed to ensure safety.

The surge in ICE flights during 2025 represented an 84 percent increase from 2024, according to monitoring by human rights groups.

The new interface allows users to choose between traditional Google search results and AI-generated responses. This marks the first major visual and functional change to the search box since Google's founding in 1998, when the simple text input field became one of the most recognizable elements of the internet.

Google CEO Sundar Pichai addressed public concerns about artificial intelligence during the announcement. The company is positioning the change as part of what it calls the "agentic Gemini era," referring to its Gemini AI system that now powers search responses.

The redesign affects how billions of people access information online. Users will now see the option to receive AI-generated summaries and answers alongside traditional search results. The change has prompted discussions about the future of web browsing and how AI will reshape internet usage patterns.

Time Magazine described the shift as a major change in how people use the internet. The AI-powered search can provide direct answers and summaries rather than simply listing links to websites, which has raised questions about how this will affect web traffic to news sites, blogs, and other online publishers.

The Register reported that Google is using web content to train and feed its AI systems, describing the process as the company cannibalizing the web. This has sparked debate about the relationship between AI companies and content creators whose work is used to generate AI responses.

CNN noted that AI is changing the internet forever, while Axios characterized Google's overhaul as marking the end of the internet's golden age. The change represents Google's most significant response to competition from AI chatbots like ChatGPT, which have attracted users looking for conversational answers rather than lists of links.

The new search interface is rolling out to users, though the timeline for full deployment was not specified in the announcements.

Sonny Rollins, Jazz Saxophone Legend, Dies at 95

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest saxophonists in jazz history, has died at age 95. His death was announced Monday on his website and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York. N...

11:00 PM ET

Sonny Rollins, one of the greatest saxophonists in jazz history, has died at age 95. His death was announced Monday on his website and confirmed by his publicist Terri Hinte. He died at his home in Woodstock, New York. No cause of death was provided.

Rollins was widely regarded as a colossus of the jazz saxophone, known for his technical mastery and improvisational genius. Saxophonist Branford Marsalis called him "the greatest improviser in the history of jazz" alongside Louis Armstrong. When presenting Rollins with the National Medal of the Arts in 2011, President Barack Obama said the musician had inspired him to "take risks that I might not otherwise have taken."

Born Walter Theodore Rollins in Harlem in 1930, he earned the nickname Sonny from his grandmother. He began learning saxophone at age seven, inspired by musical family members and jazz legends like Louis Jordan and Fats Waller. His early bands included future stars Jackie McLean, Kenny Drew and Art Taylor.

Rollins described himself as "primitive," saying he went "with my feelings more than my brain." This willingness to break with convention helped chart a new course for jazz alongside Miles Davis and others in the bebop movement. Davis wrote about how Rollins quickly became "a legend, almost a god to a lot of the younger musicians" who was always generating "fresh musical ideas."

Rollins struggled with heroin addiction in his early career and served ten months on Rikers Island in New York after committing armed robbery in 1950. He overcame his addiction through rehabilitation in 1955, which sparked remarkable creative output. He released his debut album as bandleader in 1953 and recorded 17 more by the end of the decade, including landmarks like "Saxophone Colossus" (1956), featuring his signature tune "St Thomas," and "Freedom Suite" (1958).

In 1959, Rollins took a three-year break from performing and recording, practicing up to 15 hours daily on the Williamsburg Bridge pedestrian walkway to avoid disturbing neighbors. This intensive preparation led to his 1962 comeback album "The Bridge." Another sabbatical between 1969 and 1971 saw him travel to an Indian ashram to study yoga, philosophy and meditation.

Throughout his career, Rollins evolved with jazz's changing landscape, exploring avant-garde, fusion, Latin American, and R&B-influenced directions. He composed the soundtrack to the 1966 film "Alfie" and added uncredited soloing to the Rolling Stones' 1981 album "Tattoo You."

Rollins married Lucille Pearson in 1965, and they remained together until her death in 2004. His recorded legacy includes more than 60 albums and remains essential listening for jazz students and music lovers worldwide.
